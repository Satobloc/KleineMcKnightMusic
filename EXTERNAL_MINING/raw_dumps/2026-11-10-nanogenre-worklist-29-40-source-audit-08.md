## 2. Item-by-item source audit

### 29. Ghost Dance Song

**Classification:** religious movement with multiple local song repertoires, not one uniform pan-Indigenous genre.

- The Ghost Dance movement arose among Paiute communities and spread to multiple Indigenous communities, each with its own languages and locally adapted songs.
- The Library of Congress describes James Mooney's 1894 recordings of songs in several languages. It notes that these may have been sung by Mooney himself, after learning them from participants, rather than being direct field recordings of the original singers.
- A scholarly musicology article emphasizes regional and community differences in Ghost Dance songs, including Arapaho, Caddo, Paiute, and Plains examples.

**Prompt consequence:** if retained, specify a particular community, language, time, and source recording. Do not write a generic “Ghost Dance style” or invent a pan-tribal sacred text. Any adaptation of sacred or community-owned material requires additional care and appropriate sourcing.

**Sources:**
- Library of Congress, James Mooney Recordings of American Indian Ghost Dance Songs: https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2017/11/james-mooney-recordings-ghost-dance-songs/
- University of California Press, “Songs of the New World and the Breath of the Planet at the Orbis Spike, 1610”: https://online.ucpress.edu/jams/article/76/1/57/195291/Songs-of-the-New-World-and-the-Breath-of-the
- Encyclopedia of the Great Plains, Ghost Dance: https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.rel.023.html

### 30. Calumet / Hako Sacred Peace Choral

**Classification:** a broad calumet ceremonial complex plus a specific Pawnee ceremony, not a generic choral genre.

- The Hako is documented as a version of the Calumet Ceremony practiced by the Chaui clan of the Pawnee.
- Alice C. Fletcher's 1904 account, assisted by Pawnee scholar and ceremonial leader James R. Murie and translated with music transcribed by Edwin S. Tracy, describes the ceremony's songs, rhythm, and structure.
- The Nebraska Press description notes that the ceremony's songs were transcribed, translated, interpreted by a Pawnee ceremonial leader, and analyzed by Fletcher.

**Prompt consequence:** do not collapse all calumet ceremonies into Hako or reduce the ceremony to a “peace choral.” The source documents a specific Pawnee ceremonial system with a specific historical record. Do not invent or imitate sacred lyrics from the title alone.

**Sources:**
- Nebraska Press, *The Hako: Song, Pipe, and Unity in a Pawnee Calumet Ceremony*: https://www.nebraskapress.unl.edu/bison-books/9780803268890/the-hako/
- Smithsonian Libraries and Archives record: https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_503173

### 31. A Cholla mo run

**Classification:** specific Gaelic piobaireachd tune title, not a separate genre.

- The specialist Piobaireachd Society / Pibroch source identifies **A Cholla mo Rùin** as an alternative title for **The Piper's Warning to His Master**, also associated with **Piobaireachd Dhùn Naomhaig**.
- The source's note on the Gaelic title explains the phrase's historical address to Colla Ciotach; it cautions that the emotional force of `mo rùin` is not captured by a simple modern gloss.

**Prompt consequence:** this item belongs with the piobaireachd source / repertoire branch. It can be a specific tune case study, but should not be described as a standalone musical tradition.

**Source:**
- Pibroch.net, PS 201 — The Piper's Warning to His Master: https://pibroch.net/ps201/

### 32. Sweating-up / Fore-sheet Shanty

**Classification:** maritime work-song use / operation, not a single genre label.

- *The Shanty Book, Part I* describes “We'll haul the bowlin'” as a shanty used for “sweating-up,” with many variants. Its final word was shouted rather than sung.
- The source also associates the fore-sheet / bowline operation with hauling and sail-handling work. The operational context is more important than treating “sweating-up” as a fixed musical genre.

**Prompt consequence:** define the exact work action, line, crew configuration, call-and-response timing, and point of maximum effort. The work operation determines phrase length and the shouted response.

**Source:**
- *The Shanty Book, Part I, Sailor Shanties*: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20774/pg20774-images.html

### 33. Bunt Shanty

**Classification:** a specific work-song function associated with bunting / furling sail.

- *The Shanty Book* describes “Paddy Doyle's Boots” as a bunt shanty, sung tutti throughout, with a single verse repeated; it was used for bunting up the foresail or mainsail.
- The coordinated effort occurs at the shouted word “boots,” when the gathered sail is hauled into its bundle and lifted onto the yard.

**Prompt consequence:** preserve the single repeated verse, all-hands delivery, and one decisive effort-point. Avoid adding a generic verse/chorus architecture that conflicts with the work process.

**Source:**
- *The Shanty Book, Part I, Sailor Shanties*: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20774/pg20774-images.html

### 34. West Indian House-Moving Land Shanty

**Classification:** documented Caribbean work-song practice, but not a uniform pan-West Indian genre.

- Smithsonian Folkways lists “Spider / Myanoh (House-Moving Song)” on its *Grenada Stories and Songs* collection, recorded in 1957.
- Cultural Equity's Nevis field notes state that coordinated lifting, including house-moving, called for shanty singing; the singers themselves used the term shanty in that region.
- Historical discussions of the term “shanty” and its proposed connection to house-moving are contested. A. L. Lloyd / Fox Smith-era explanations should not be treated as settled etymology.

**Prompt consequence:** use the specific Grenada / Nevis evidence, not a generic “West Indian” stereotype. Treat the song as a coordinated lifting work song with locally documented language, social roles, and effort timing.

**Sources:**
- Smithsonian Folkways, *Grenada Stories and Songs*: https://folkways.si.edu/grenada-stories-and-songs/caribbean-spoken-word-world/album/smithsonian
- Cultural Equity, Nevis field notes: https://www.culturalequity.org/sites/default/files/2018-08/Nevis%20notes.pdf
- A. L. Lloyd / C. Fox Smith, *A Book of Shanties*: https://s9.imslp.org/files/imglnks/usimg/8/85/IMSLP835530-PMLP1314967-CFoxSmith_A_Book_of_Shanties.pdf

### 35. Proportio Sesquialtera Sea Shanty

**Classification:** a rhythmic-proportion experiment applied to a shanty, not an attested genre.

- *The Shanty Book* observes that a particular shanty exhibits a rhythmic device known in medieval music as **proportio sesquialtera**.
- The description is an alternation / interpolation of bars in 3/4 within a 6/8 context, with the same number of quavers but a changed grouping.

**Prompt consequence:** define the actual metric relation and work-song action. The name should be presented as an analytical / compositional experiment, not a historical shanty subgenre.

**Source:**
- *The Shanty Book, Part I, Sailor Shanties*: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20774/pg20774-images.html

### 36. Volynka

**Classification:** bagpipe / instrument name, not a genre.

- The Encyclopedia of Ukraine identifies `volynka` as a name for the Ukrainian bagpipe also called `duda`, `koza`, `baran`, or `mikh`; it describes a goatskin bag, blowpipe, drone pipes, and chanter.
- Other specialist organology sources use `volynka` for Russian bagpipe types as well, so region and construction need to be specified.

**Prompt consequence:** choose a regional instrument and repertoire. “Volynka” alone does not specify a single musical tradition.

**Sources:**
- Encyclopedia of Ukraine, Duda: https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CD%5CU%5CDudaIT.htm
- Bagpipedia, Volynka: https://www.bagpipedia.nl/lemma_term.php?id=7693

### 37. Canntaireachd

**Classification:** oral vocal encoding / teaching and memory system for piobaireachd, not a genre.

- The National Library of Scotland describes canntaireachd as chanting that represents classical Scottish Great Highland Bagpipe music, piobaireachd, and notes its later written form.
- A 2025 *Scottish Studies* article describes it as an orally devised method used by pipers to remember compositions and transmit them to others. It can encode stylistic and expressive information beyond a simple pitch contour.

**Prompt consequence:** this is a particularly interesting vocalization / representational-language experiment. Do not reduce it to generic vocables or solfege. It encodes a specific piping idiom and expressive features.

**Sources:**
- National Library of Scotland, Canntaireachd subject record: https://manuscripts.nls.uk/subjects/4303
- Buisman, “Pipers Canntaireachd and Scottish Gaelic: Basic elements and expressive variability”: https://open.journals.ed.ac.uk/ScottishStudies/article/view/145

### 38. Coronach

**Classification:** Scots / Gaelic funeral lament or dirge, sung or played on bagpipes; not a single fixed genre.

- The Dictionary of the Scots Language defines coronach as a lament for the dead, a dirge, either sung or played on bagpipes.
- The Library of Congress holds an early-19th-century notated work titled *The Coronach, or funeral song*, from Walter Scott's *The Lady of the Lake*. This is a composed song with piano, not direct evidence of one universal traditional form.

**Prompt consequence:** specify whether the target is a vocal funeral lament, a bagpipe lament, or a later composed art-song treatment. Avoid treating all coronachs as one standardized arrangement.

**Sources:**
- Dictionary of the Scots Language: https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/snd/coronach
- Library of Congress, *The Coronach, or funeral song*: https://www.loc.gov/item/2015563713/

### 39. Piobaireachd

**Classification:** classical Great Highland Bagpipe repertoire (Ceòl Mòr), with a specific formal and oral transmission system.

- The National Library of Scotland's canntaireachd record ties the oral notation system directly to piobaireachd.
- The Piobaireachd Society's catalogue documents named tunes and their manuscript / performance lineages.
- Specialist scholarship stresses that staff notation alone cannot fully preserve the expressive and oral knowledge needed for a valid reconstruction.

**Prompt consequence:** if writing a genre field, focus on the ground / urlar and its variations, the piping idiom, grace-note vocabulary, phrase development, and the oral teacher-pupil lineage. Avoid using canntaireachd as a decorative “ancient vocal” effect.

**Sources:**
- National Library of Scotland, Canntaireachd: https://manuscripts.nls.uk/subjects/4303
- Piobaireachd Society tune library: https://www.piobaireachd.co.uk/library
- Donaldson / Piobaireachd scholarship: https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/002/MR55203.PDF

### 40. Medieval / early-modern / classical structural forms

**Classification:** a research umbrella, not a genre.

The item needs to be broken into specific forms or compositional procedures before writing prompts. Candidate branches could include fixed-form song, dance-form architecture, ostinato / ground-based forms, variation procedures, sequence, isorhythm, canon, hocket, cantus firmus, ritornello, and other historically bounded structures. These should be sourced and dated individually rather than treated as one continuous “classical” tradition.
