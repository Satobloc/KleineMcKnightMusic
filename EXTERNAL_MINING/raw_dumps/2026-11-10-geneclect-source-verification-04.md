**Research date:** 2026-11-10  
**Scope:** targeted verification of structural claims underlying the five proposed pairings in the GENECLECT terminology corpus.  
**Method:** focused web research using specialist / institutional / scholarly sources. This is a sampled audit, not verification of all 304 CSV entries.

## 1. Pair A — Balinese kotekan × Appalachian lined-out hymnody

### Supported
- UNSW's Interactive Gamelan documentation describes kotekan as interlocking figurations with two parts, polos and sangsih; typically polos plays on the beat and sangsih off the beat. The same source distinguishes this from kekenyongan, where gangsa play together rather than interlocking.
- Edward Herbst's Bali 1928 glossary documents specialized categories including tetorékan, ubit-ubitan, and kotékan, with ubit-ubitan described as a particular kotekan type.
- Smithsonian Folkways documents Old Regular Baptist lining out: a leader briefly sings a line, the congregation repeats the words in a longer, more elaborate tune; the response has no regular beat or harmonizing parts and is heterophonic.
- Smithsonian's booklet says the rhythm is governed by breath and spirit rather than metronomic time.

### Refinement
The proposed contrast between precision and elastic collective expansion is well supported as a useful compositional abstraction. Do not generalize it to all Balinese music or all Appalachian hymnody. The Appalachian evidence is specifically Old Regular Baptist lined-out hymnody in southeastern Kentucky.

### Sources

- Edward Herbst, *Bali 1928* glossary: https://edwardherbst.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Glossary.Herbst.Bali_.1928.Kebyar.pdf
- Smithsonian Folkways, Old Regular Baptists: https://folkways.si.edu/news-and-press/old-regular-baptists-lined-out-hymnody-from-southeastern-kentucky-named-to-national-recording-registry

## 2. Pair B — Andean siku × Shona mbira

### Supported
- Ohio State's Center for Latin American Studies describes ira and arka as complementary siku rows whose alternating notes are both required to complete the melody. It explicitly calls the technique hocket and describes dialogue between players.
- The same guide describes siku sizes (including chulli, sankas, and toyos) and common accompanying instruments such as bombo and chakchas.
- MBIRA's Shona mbira guide describes a basic cyclical pattern with intertwined melodies and contrasting rhythms. It names kushaura (leading) and kutsinhira (intertwining) as the two common parts when two mbiras play together.
- Scherzinger's music-theory analysis describes kushaura and kutsinhira as interlocking paths, with one player sounding in the silence of the other; hosho, leg rattles, clapping, and singing add further rhythmic layers.

### Refinement
The contrast “sequential handoff versus simultaneous interlocking” is a useful starting abstraction, but not a hard binary. Siku hocket is an ensemble-level interdependence; mbira interlocking also includes temporal offset and hocket-like relationships. Both traditions distribute musical information across performers. The more defensible distinction is **alternating note ownership in a shared melodic line versus multiple cyclic patterns coexisting and generating additional relationships**.

The OSU guide uses a male/female complementarity framing for ira/arka. Preserve that as a source-specific cultural explanation; do not universalize it as a timeless or uniform account across all Andean siku communities.

### Sources
- Ohio State, Andean Music Teacher Guide: https://clas.osu.edu/andean-music-teacher-guide
- MBIRA, Shona Mbira Music: https://mbira.org/what-is-mbira/mbira-music/shona-mbira-music/
- Scherzinger, “Temporal Geometries of an African Music”: https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.10.16.4/mto.10.16.4.scherzinger.html

## 3. Pair C — Balkan aksak × Ainu upopo

### Supported
- Fracile's article describes aksak as an asymmetrical rhythmic system and documents its forms and distribution in Balkan vocal and instrumental traditions.
- Chiba's peer-reviewed study examines the melodic structure of Ainu songs and finds that Ainu song types involve more than one melodic system, including timbral / phonation variation and pitch / interval organization.
- Endō 2022's Ainu terminology record distinguishes seated songs: upopo in Hokkaido and heciri in Sakhalin. It identifies ukouk as a distinctive round-singing form used in seated songs.
- The *Handbook of Japanese Music in the Modern Era* excerpt describes ukouk as a round in which the same melody is offset between singers, usually by one or more beats.
- Cambridge's chapter on Ainu music describes upopo as domestic work songs, often simple in structure, with many songs sung in rounds, but also emphasizes diversity.

### Refinement
The pair should be named more precisely **Balkan aksak × Ainu ukouk within seated-song/upopo practice**, not aksak versus all upopo. Ukouk is a specific overlapping-round technique, not a universal feature of every upopo. The Ainu terms also vary by region and song type; do not collapse Hokkaido upopo, Sakhalin heciri, dance-song forms, and lyrical song categories.

- Chiba, “The melodic structure of Ainu songs”: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/toyoongakukenkyu1936/1996/61/1996_61_1/_article/-char/en
- Endō 2022 Ainu terminology record: https://grammar.aynu.org/kb/sources/endo2022

## 4. Pair D — Finnic runosong × Sardinian cantu a tenòre

### Supported
- Saarinen's *Oral Tradition* article describes verse parallelism as a constitutive feature of Finnic oral poetry, alongside meter and alliteration.
- Kati Kallio's research on performance, music, and metre in Kalevala-metric oral poetry uses the terms Kalevala-metric poetry, runosong, and regilaul for related local traditions, while emphasizing variation in historical performance.
- The European Polyphony Research Centre's Sardinia page documents cantu a tenore's four-part vocal structure and the special vocal timbres of the lower voices, contra and bassu. It also records local names for related structures and local variation in voice-part names.

### Refinement
The proposed opposition is useful but compares different analytical levels: runosong is a poetic / oral-performance register with metrical and parallel-verse constraints; cantu a tenòre is a specialized multipart vocal practice with differentiated roles. A future prompt experiment should isolate **textual / metrical progression** from **simultaneous vocal-role architecture**, rather than implying that one tradition is purely sequential and the other has no narrative or text.

### Sources
- Saarinen, *Oral Tradition*: https://www.journal.oraltradition.org/said-a-word-uttered-thus-structures-and-functions-of-parallelism-in-arhippa-perttunens-poems/
- Kallio, University of Helsinki: https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/performance-music-and-metre-in-kalevala-metric-oral-poetry/
- European Polyphony Research Centre, Sardinia: https://www.mdw.ac.at/ive/emm/?PageId=155

## 5. Pair E — Javanese pathet × Cantonese muk jyu / muyuge

### Supported
- *Traditional Music in Modern Java* describes pathet as a Javanese system for classifying gamelan pieces, often translated as mode, with musical differentiation and associations of mood and performance time; it also connects pathet to wayang kulit's dramatic/time divisions.
- A more recent Javanese karawitan study describes pathet as multidimensional, organizing tonal hierarchy and operating through garap, rasa, and performance context.
- A University of California / UCI introduction to Javanese gamelan describes pathet as a model classification system involving tonal range, melodic patterns, and principle notes.
- Research on Cantonese narrative singing distinguishes wooden-fish songs (muyuge / muk jyu go) from the wooden-fish percussion instrument and from related forms such as nam jam and lung jau.
- A NACCL paper describes wooden-fish narrative songs as sung in Cantonese without accompaniment in some contexts, while allowing simple percussion in others.
- The Chinese Intangible Cultural Heritage page describes muyuge as commonly organized in seven-character lines, often in four-line groups, with a banqiang-style tune and intermittent accompaniment such as sanxian or qinqin.
- The Fairbank Center notes that wooden-fish books are often long narrative ballads and that the performance need not be accompanied by the wooden-fish instrument.

### Refinement
The original opposition “modal constraints versus narrative-language organization” is plausible but too broad. Pathet is not merely a scale or key; it is a context-dependent melodic / performance framework. Muk jyu / muyuge is not a single uniform style: it includes long narrative forms, seven-character quatrains, variable accompaniment, and speech-like or sung delivery. The CSV entry `muk jyu / muk'yu (木魚)` should be reviewed because the precise genre label is often `muk jyu go` / `muyuge` (木魚歌), while 木魚 can also denote the percussion instrument.

The source's earlier claim that the “original muk jyu form” is necessarily unaccompanied should be softened: sources describe unaccompanied performance as common in some forms, but simple or intermittent percussion / sanxian accompaniment also appears in documented variants.

## 6. Revised reading of the five pairings

The original pairings remain creatively useful, but the contrast labels should be updated:

| Pair | Keep | Refine |
|---|---|---|
| A: Kotekan × lined-out hymnody | Precision vs elastic collective phrase expansion | Scope to Gong Kebyar / paired gangsa techniques and Old Regular Baptist lining out |
| B: Siku × mbira | Alternating melody completion vs coexisting cycles | Both use interdependence / interlocking; not a strict sequential-simultaneous binary |
| C: Aksak × upopo | Asymmetric meter vs overlapping phrase entries | Pair aksak with the specific Ainu ukouk round technique, not all upopo |
| D: Runosong × cantu a tenòre | Poetic-metric organization vs specialized multipart vocal roles | Different analytical levels; isolate text / metre from vocal-role architecture |
| E: Pathet × muk jyu | Contextual modal framework vs narrative-text organization | Distinguish pathet from pathetan; distinguish muk jyu instrument, muk jyu go song, and muyushu songbooks |

## 7. Term-level corrections to apply before a v2 lexicon

- Keep `hocket` marked as an analytical term in the siku context, not a native term.
- Keep `polos` and `sangsih` attached to kotekan / ubit-ubitan practice; avoid reducing them to universally on-beat/off-beat labels without local/context qualification.
- Treat `ukouk` as a specific Ainu round-singing technique, with region and song-type qualifiers.
- Preserve the distinction between Hokkaido `upopo` and Sakhalin `heciri` where relevant.
- Keep `aksak` as a particular asymmetric rhythmic system, not a label for all Balkan odd meters or dances.
- Keep `Kalevala-mitta`, `runosong`, and `regilaul` as related but regionally and historically differentiated terms.
- Preserve local variation in `cantu a tenòre` voice-part names; do not assume one uniform naming system across Sardinia.
- Treat `pathet` as a multidimensional Javanese musical concept, not a simple scale or Western mode.
- Separate `muk jyu` (wooden fish / term used in performance contexts) from `muk jyu go` / `muyuge` (wooden-fish song) and `muk jyu syu` / `muyushu` (song books).
- Avoid describing all Cantonese narrative singing as tone-to-melody mapping; prosody matters, but the relationship is not a simple one-to-one rule.
