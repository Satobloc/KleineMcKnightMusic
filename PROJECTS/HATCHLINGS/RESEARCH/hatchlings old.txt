Hatchlings

BOOK 1: Flotsam
Chapter 1
I brought the heavy industrial tape measure down over and over again, feeling the crunch of bone, the greasy smear of brains, shouting at you over and over again, “Why…did you…make me…do this!?” until my voice was hoarse. I was surprised at myself. I had never done such a thing to a living being,  although I had seen such acts often enough.
[Rewrite below to be in 2nd person, addressing the Koryak?]
It was a new kind of darkness. A shock. For I had come to know darknesses so well. It was queasy, lolling, and dripping wet, pierced by thumb-thick shafts of light from far above, surrounded by uncomfortable, lightweight, boxy things which tumbled all around, over and under me. With newness, fear. With fear, questions. Should I alert my captors that something felt wrong? Or should I shut up and endure? Endurance was so often the only choice, but when changes happened, as they occasionally would, if the Yakuts thought I meant to take advantage, keeping my mouth shut about them would get me a beating or worse.
I touched my head--it was bleeding. Had I already received a beating and would be beaten again for forgetting the previous beating? I touched my feet. They were not bound, and that is what made me speak up.
“Hello,” I said in Yakut, “Hello, my feet are not bound. Will you bind my feet?”
No answer.
“hello, sirs. I need to be bound. Do you wish to bind my feet, and hands?”
Wrongness compounded wrongness, and I knew I had to do something. I curled into a ball among the boxes and let myself be rocked by the lolling dark space.
            #
After what seemed like hours, I determined that I was floating. Slowly, the rocking sensation had resolved, and it wasn’t just me. The room, the boxes, everything was somehow buoying on gentle waves. I also slowly began to recognize shallow breathing, and if my ears were not hallucinating with the afterimage of terror, I knew the sound of that particular breathing. My blood inched through my veins, afraid to move for fear of waking the Koryak. I tried to quiet my mind, but it would not quiet.
I must get the attention of one of the Yakuts. If they saw my untethered feet, there might be an argument and perhaps the Korkyak would storm off. Or perhaps he would take his anger out on me. But the presence of a Yakut would temper him. Moving carefully away from the Koryak’s subtle breathing, I felt for a ladder, a staircase, a rope, but there was nothing. Just boxes. I looked up at the openings. When my eyes managed to focus, I could tell they weren’t as large or as high as I had first thought. They were perhaps pinpricks.
I stumbled over the boxes and didn’t get far before I felt a ribbed composite wall. The ribs were sloped the wrong way, but I managed to pull myself above the boxes, and immediately I hit my head on a ceiling, knocking myself back into the boxes. I froze, bracing myself for a beating, but there was none. This was unprecedented. I stayed still until I could hear the Koryak’s breathing again under the pounding of my heart.
This time, more slowly, I pulled myself up, ignoring the bleeding of my hands’ cracked skin as I struggled to hold on to the little flaws and formation-marks on the composite. I reached a hand up and felt the ceiling. It was very low, and there was a rail running along the seam with the wall. Leveraging myself was a triumph of will against my scarred and malnourished body, but I could just bring my eyes to one of the holes. It was no bigger than my pinkie finger, and I could make out nothing but cloud-brushed sky. I felt the edge of the hole with my finger. It was a bullet hole.
I had a flash of memory. Shots had been fired. The clickety-clack-whump of bullets ricocheting off composite and burying themselves in cardboard.
Rain dripped into my eye, down my face. My body was beginning to shake from exertion. Besides the few occasions when the Yakuts made me carry their packs in the early days, and of course the frequent beatings and other tortures, this was by far the most strenuous activity I had undertaken for years. It felt terrible. It felt wonderful. My muscles aching with exertion, my ankles open to the air, not knocking together between ropes. For the first time, I felt joy. Joy tinged with fear, of course, as all emotions were tinged, but still recognizably joy.
The rainwater seeped between my cracked lips, between the cracks in my lips, stung terribly, and I tasted it. Not rainwater--seawater. And I remembered. We had been at sea. I could remember the sea train. I was supposed to be released. Released!
I brought my mouth up to the bullet hole.
“Hello!” I rasped, lips chafing against the sharp edges of the hole. “Sirs, hello!”
My legs were quaking fiercely and one of my feet slipped. In the second before my other foot slipped, I reset my hold on the rail, and when my legs went, I swung. No no no, I thought. Don’t let me fall and die. Not this time. No deathwish anymore. Just a wish for release. I knew the boxes were not far below me, but the darkness felt like infinity--another new kind of darkness. I grasped for my life and kicked my legs in the open air. Tears streamed down my cheeks. No!
My hands slipped, and I was back in the boxes, heart racing. But my mind awoke from its pain and confusion with the memory that I was to be released. The last thing I remembered was being let out of a cargo container--that must be what I was in now--and allowed to walk around on the deck. The sea air had been salty and rank, and the vast emptiness of waves as far as the eye could see was agoraphobic, but the open daylight had been a blessing, and the warm breeze had been an impossible delight after so long in chilly Sakha.
My guards had been a Yakut and three Russians, and they had seemed to be having great fun firing their railguns and xray laser rifles into the water. The size of the sea train had been astonishing, terrifying. The barge we were on had stretched perhaps a quarter mile, and I could count at least three more attached barges in front and two behind, all of them stacked five-deep with cargo containers. When I had first been captured, I only knew sea trains to be two or three barges long, and not nearly so large. Much must have changed.
I lay among the boxes, absorbed in glorious memory until I heard a sound. Movement. I scrambled, moving in a direction I thought was away from the sound, but as soon as I did, I was on top of the Koryak. His sweaty shirt stunk, but his muscles were slack. He slouched against a composite wall, collapsed upon a pile of crushed boxes. I jumped back, and hit another wall, and felt along it, tripping on boxes and clawing at the composite all the way. In short succession, I turned three corners and found myself back where the Koryak rested. This container was small, with boxes piled deep enough that I couldn’t tell if there was a floor at all.
I carefully ran my hands over the Koryak’s pockets, looking for a gun. The promise of freedom had whet my appetite for survival. I could not remember when I last cared like this. The Koryak had empty pockets. He wore a tool belt, but the only thing on it was a tape measure. I took it, feeling for sharp edges, and briefly flicking a built-in laser level. The sudden flash of red startled me. There was a belt clip, and in desperation, I climbed the wall again with the tape measure clipped to my tattered blue jeans. I propped myself in place again, arching my back and pulling my arm back hard and swinging the tape measure hard against the bullet hole. Nothing happened, but an echo that seemed to tumble around the container as the container bobbed like the sound itself was being rolled by the waves. I scratched at the hole with the corner of the tape measure, and managed to get the belt clip to catch the rough edge. I worked at it until my arms and legs trembled, and still I persisted. Finally, I managed to get the clip all the way through the hole, but I couldn’t yank it hard enough to tear the composite, so I put all my weight on it, quickly grabbing with both hands and letting my legs swing. I fell almost immediately and I could feel my skin tear and crack and leak warm blood even before I landed with a crunch on the boxes.
I looked up and saw that I had torn a hand-sized flap into the composite. I gritted my teeth in the closest thing to a smile I could manage, then felt around until I found the tape measure. When I stopped to bend the belt clip back to a useable position, I heard that the Koryak’s breathing had changed.
I could not see him, but his breaths were shallower, quieter. More in control. With a sudden rush of adrenaline, I flicked the laser level towards where I thought his eyes would be.
            He chuckled and lunged.
I covered my face and swung the tape measure hard at his temple, but he was on top of me and I couldn’t land a solid blow. His knees were in my ribs, and he shouted in Koryak. I bit his arm as hard as I could, not stopping when I tasted blood. He groaned and pulled back, giving me just enough distance and just enough time to slash his face with the ragged metal belt clip. I did it again, feeling the rough cutting of his flesh with satisfaction, then again, and he was backing away. He hit a wall and slipped and I used the opportunity to jam both of my knees into his groin and bash at his face. I kept bashing him with the heavy tape measure over and over again, and with each tear of flesh, each crunch of bone, I remembered the burns, the cuts, the fingernails, and I hit him harder each time until I was bashing nothing but slippery tissue into soaked cardboard.
My fingers tightened on hair and skin and blood and I stood, pulling the gore away from its source, then I flung it down and climbed the wall for the last time. I didn’t care if I had to jump off the boat and swim home. I was free. Damn the Koryak.
I climbed again and stuck my arm out the flap in the container, feeling cold saltwater bite my wounds. I pulled on the flap, and it widened so I could get another hand, another arm through. I felt a rail on the outside surface and grabbed it with both hands, pulling my body through the hole, letting the composite slice into my belly and thigh. It could have gutted me for all I cared. The sun was blinding, but I was out and crouching atop the container and I felt around as I tried to strain my eyes to open. There were puddles of saltwater and I started sliding on the shiny surface. I slid a short distance, then hit a rail and went over the edge.
To my horror, found myself underwater. Now, despite the saltwater sting and the burn of the sun, I willed my eyes open and reached for the shadow of the container, grabbing a rail and clinging tight. I looked around and saw nothing but the container in front of me and open sea all around. The great red bulk of the container swayed with the lapping waves. I lifted myself on top of it again. It had been upended, so it floated vertically, and I crouched on its narrow end, looking around at sea and sky. Fourteen years as a hostage, and this is how it ends.
I was alone in the world.
And I was free.
#
It’s hard to say how long I was sitting on that container. Everything of my former life seemed entombed in that cherry red sepulcher, the great gash in its composite skin matching the gashes in my own, a nasty-torn umbilical. I roused from my half-sleep half-daze as the sun set over the craggy waves. It was so beautiful I cried.
I stared at the spot where the sun had set for an apparent infinity, watching stars rain almost imperceptibly over the horizon. The sea became choppier as I stared into the night. In time, I had to hold the rail. Rain began to rain down, washing the layers of filth from my skin. I had had no shower in…weeks? Months? They told me they would allow me to wash before I reached home. As I rocked in the rain, I let my mind wander. I was remembering now.
The sea train was my ride back home. The Yakuts said they were trading me for prisoners held by Moscow. My life, they said, was worth sixteen prisoners, they’d said, both triumphant and disdainful. I had dared to chuckle. My rapport with the Yakuts had long since mellowed, and when they were around, I had dared to show joviality because I could. Not like earlier on. When the conflict was still fresh and the youths blazed with fervor. At one point early on, they decided they were going to torture me with Thai bamboo. They bought the seeds, and taunted me for weeks about how they were going to strap me down and let the fast-growing stalks grow through me. An excruciating death, it haunted the dreams I had in what little sleep I got back then. In the end, the seeds had refused to grow in the soggy Siberian soil. I had giggled silently at night for weeks after that in my cell. They only caught me once, and that was worth all the nails on my right hand to them---and to me. You take humor where you can.
But I had eased my captors’ fervor. I talked to them when I could, mostly to practice my Yakut or Russian, but it had the effect of easing my captivity. I suppose that’s why their directorate or overlords, or whomever they answered to had hired the Koryak in the eventually. I kept befriending my Yakut and Russian captors. But there was no befriending the Koryak. We didn’t have a common tongue, and he had no interest in talking, to anybody apparently.
The Yakuts told me he’d been a reindeer herder until the reindeer could no longer weather the warming steppes. He had done the honorable thing, slaughtered his herd, his wife and children and mother-in-law, cut ties with the path and found new work in the traditionless future.
The rain was calming to a tiny pitter, and I let myself sleep.
#
I awoke feeling like I was falling into a swamp, grasping at a vine that kept slipping out of my hand. When I realized the vine was the rail and the swamp was several inches of water running in thick rivulets into the gash in the container, I panicked. I slipped on the polished surface, and tried to get to the higher end of the rail, but it was no use. My body had been blocking the flow, and when I moved, seawater poured in a torrent into the hole. In moments, the high end was as low as the low, and my ankles and butt were in water. The whole hole was underwater now, and I could see bursts of air shooting out as the huge container sunk beneath me. I held on until I was up to my waist in water, but at that point it sunk so fast that I was pulled under after it in the undertow. This was the end, I thought. The dim glow of dawn receded above me, lost in bubbles and turbulence, and I tried to gasp but only took in water. I was going to drown.
As the surface darkened and I felt the pressure close in on me, something grabbed me from below, punching my body with the force of a brick wall, all corners and sharp edges. The impact was so forceful that it made a shockwave of sound that felt as though it would pierce my eardrums. The orangey light appeared suddenly before my eyes, and I was forced again above the surface of the water. I flailed, coughing and gasping for air, and I tried to paddle, but my arms just hit cardboard. All around me, an oil slick of shoeboxes, buoying me. I floated on them, spread eagle, coughing the water from my lungs.
I lapsed into and out of consciousness. I know day and night passed at least twice, but I could not be sure of how long it had been. I felt dizzy, cracked lips and muttering drunk in the moments of consciousness, but mostly I slept.
#
The boxes had turned to a slimy foamy mush. My body ached with hunger and thirst. I tried to eat some of the paste that floated all around, but it was fibrous and coated my tongue. I had to rinse with the little seawater I could squeeze out of a handful of the mushy box residue, and the effort left my mouth dry and sore.
Chapter 2
When the old man found me, he prodded me. And in my stupor, I asked if he was real. He put his hand on my shoulder, and I flinched, but all he did was to hoist me onto the little dinghy he towed behind his jet-ski. I looked around with my cracked eyelids and gummy vision, and saw that he had perched me atop a slimy pile of Nikes. I chuckled, and was out again.
#
A dribbling of earthy-green flavor roused me. It took me a moment to realize it was water, but by that time, my body had already instinctively lapped it up. The old man was using some sort of plant roots to dip and dribble into my mouth from a large algae-laced jar.
He offered the root wad to me, saying “De-saleen,” and I dipped it myself, hesitantly at first, then greedily, sating an infinite thirst over and again until I was glutted on the delicious green water.
He offered some sort of seafood jerky, covered in a white powder, and I took it, touching it to the tip of my tongue. The powder was acrid, acid, metallic and wrong-salty. Evidently noticing my trepidation, he handed me a little condiment packet.
“Texas bah-bah-koo,” he said, “Mmm-mmm.”
He gave a rotten-toothed chuckle, and I almost smiled. I squeezed the barbecue sauce on the salted squid and ate as much as I could stand. My stomach was full of water, but still churning with hunger. It had been so long since I had eaten.
The food tasted wrong, but I knew I had to eat something.
I lay on smooth wooden planks, and I felt nauseous, as if the whole world were swaying. I looked around; there were improvised shanties in all directions. Men, women, and children peered from most of the doors. These were homes, studded with bubble wrap and Styrofoam and industrial garbage. The sky was clear blue over their rooves, no sign of larger development. The houses were connected by wooden plank bridges and lashed together with replastic ropes. I had seen such curiosity towards westerners before—we were a rare breed, and getting rarer—but otherwise, it was like no rural village I had ever visited. There wasn’t a single patch of open space; the houses tumbled over one another like a densely packed cityscape. But this was not urban shantytown. It was deathly quiet save for the occasional squawk of a seagull.
As I ate, I stared at the old man. At first, I had thought him a Yakut wearing some kind of traditional cap. But the patterns were wrong…fine threadwork in delicate filigree, tracing elaborate geometries around what looked like an Islamic crescent-and-star. Moreover, his face was wrong; brown-dark instead of ruddy, sunbeaten-leathery instead of cold-bitten leathery.
When he spoke, there was no longer a question.
            “Ya mirá yo con Noe. Noe, Noe,” he said, tapping his chest with bunched fingers.
He repeated the whole exercise several times before I recognized he was speaking some form of Spanish. I hadn’t spoken the language in decades, but I had grown up in the Bronx, and the words came to my lips without hesitation.
“Mi nombre es Lucius. ?Quién eres tú?” Who are you?
“Ya mirá yo con Noe, soy Banguingui.”
“?Que?”
“Baa--ngui-ngui,” he said it as if speaking to a child. “Esta gitano del mar. Ya mir--ah…mi nombre es Noa.”
Gitano del mar, I thought. Sea gypsy.
My stomach began to contract of its own accord. The sea gypsy—Noe—grabbed me under the arms and dragged me to the edge of the nearest walkway. He was ancient and stringy, but strong. His muscles felt like two granite bannisters under mine. As he laid me out on my belly, I wretched fish-tinged algae water over the edge into sloshing seafoam a few feet below. Noe braced himself expertly against a taut rope, stretching a leg through the gap and flicking his foot in water to rinse off the vomit.
He left me alone and waved off the others, and I lay there for long enough to notice late afternoon fade into early evening. I heaved a lot at first, then less and less frequently until I felt wrung-out, empty but for a knot of muscle that was my stomach, almost as hard as Noe’s arms.
When the slow rocking of the whole village slowly became predictable, and when I was finally certain it wasn’t just me, and when I was sure I could, I stood. I seemed to myself to be an emaciated cadaver, weeks beyond death. Yet I stood, doddered.
On one side, the village rose towards a boxy, spire-like structure. On the other, walkways extended between shacks and shanties and, strangely, amid all the roughshod architecture, there stood boats of all sizes and styles, embedded in the village, built into the houses--houses built on, around, and through the boats. The whole structure creaked and lurched subtly. Some of the boats were new, but most were weather-streaked, bubbling rusty or sunbaked, crumbly fiberglass mesh. Sea birds squawked about the masts and corrugated fiberglass roofs.
Noe appeared from one of the boats, a formerly-luxury yacht whose bow jutted through a large structure that could've been straight out of the American Old West. He took me by the elbow, and walked me into the yacht. The smell of campfire tinged with burning plastics hit my nose.
A gathering was taking place around a central fire. Smoke pooled in the high ceiling area, and from the deep chocolaty surfaces, I assumed this must have been used as a meeting place for a very long time. Noe introduced me to a young man in a perfectly clean white daishiki, tallish and sharply chiseled, obviously muscular despite the loose clothing, but with the same features and weather-battered look as Noe. He wore a similar Moslem hat as well.
“You will come tomorrow,” said the man.
“Yes,” I said. I had been well-trained to answer such non-questions.
“Yes?” said the man, clearly surprised. “We had feared you would not want to join us. We are going to scavenge, and it is not safe here. But it is decided then.” He said with a smile. “Come,” he said.
The pair, the old one, and the young, led me into a former ball room, where the fire blazed on bare metal, ringed-in by heavy anchor chain, links big enough to put your foot through.
The sea gypsies lined the walls, kneeling in rows four-deep all the way around, six-deep in some places. A young boy held the ubiquitous hat out to me, and I bowed for him to put it on me. It was tight, but it made me feel slightly less awkward.

The chiseled young man took me to the top of the tower, supporting me much of the way as I protested on behalf of my atrophied legs. But he was persistent, and when we arrived in a spire peak, I saw why. Over a low rail, I could see the whole town sprawling around the base of the tower. We were afloat in the ocean on an artificial island cobbled together of flotsam.
After the ceremonies, I joined Noe in his family's cabin built atop a catamaran. Through the walls, I heard the rattle of chains clacking across metal. At first, there were only one or two, then a few more, and soon, the individual clatters became one gurgling noise. Noe and his family were wrapping and placing seemingly random household items—a brass vase, a box of tea—into a large metal box. When a woman, presumably Noe's wife, saw me, she protested, saying something in the strange Spanish patois. It was clear she didn't want me there, and I bowed my head to step back outside onto the boardwalk, where the clatter was a waterfall roar.
Seeing the family struck a chord in me. I never did have a family of my own, but I remember being part of several. Behavior problems sent me from home to home throughout my teen years, and I never did participate in anything like helping my parents pack a trunk of valuables. There was something about that. I don’t know. It got to me.
Disgusted with myself, I wiped the tearwelling from my eyelids.
“Siento, siento,” said Noe with his hands in the air before him. He shut the rickety honeycomb plastic door behind him.
Noe put a hand on my shoulder, and though it was gentle, I flinched. Noe grimaced at my timidity and almost shook his head. Then, his demeanor shifted abruptly. 
“Durme por Jiho la noche,” he said, laughingly.
#
Jiho turned out to be a tiny kayak suspended above the entrance to the town hall, and accessible only after Noe set up a one-post ladder with a few rungs missing. “Honor grande,” Noe had said, but I suspected the reason they had me sleep with Jiho was because she was visible to virtually every shack and hut in what passed for a central square.
The shiny red kayak was a cocoon, swaying at about twice the rate of the village itself, the crimson glow of the Hall’s stadium lighting filtered through Jiho’s hull cast a shadow across my eyes in the shape of the craft’s name. When the light shut out with a klang! I was alone, floating in the swaying darkness, swaddled in my clothes alone as I had slept every night for the last fourteen years. I got to sleep well enough, but I awoke before dawn with dreams of jelly sea creatures storming the village, prying me bodily from a red locker that must’ve been dream-Jiho.
Noe found me staring down the dock, and put his hand on my shoulder in a gesture that was becoming almost comfortable. I had stopped flinching at any rate.
He pointed to the end of the dock and I saw, like the specks of distant birds in flight, a fleet of what must've been jet skis."Vamos!" he shouted with a krinklefaced chuckle. He shooed me down the dock. "Vamos, vamos, vamos!" he repeated all the while as I boarded the bobbing jet ski. I cranked the accelerator fast, too fast. It tossed me in the water and I flailed and choked and saw a splash next to me that I mistook for an explosion. By the time I realized it was Noe, jumped in to save me, I was in tears with laughter. Noe shook his wet hair, splashing me like a freshly bathed dog. It was my first laugh since I lost my fingernails, and the first genuine, untempered joy since I don't remember.
#
We worked til dusk, skiing from patch to patch, picking pieces from submerged masses of flotsam. When we were about to head back--or, at least, I'd hoped we were--a sound, a shout from the lead skier. A wave of the arm propagated from person to person, back through the dispersed villagers, and like a pack of wild dogs, they fell into one double line.
In the distance, I saw a peak, like a little mountain on the ocean. As the line of jet skis forded the wide ocean into orange pools of sunset, the dark shape grew. It was an island.
No, as we drew closer, I saw it was no island at all. It was a floating yacht, upended so the bow jutted from the water vertically. Around it like seaweed surrounding floating driftwood, was a clot of…of…things. All sorts of things, but mostly plastic zip ties. I had to laugh. It was a bitter laugh. I had spent so many years shackled about the wrists and ankles with such ties. Noe's wife gave me a quick quixotic glance and I stopped.
Following the suit of the villagers, I slowed my jet ski as we plowed through the floating zip tie jungle, and slowly scooted up onto the edge of a more substantial clot made primarily of milk jugs, hardened over in calcarious concretions. The jugs cracked under the hulls of the jet skis, but soon our vehicles were moored as solidly as if we were beached in lime sand.
I looked around, and was reassured that a couple of the villagers were circling out at sea, keeping watch, and carefully, I dismounted the jet ski and tip-toed onto an island of mineralized plastic. The yacht's hull jutted from the concretion of trash, and I used it for balance. As the villagers rounded this artificial peak, they picked among the milk jugs, finding a piece of glass here, a rusted key chain there. One time, a child yelped with joy at pulling a thick silver chain from between the milk jug boulders. As we crossed around the bottom of the boat, a great squawking arose. We had disturbed a bird colony.
The villagers waved their arms, as if imitating the birds, and the birds mocked back, adding to the taunt biting beaks. But the villagers persisted, methodically gathering eggs from guano-covered nests. The eggs were passed back one pair of hands to another, in a train, that included me, and concluded in a pile near the beached jet skis. I passed the eggs along. They came quickly as I could pass. In the brief moment between passes, I tried to fetch a turtle scute that was wedged between the calcified milk jugs, only to find that I was holding up the line. It was the first scornful look I had earned since my time in the village, but it made me smile because I realized I had received not a single rebuke--save for the one from Noe's wife, but that may have been something else--since I had been thrown off the sea train.
At last, my desire got hold of me and I bent down to pick the scute out during a momentary pause in the egg train. It was jammed tight, and I had to stick almost my whole hand, more than halfway up the palm to get a pair of fingers forked awkwardly around its sharp end, like clumsy pincers. I wiggled it back and forth and back and forth for what seemed like a thousand wiggles before it cut me and I yanked back my hand with a yelp. In that moment, I looked up and saw that though I’d blocked the narrow ledge the egg train wound over, the villagers had not been slowed at all by my selfishness. The one in front of me pitched it over my head, higher now that I stood, and the one behind me, a young girl caught it in both her arms before swinging it around to the next villager and snapping round to catch another.
Without taking the time to further consider this odd behavior, I tucked the scute into the fold of my waistband. Immensely satisfied with my efforts today, and warmed in my heart by my fortuitous discovery of the gorgeous, waxy scute, I boarded my jet ski with the villagers and followed them back to the floating village in the sea.
#
After three weeks living with the village, eating their food, working their work, it occurred to me that I hadn’t stopped counting days.
For the first fifty-five days of my captivity, I had been plagued by the Yakut guards’ efforts to disrupt me by erasing the marks I made on the walls, or by making new ones. I used pebbles for another twelve days before they caught on, and for three more, I tried to deliberately soil the corner of my pillow once for each sunrise.
The guards’ removal of that lasted until I was loaded in a coffin-sized box and onto the sea train. That coffin-sized box with a pillow was a brief, dark heaven.
On the seventieth day of my captivity, I had spent several hours investigating a clot of crabgrass that grew out from under a small cobble that sat in the ray of sunlight that shone in from the hole in the roof through which my cell was constantly dampened by the cold Siberian rain. I found that if I lifted the corner of the cobble, I could tease the grass roots into a pattern, two parallel roots for each day. Five and two, five and two…one parallel set for each workday and two sets for each weekend. Five and two, five and two. Every week, I twisted up the roots and tucked them into the corner, laying them end to end so it looked like a tree root was slowly sneaking its tendrils along the wall. That lasted me eight hears,  seven weeks, and three days before I realized I didn’t need the grassroots anymore to count the fives and twos. 
I’m not sure why I never stopped counting weekends.

A few fives and twos into my stay with the Sea Gypsies, and I found myself invited to stay with Noe and family. They’d rigged up a little rowboat to hang underneath a trap door in the planked floor that served as their kitchen, a few feet above the frothy ocean. They gave me a few minutes of time alone in the kitchen every evening to wash up, but in the mornings I awoke to the smell of Mrs. Noe and her daughter, whose name I never learned, cooking sticky rice.
