The Woman Who Was Too Colorful

I first met Marla Quinn under fluorescent café lights at dawn. She was wearing a jacket made of mismatched neon fabrics, riotous against the dawn-gray world outside. I was grading papers at a rickety table when she sat down across from me, eyes like two green photons in the dim. “You’re covered in glitter,” she said, pointing at my notes. She smiled and I realized we were starting a conversation I didn’t know how to finish. The air was normal coffee-shop air: the slight burn of espresso, nervous chatter. And then a single word—color—slithered into my brain. It was as if every color in the world was squatting in that café. She was too colorful for reality. My chest felt a little lighter. It could have been my heart trying to speak. I took a breath and said the first thing that came to mind: “I’m Nathan.” She handed me a smile and a business card embossed with lightning. “Call me,” it said, in scrawled Gothic letters. I left with a head dizzy with delight and confusion. That night, my phone blinked with her messages as I lay awake. She typed of our shared exhibitionism, how we each get to be seen, how piggyback rides to the infinite might start with a feather. We traded secrets by text in the dark. She laughed at my fascination with language and the word polynormal, like it was some inside joke yet to be born. When I teased, “So I should be honored to share your secret fetish album?” she responded with a wink and a sly remark that the real prize was honesty itself, laid bare. I realized our game was sincere: to cultivate play, even in the strangest tasks. It felt exhilarating, like learning a new syntax with each keystroke. A few days later, I found myself at her apartment on a Tuesday night. The door opened into a thunderstorm of half-packed books, paint-streaked walls, and amber lamplight. She draped a scarf of polka dots around me, as if anointing me with her warmth. “Welcome to the Vernacular,” she said softly, and I didn’t know if she meant her tongue or this maze of conversation. In her living room, the air smelled of cedar and ginger tea. Jazz played on vinyl, notes leaping like electric fish. Marla collected the broken words of our chats — doodles in spiral journals and tip-drunk scribbles — and arranged them into a shrine on the floor. Each scrap was a world of its own: the outline of a constellation we called Hope, a frightened rabbit sketched in ink, a feather pinned with a poem. I realized she meant for me to read this altar. It was her personal mythology, vibrant and alive, waiting for a chapter that had yet to be written. I woke up holding the last page of her journal at dawn, sunlight burning through the curtains. Marla was still asleep on the couch, face smudged with dream-seraphim. I leaned closer and realized her breath was shallow, her skin almost translucent. In that early light, it was like my colorful goddess had turned to a pale ghost. She moaned softly, eyes fluttering through sleep. Suddenly she bolted upright, a scream tearing out of her. Her fingers clutched her chest as if something inside was crucified. I had never seen her panic like this. The air snapped; even the shadows recoiled. Without thinking I called 911 and rocked her back and forth. She was speaking in fragments now — phrases about wiring and bright storms in her head — and glass shards of delirium. Ambulance lights bled through the blinds. When the EMTs arrived, she was hurling cosmic shapes into the linoleum. In the emergency room, the world was sharp corners and white noise. The nurses taped warning labels, checked boxes. A doctor murmured a diagnosis I couldn’t hear. Behind me, a monitor counted her pieces: a thundering pulse in green numbers. I gripped the counter and felt the countertop engrave itself into my palm; they moved me away politely, as if I were an echo. I talked, but my voice lost its edges — just another fixture in that clinical box. When they finally delivered her to a bed, it felt like watching her live in X-rays. It was someone else’s body flickering in an old film projector. The next morning, light crept through the curtains like something hopeful. Marla hummed in bed and sipped tea, as if uncertain how the day should start. She didn’t remember everything, only that I had been there, a constant in her nightmare. We sat at the kitchen table in silence, and I realized everything had changed. She gave me a crooked smile — for the first time since the fever, the world felt possible again. She is a marvelnot, a cardinal error recast as lotion of luminous anguish; I, an itinerant firestorm, clasp her echo to confess: we are star-packed cannibals of meaning, splitting photon-grief like blood in a rainbow. Cascading idioms of want flare: toxic-glitter nostalgia, everybird logic, seraph-cry. Neon filaments of feeling coil the sky. Our vernacular is soredshell and dreamborne revelation, an aurora-grown manifesto. The night itself blinks and bleeds, syntax fractured into petals. And in this unscripted cosmos we bloom unaccountable, irrevocable.
