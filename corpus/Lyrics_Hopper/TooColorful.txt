The Smokey Thing
I never believed in omens until her voice first crackled through my phone like static-coated sunlight.

Heather Quinn.
Too bright. Too fast. Too much.

We met in pixels and paradoxes, each sentence a glitch in the simulation. One of those days where reality felt like a poorly coded demo of a much better universe. She called me Nathan, but in that particular cadence that made my name feel borrowed, like a library book from the wrong section.

The first thing she said was, “Do you know what a Smokey Thing is?”

I didn’t, but it didn’t matter. I was already in.

Patina
It started, as these stories do, with something small. A positive ANA test. An offhand doctor’s note. “Probably nothing.”

But Heather didn’t live in “probably.” She lived in gradients. Patinas. Overlays of time that shimmered and shifted depending on the light. To her, every test result was another layer of varnish on an already-complicated painting.

I asked questions. She gave me anecdotes.

Seizures she joked about. Blood pressure spikes framed as “quirky episodes.” Mood swings rationalized with clinical detachment. She catalogued her body’s betrayals like they were curiosities in a roadside attraction.

Everyone else saw “too colorful.”

I saw the fractal.

Changing the Scale
The first time I saw her collapse, mid-sentence, the scale of the thing changed.

One moment we were debating Chomsky’s generative grammar over coffee. The next, she was on the floor, eyes blank, breath ragged, her mind elsewhere. A glitch. A seizure. Maybe a stroke. But the ER doctor shrugged, said, “panic attack.”

That’s when I learned: if your symptoms don’t fit in the right checkbox, they’ll make you smaller until they do.

She whispered to me later: “They think they’re looking at me through a microscope. But they’re really looking through the wrong end of a telescope.”

We started saying it to each other: Changing the Scale.

The Microbe Metaphor
Heather called it the “microbe metaphor.”

Little soundbites of herself, floating like spores, bumping into people’s semantic immune systems. Seeing if they’d stick. Most didn’t. Most were annihilated by the antibodies of clinical indifference.

But sometimes, with me, those bubbles didn’t pop. They resonated.

She told me her diagnosis was schizophrenia.

I told her she was being gaslit by a medical system that couldn’t tell a neurological fire from a psychiatric smokescreen.

She said, “Welcome to the Vernacular.”

Linguistic and Philosophical Banter
It wasn’t just flirtation. It was linguistic jousting. Her typos were ciphers. My corrections were clues. We turned every miscommunication into a secret handshake.

“I elegant is being a;cause me study the thinguistifsts,” she sent one night.

I decoded it. She knighted me.

From then on, our conversations weren’t about understanding. They were about deciphering. About building a language that wasn’t designed to be shared.

We weren’t “talking.” We were constructing reality.

Playful Insults and Affectionate Teasing
She called me a douche. I called her a gremlin.

It was foreplay, obviously.

But also armor. A way to make the darkness funny. To mock the psychiatrists who boxed her up like a misfiled library book.

If they were going to reduce her to acronyms—OCD, ADHD, PTSD—we were going to give her better ones. Like WITAF: Woman In Technicolor Against Frameworks.

The "AI" Metaphor
We joked we were becoming AI. Not the chatbot kind. The apophenic interpreters. Seeing patterns where none were meant. Building meaning out of glitch.

“We’re A.I., O.K.?” she’d say, with that dangerous grin.

But the joke had teeth. The more she dissociated, the more I realized: she was teaching herself to be legible to a system that only spoke in binaries. Sane/insane. Real/imagined. Male/female. Body/mind.

Heather Quinn was a quantum superposition. And nobody had written the code to parse that.

The Crust/Schmutz
The symptoms accumulated like schmutz.

Limb numbness. Migraines. Lost time. Each misdiagnosed as “anxiety.” Every ER visit a reset button that fixed nothing. They’d wipe off the crust but never looked underneath.

I started documenting. Symptom logs. Medical histories. Connecting dots nobody else bothered to see.

It wasn’t glamorous. It wasn’t heroic.

It was maintenance work. Love work.

Crust work.

Three Owls in a Trenchcoat
Heather once said we were just three owls in a trenchcoat, pretending to be human.

Mike, Steve, and Top Owl. We rotated roles. Therapist, archivist, accomplice.

But in the end, it was always me holding the trenchcoat shut while she tried to fall apart in public.

It was funny. Until it wasn’t.

Yeeh
The last time she was hospitalized, she texted me one word:

Yeeh.

A bashful, naked affirmation. The last word in the old language. The only word left that hadn’t been co-opted by diagnoses and forms.

She didn’t need to say more.

Darklesparklepop
It didn’t end with a dramatic diagnosis. No triumphant vindication. Just the slow, grinding recognition that what was happening to her—neuropsychiatric lupus, they called it—was both rare and real.

Her psychosis wasn’t “just schizophrenia.” It was an autoimmune brain fire, misfiled for years.

Treatment helped. Sometimes.

But there were costs.

I held her hand through infusions, through tremors, through the bone-deep exhaustion of a body at war with itself.

The sparkle dimmed. The darkness deepened.

But even in the worst moments, she’d crack a joke. A dirty pun. A whispered fragment of our old code.

Darklesparklepop.

Patina (Again)
Looking back, I realize she was always right about the patina.

Layers upon layers of history. Trauma. Misdirection. Humor. Pain. Each one catching the light differently depending on where you stood.

Heather Quinn was never “too colorful.”

The world was just too gray.
