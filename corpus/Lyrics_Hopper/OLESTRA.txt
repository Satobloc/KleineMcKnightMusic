Overall, it should be seen as a posterboy of the harm of the anti-science streak in our culture

Yes—with the important qualification that the anti-science failure wasn’t simply “people distrusted a chemical.” Initial caution was reasonable: olestra was novel, long-term nutritional effects deserved study, and much of the favorable research was industry-funded. The failure was what happened afterward: caution hardened into cultural certainty while the evidence continued to accumulate in the opposite direction.

It is an unusually clean example of several anti-scientific habits converging:

Treating disgust as toxicological evidence.

Ignoring dose and substituting “can happen” for “normally happens.”

Giving anecdotes more weight than controlled trials and surveillance.

Demanding near-zero risk from an innovation while leaving the harms of the incumbent—calories, saturated fat, and then-common trans fat—outside the comparison.

Applying intense skepticism to corporate evidence but almost none to activist rhetoric.

Refusing to update after FDA reconsidered the evidence and removed the warning.

Converting a technical question into a permanent comic identity that no subsequent finding could penetrate.

The deepest pathology was precautionary asymmetry. Olestra had to answer for every imaginable consequence of its existence; ordinary snack fats never had to answer for the consequences of remaining dominant. The status quo was treated as neutral when it was itself an intervention with an enormous accumulated health burden.

So yes: olestra belongs beside nuclear power, genetically engineered crops, and perhaps certain artificial sweeteners as a poster child for how culturally amplified fear can destroy a potentially useful technology without ever conducting the relevant comparison. Not “Is it perfectly harmless?” but “Compared with the thing it would actually replace, what is the net outcome?” Our culture largely never asked that question. It heard the world’s most lethal punchline and stopped thinking.
