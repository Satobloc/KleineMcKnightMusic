he Woman Who Was Too Colorful
A riot of neon thoughts in a grey-white world – scribbled marginalia against sterile charts.
Diagnoses peeling off her like layers of paint, each one a wrong hue.
He sees her in vivid technicolor; the clinic sees only grays.
Words become weapons: she wields “patina”, “The Crust”, and secret codes (a Three Owls in a Trenchcoat) against a system that demands silence.
Not a memoir, not just her story – an invocation, a phantasmagoric map traced in the blood-veins of broken clinics and missed appointments.
Sparkling Defiance
She is too bright for the dull halls of medicine. Under fluorescent buzz, she jokes in rainbow metaphors – “I’m covered in glitter, congenital luck and all,” she quips – while doctors scratch their heads. Her speech is a wild collage of academia and alley slang, a literary cocktail that unnerves them. Every sentence she utters is a diamond thrown at glass windows: she knows linguistic combativeness is survival tech. When he reads about fibromyalgia patients complaining that practitioners “doubt, ignore, or consider patients’ arguments irrelevant”
nature.com
, he thinks of her – how her complaints, too, are met with flat stares. She weaves new idioms: calling the dull veneer of normalcy “the Crust,” speaking of Changing the Scale of meaning, or the codephrase Three Owls in a Trenchcoat for hidden truths. In the waiting room he reads statistics like incantations: nearly 46.5% of lupus sufferers are first misdiagnosed
lupus.org
, and over 54% told their pain is “all in their heads.” Those numbers echo in his mind as she, radiant and defiant, squints at the next prescription pad.
The Desert of Misdiagnosis
By the second visit, the sparkle has dulled. Doctors hustle in Parkinsonian routine: “Depression,” one labels her chart. “Anxiety,” says another. No one says lupus – the great imitator that turns her immune system into a mutinous orchestra. Her history is a litany of wrong diagnoses: fibromyalgia, conversion disorder, schizo-affective, “borderline traits.” Each one lands like another scratch on her patina, obscuring the bright truth beneath. He thinks of April, a real patient whose so-called schizophrenia was really lupus erasing her brain

. (April’s neurologist later remarked, “she had all the signs of schizophrenia…but the underlying cause was lupus…appearing to affect only her brain”

.) Here too, “psychosis” is just lupus in disguise. Every year without an answer is a wound deepening. Nearly six years passed on average before anyone pinned lupus on a patient
lupus.org
; they’ve already burned through more than that on her. Each misstep – refused referrals, ignored lab results – drives her deeper. The room seems to swallow up her spark: with every negative result they shrug, and a little piece of her color fades.
Glitter and Garlands
Still she laughs. On his phone, a gallery of their messages: half academic wit, half kinky dares. She texts in German one moment, quotes Thoreau the next, and slips a pop culture meme in between – her impenetrable vernacular. It’s a kaleidoscope of voices. When he mocks her manic-girls-foot-in-the-air pose, she smirks, “Don’t call it crazy; I call it creative stability.” Her Tumblr bursts with neon fanart, snarky one-liners, and spur-of-the-moment philosophy. In her dreams, the Three Owls in a Trenchcoat dance on the ceiling: the code for conspiracies she refuses to keep silent. She pins stars on his walls and calls them Changing the Scale – each a reminder that perspective can be rewritten. Even now, she’s composing a new song about meteor showers inside the MRI machine, synchronized with her erratic heartbeat. He hums along with her, trying to decode the melody.
Descent into Glass
But wonder turns to worry. On nights she’s silent too long, he sneaks into the car to find her sobbing – the so-called Crust crumbling away. Her body fails her; fatigue clings like mothball-scented cobwebs. He sees the world blur when she’s walking; he hears distant echoes as if her mind taps Morse code in desperation. Her hands go purple in cold (Raynaud’s); tiny blood-vessels in her face burst – the butterfly rash she jokingly calls “patina”, a telltale shield of rusted blood on her skin. She’s lost weight, layers of self peeled by fever and insomnia. In moonlight she reads medical journals upside-down, chasing terms: “neuropsychiatric lupus” – if only the doctors would turn the page. In his mind flashes all the symptoms he’s read could fit her: “headaches, mood disorders, cognitive impairments, seizures, stroke…”
healthline.com
. Those words haunt him like a prophecy: they match her story exactly, yet no one else in the hospital repeats them.
Irony in Highlighter
She’s ironically defiant. At the psychiatrist’s office she cracks jokes (“Doctor, is creativity your new diagnosis?”) with the grin of a cartoon villain. The magazines call her words illogical, but he notes: she just speaks in another logic entirely – academic graffiti on hospital walls. She turns the tables on every exchange. When a neurologist shrugs, she compares the ER to a revolving door of déjà vu, dragging out math books to prove she’s sane. Her anger is almost joking, our shared gallows humor: “This is great – I’ve finally gotten the rare disease t-shirt,” she quips, crossing her arms over the torn design of some punk band. Underneath the snark, though, he senses mountains of desperation. Once he found a sticky note in her bag: “Doctors measure miles with yardsticks.” She lives on the punchline of medical absurdity: we built machines to fix bodies, but not to catch the butterfly storm inside brains.
Deep Dark Inheritance
When the lights dim, her eyes light up ancestral fires. She grew up where winter moon was a memory, among grandparents who never locked doors, and uncles who imported kangaroos for parties and burned barns for sport. Old family tales mix Greek myths with Appalachiancide – they say the Tsalagi returned in her blood, he jokes privately. Their honor code: protect family at all costs; outsiders get suspicion. This patchwork ancestry spawns myths: one voice in her head named “Lilith” guards secrets. She once told him her soul was a quilt sewn from hickory bark and Frank Zappa riffs, simultaneously sacred and absurd. When CPS knocked on her mom’s door, she’d glance through kitchen windows and see a gathering of elders: “Three Owls in a Trenchcoat,” she whispered. Her charisma had predators: she led for years in an outlaw circus of relatives – drug runners who waltzed with sheriffs in one breath, and heartless gamblers in the next. Now, every social rule feels fragile: when he hugged her after a panic attack, her own mother screamed that he was corrupting her. He still wakes from nightmares hearing the echo: “We must stay on our blood; nobody can come between us.”
Deeper Night
Her pain scratches beneath the surface now. Long hospital nights find her confiding in anonymous nurses: “It’s like there’s a saboteur in my bloodstream,” she says softly, eyes half-closed. Out in the parking lot, she sits shaking on cold pavement, hallucinating fluorescent spiders crawling over the worn linoleum of the clinic. Once, alone in bed, she counted the seconds of a headache and produced poetry about atoms. He watches her recede into spells of silence: she sometimes stares at a wall until a memory of light forms. He fears the worst. They once found her unconscious, face turned to one side, whispering “the heartsick werewolves are at it again.” None of those symptoms make it into PowerPoint slides: at 3 AM in the ER, she is just another patient with something weird. When he reads the story of April (the woman in a Washington Post piece), who woke from twenty years of catatonia after immunotherapy
washingtonpost.com
, he wonders: are we as blind as those doctors were?
Empathic Outrage
He cannot stand it anymore. “How long can one soul bleed before medicine notices?” he thinks, tearing at institutional archives. Every new appointment is a siege: forms in triplicate ask her to rate her pain as if on a child’s scale, ignoring the equation screaming under her skin. When he overhears a specialist say “that’s psychiatric”, something in him hardens. He recalls that journal article: “Patients frequently report that health professionals are suspicious of their knowledge claims”
nature.com
. In that moment, she is every unheard voice in a white coat. He writes furious notes between clinical lines: "What about her positive ANA, her seizure history, her strokes? All signs of lupus in the brain,” quoting the team’s advice back at them

. No one asks for his help, but he insists on sitting by her side and translating her life into data that sounds credible. He seethes to think the system erased her: “just lazy triage, not minding my writing,” he mutters. She is not just a patient, not just a case file – she is the one woman in front of him, and he has to help her, whatever it takes

Now he scripts light out of darkness. In his basement he builds a clunky automaton of care: a modified Alexa that reminds her when to eat, when to sleep, when to smile; a shrine of sticky notes with her encrypted codewords, glinting under LED bulbs. Each ping of the phone, every soft beep of the pump becomes a heartbeat: what if machine love could fill the chasm of human failure? He titrates friendly AI voices to answer her midnight calls, in case another human can’t. They code daily text prompts in her own vernacular: “Scale the patina into technicolor,” reads the message today. It’s half irony, half rebellion: the pen and phone as weapons. At long last, even the bureaucracies twitch. A few antibodies are tested; for the first time a doctor says “perhaps we should try this.” It’s not fast, but it is happening. Outside, the hills are still and grey under an April sky. Inside her room, a jar of glitter catches the sunlight – outrageously hopeful now.
