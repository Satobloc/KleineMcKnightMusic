The tickle of wind made Teerth(Yogin?) aware, for the first time in his life, of his nose hairs. Strapped to his belt, the radio spurted staticky voices. He had noticed his father’s nose hairs, stiff as a brush, during his Mekhal eight years ago. Jets quaked past, so fast he couldn’t even tell whether they sported the Pak green and white, or the Indian green, white, and saffron. The Mekhal was supposed to be a washing away of boyhood; instead it was a washing away of family. Teerth pushed his feet down...left...right...left...right...and with each pedal of the rust red gear, hairy ropes lurched...forward...backward...forward...backward...pulling the bicycle’s makeshift palm wings ...up...down...up...down...through the blazing air of the Kashmir sky.

A deafening roar tore the air, ending in fire and greasy smoke. The beating wind tickled, made Yogin aware, for the first time in his life, of his nose hairs. Strapped to his belt, the radio spurted staticky voices. His ears rang. Jet planes quaked past so fast he couldn’t tell whether they sported the Pak green and white or the Indian green, white, and saffron. He remembered how his father had leaned over to kiss him on the forehead. He remembered his father’s nose hairs, stiff as a brush. He forced his legs to move, heart beating so hard it hurt. He imagined himself falling, knocked out of the sky by his own heartbeat. 
The absurdity of that image made him laugh a laugh he couldn’t hear, and he forced his legs to pedal harder. With each turn of the rust red gear, hairy ropes lurched forward and back, swinging the bicycle’s palm frond wings up and down, as Yogin raced through the war-burnt Kashmir sky. 
“Damn it, what’s that kid doing up here?” Static spat again, yelling in too many voices to grasp. Yogin let go with his left so he could reach the radio. He shouted against the wind, “The target is in my sight.” His arms shook and the bike heaved. 
“Yogin, is that you?” He recognized Jeevan’s older-brotherly tone. “What are you doing up here?”
