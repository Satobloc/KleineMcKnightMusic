The air in the small apartment felt thick, heavy with unspoken things. Dust motes danced in the weak afternoon light slanting through the blinds. I hadn’t bothered to turn on the main lights. Didn’t seem much point.
Kendra stood just inside the doorway, a travel bag slung over her shoulder. She hadn't said anything yet, just looked at the place. Her gaze swept over the clutter – the empty food containers, the discarded clothes, the notebook open on the coffee table, filled with my scratchy attempts at Jovian script. Her lips were pressed into a thin line.
“God Lucy, you’re such a mess,” she finally said, her voice low, almost a sigh.
I just looked at her. Tried to make sense of the familiarity. It had been… how long? The years blurred. Toronto seemed like another planet.
“Sorry,” I mumbled, looking down at my hands. They felt too big, clumsy. “I’m so sorry; I know you’ve been through a lot; I shouldn’t have. I was just answering the message from Ramloco”. The excuse felt flimsy even to my own ears.
Without thinking, I took a step towards her. She flinched, a quick jerk of her shoulders, but I kept coming. I put my arms around her and pulled her tight. Tears pricked at my eyes, a stinging behind the lids.
I hugged her, buried my face in her hair. It smelled faintly of something clean, something… Kendra. The sounds coming from my mouth were inarticulate, a strangled mix of a laugh and a sob.
She pulled away, her hands coming up to rest on my arms. I braced myself. Feared she’d bolt. But instead, she put her hands on my face, her fingers digging lightly into my beard, her forehead leaning against my chin.
“Luce, I don’t know where to begin,” she said, her voice muffled against my chest.
I pushed her back a little so I could look into her eyes. My sister. My real sister. I shook my head, a small, disbelieving movement.
The rims of her hazel eyes welled. Soon we were both crying, a silent downpour of joy and… something else. Loss, maybe. It was too much. My legs felt like they were going to buckle. I crumpled, went down on all fours on the bare wooden floor, and just… bawled. She was there, her hand rubbing my back, a soft, shhhh-shhhh sound.
After a long moment, the sobs subsided, leaving a raw ache in my chest. I pushed myself up, leaning against the edge of the couch. Kendra was still kneeling, her eyes red-rimmed but steady.
“Stay,” I said, the word barely a whisper.
She just looked at me. A long, searching look that seemed to see right through the layers of grime and exhaustion I’d accumulated. Then, she nodded, a small, decisive movement.
“I called a ground car,” she said, her voice still a little thick. “It’ll be around the corner any minute”.
The silence stretched again, not uncomfortable this time, but charged. We just looked at each other, two people who shared a past neither fully understood, facing a present that felt fragile and uncertain. The blinking Christmas lights across the street threw faint green shadows on the wall. It wasn't much, but for the first time in a long time, the darkness in the room didn't feel quite so absolute.
