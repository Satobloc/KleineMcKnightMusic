Prologue: The Flying Dutchman

Josey fought against the cuffs, his wrists aching, but the three coppers held him, two by
the arms and one by the back of his dingy overalls.
"Do I look like Dutch royalty to you?" He hurled the words like spittle and then hocked and
spat phlegm on the jailhouse floor. It wasn't a lie exactly. It was true, he looked more like he'd
just come off a chain gang than a royal frigate. Moreover his deep, woody irises and blackolive
skin bespoke anything but royal linaeage.
"Who you think you're foolin?"
"I'm from Trinidad," shouted Josey, "just look at me. I'm no Dutchman!"
"Could be black Dutch," said a fourth cop, calmly filling out paperwork on a clipboard,
"anyway it ain't none of my business, all's I know is I got orders to arrest one Josiah Davis
Coelho. And, according to your papers, that's you."
"You got the wrong guy!" coughed Josey. "Just look at my papers, I'm no Dutchman.
Don't you see, whoever you're looking for--they're--they're just using my name. Anybody could
have gotten hold of the manifest and--"
"Shut yer trap, prisoner," said the beefiest of the four cops, a young man of an officer who
looked almost as tough as Josey himself.
"He ain't Dutch."
The voice came from behind, but Josey couldn't see who was speaking. He tried to
stand, but the cops were putting the weight of three men on him, forcing his knees to grind
concrete.
"Could be black Dutch," said one of the coppers.
"Ain't no such thing," came the voice again.
"And what do you know?" The younger cop let go and spun around to face the
newcomer. "Oh," he gasped, "Sargeant van Niedermeijer. I didn't realize--"
Josey tried to shake free of the two remaining cops, only to be wrestled to the floor, face
in the dust.
"You trying to say I don't know what a fuckin Dutch looks like? Is your name Sargeant van
Niedermeijer?"
"No," mumbled the young officer, looking down as he abruptly doffed his hat.
"Speak up rookie, I'm talking to you."
"No, sir! Um... I mean... Sorry, I meant, 'No sir, Sargeant.'"
"Alright then. Let the negro go," said van Niedermeijer.
"I'm Indian," said Josey.
"What?"
"I'm no negro, I'm East Indian," repeated Josey.
"Shut your trap and count your blessings, because you're off the hook for now. Now get
out of my sight." Sargeant van Niedermeijer took the clipboard and shuffled around a corner as
the other coppers led Josey, still cuffed, to the station counter and waited in line behind a couple
of prostitutes and a man with the dull red eyes of an addict. The young officer held his hat in his
hand and all of them waited silently while paperwork shuffled across the desk. Josey glanced
around expectantly at the coppers' stony faces.
Finally, "I thought you were going to let me go," said Josey.
"We'll get you your stuff and have you on your way just as soon as possible," replied the
young officer in a polite but unapologetic tone.
More than half an hour later, they reached the counter and the haggered old cop behind
it, who said, "You sure this ain't the guy? He matches the description posted in the head."

"Yeah," said the young officer, "we gotta let the negro go. Straight from van Niedermeijer."
"Alright," said the older cop as he pulled out the evidence box from under the counter,
"here's your belongings." He eyed Josey suspiciously but said nothing more.
One of the cops asked the other behind the counter to fetch the suspect description for
them to read, then he unshackled Josey's wrists and said, "You're free to go, Mr. Coelho. Grab
your stuff and get going."
In the beat-up wooden box were belongings that had been pulled out of Josey's pockets
by his arresting officers--a pouch of tobacco, rolling papers, and a mother-of-pearl pen knife
attached to a small ring of keys. He noticed two things immediately: First, his six silver dollars
and change were gone. This was no surprise, of course. Josey had been arrested before, and
lifting a suspect's cash and coin was the closest he had yet seen to "standard procedure".
What was surprising to Josey, however, was the second thing he noticed: There were
several objects here that had definitely not been in his pockets when he'd gotten jumped by the
coppers on his way home from the docks--an enormous red stone set in a heavy pewter thumb
ring, two other little pewter objects that looked like they might be lockets of some sort, and a city
map. Josey thought it looked like a map of Brooklyn, though he didn't recognize anything
specifically, and there were no labels on the streets.
Josey gathered the small objects into his pockets and paused, momentarily at a loss as
to where he could keep the large, rolled map. Seeing the old cop return to the desk inspecting a
sheet of paper with a puzzled look on his face, saying "You sure that ain't him?" Josey stuffed
the map down his undershirt and quickly fled. One of the other officers said "Hey there, just you
wait a minute." Josey pretended not to hear and walked as calmly as he could down the short
hall to the station's entryway. "Get back here!" His heart was racing, but he made it to the front
steps just as shouts of "Get him!" and a roar of footfalls raised behind him.
Josey darted into the crowded Manhattan street, clutching the strange map in his shirt as
he dodged between businessmen and society ladies. He was on the run once more.

THE TURNERS
A young blond boy, who was roughly the size and shape of a bail of hay, took the stage
before the Turnverein, reciting a poem about a mouse in a barnyard. Though he started timidly

father had passed away had left his home because he didn't want to inherit responsibility for the
household. His brothers begged him to stay, but he left the house in the charge of the middle
brother. After starting a home of his own in the western corner of the barnyard, he found that he
was so far away from the warmth and food of the farmhouse that none of the girl mice wanted to

the mother mouse was pronounced dead and the eldest of the mouse brothers became an old

He often wished that Liesl had never asked for his hand in marriage, but she was wealthy
