It was past midnight when they first appeared, the blooms—a pulsing, phosphorescent blue—sprouting along the cracked asphalt of Route 18. By dawn, they had crept like veins up the skeletal remains of the old Texaco sign, winding around the warped metal and spreading their glow into the pre-dawn dark. The town of Greendale, tucked into the lazy elbow of the Missouri River, awoke to this new, electric intrusion.

Mara stood barefoot at the edge of her yard, the rough crabgrass scraping against her ankles. She squinted at the flowers, their petals thin as whispers and haloed in a cold, impossible light. It was the sort of light that made her think of her father’s old Polaroids—the way they caught people mid-smile, eyes bright and unreal.

“It’s a trick,” Mrs. Huxley from across the street muttered, her voice barely a rasp over the chirring cicadas. She was in her nightgown, cigarette dangling, the smoke curving up like a question mark. “Some kind of prank.”

But it wasn’t a prank. By noon, the electric blooms were everywhere—poking through the cracks in the courthouse steps, swallowing the rusted bicycles chained outside the library. The town’s children, unbothered by the wary glances of their parents, plucked the flowers and pressed them to their foreheads, running down the street with wild, neon sigils burning above their eyes.

“They’ll go out soon,” her mother said over the supper table, eyes darting to the window where the pale glow seeped through the curtains. But they didn’t. That night, the town watched in reverence as a wind, sharp and metallic, picked up, sending petals swirling into the air. They drifted higher and higher until they seemed to fuse with the sky, forming constellations that blinked in sequences Mara swore were messages—patterns she’d seen before but couldn’t name.

The days that followed blurred into each other like watercolor in the rain. People whispered that the blooms were changing things, bending time and erasing memories with each fragile fall. Mr. Abernathy, who owned the hardware store, claimed he hadn’t seen his dog in weeks, though Mara distinctly remembered him walking it the day before.

The electric blooms stayed, their light deepening to a bruised indigo by the time the harvest moon rose fat and low over Greendale. That night, Mara lay in her bed, heart thudding like a moth in a jar, as blue light flooded the room. It poured over her, prying at old thoughts like roots dislodging stones. She remembered holding her father’s hand, the gravelly rasp of his voice when he taught her the names of stars, the ache that followed when those stars had vanished into the city’s glow after they’d moved.

But there, in the thrall of the electric moon, memories and time were tricksters—silken things that slipped between the fingers like petals on the wind. When Mara stepped outside into the blue-tinged silence, she understood: Greendale would never be the same. And neither would she.
