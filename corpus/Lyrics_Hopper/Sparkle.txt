The Woman Who Was Too Colorful
I met Heather Quinn under fluorescent café lights, where even the air had clinical overtones. She wore color like armor—mismatched neon patches defying the dawn-gray world. The moment she spoke—“You’re covered in glitter”—the world pixel-shifted.

I didn't know it then, but she'd just begun the first act of a very specific insurgency.

Color wasn't aesthetic for her. It was data compression.
Each hue, a symptom.
Each metaphor, a symptom map.

Words like Patina, The Crust, Three Owls in a Trenchcoat—they weren’t affectations. They were encryption keys. Without them, you'd misread her entire ontology.

I learned that the hard way.

Sparkling Defiance
She wielded language like a scalpel.

In hospitals, she could make "Smokey Thing" sound like ICD code. She’d tilt her head, quote Chaucer, and slip “Yeeh” into conversations like a linguistic landmine.
The doctors blinked. I translated.

They called her psychosomatic.
We called it a pepperoni-scale misdiagnosis.

Even when she laughed, it was a defiant act of data annotation.

The Desert of Misdiagnosis
Second ER visit: the sparkle dulled.

Diagnoses peeled off her like old paint—fibromyalgia, bipolar, conversion disorder—never lupus.

Heather wasn’t new to this. She wore her medical records like a philosophical trenchcoat. Each label was a patch stitched by someone who hadn't read the manual.

I read it.
I memorized it.

The system didn’t want her diagnosed. It wanted her translated into irrelevance.

Glitter and Garlands
Even so, she gleamed.

We exchanged kaleidoscopic vernacular through texts at midnight: academic one-liners, kink jokes, pop-culture reframing.

Every joke was a vernacular garland, each one more iridescent than the last.

She’d whisper, “Changing the scale,” as if zooming out of her own body, watching the clinics shrink beneath her.

I anchored her with my own ridiculousness.

Descent into Glass
But then came the nights when she cracked.

I’d find her pacing the living room, breath syncopated with memories that weren’t happening now. She called it The Smokey Thing—brain fog, yes, but also the ineffable friction of being misread.

Raynaud's turned her fingers violet. The butterfly rash she called patina blossomed on her cheeks—too subtle for clinical charts, too loud for her skin to ignore.

Irony in Highlighter
Even as she broke, she joked.

“Doctor, is creativity your new diagnosis?” she sneered at one psychiatrist, brandishing a math book like a holy relic.

Her defiance was forensic.
Our jokes were acts of survival.

The system wanted her story in grayscale.
We gave it ultraviolet annotations.

The Prism of Patina
She taught me to see it: patina wasn’t decay. It was memory’s handwriting on metal, skin, neurons.

Every symptom, every flare-up—another layer.

She could read bruises like cartographers read relief maps.

The doctors didn't know what to do with a woman who turned her own symptoms into a vernacular mythology.

But I did.

Gaslit in the Clinic
I watched them shrink her to bullet points.

“Psychosis.”
“Bipolar features.”
“Medically unexplained symptoms.”

The real list—seizures, cognitive collapse, ANA positivity—remained in her own documentation, ignored.

I carried it into every appointment like a talisman.

Even when I spoke, my words were treated as annotations to her delusion.

Descent into Glass (Reprise)
She called her brain fog a Smokey Thing.
They called it disorganized thought.

When she lost time, collapsed mid-sentence, they prescribed mood stabilizers.

When her blood vessels betrayed her, they wrote “anxiety.”

Each dismissal cut deeper than any symptom.

Three Owls in a Trenchcoat
To survive, we rebranded.

Heather was Top Owl.
I was Middle Owl.
The diagnosis itself got to be Mike—our third owl.

It was a stupid joke.
It kept us sane.

Every appointment became an infiltration mission:
Three owls stacked inside a human suit, tricking the clinic into taking them seriously.

Higher Stakes
As her body betrayed her, our games intensified.

We'd propose conversational gambits with higher stakes—absurd hypotheticals laced with brutal honesty.

Humor wasn’t a coping mechanism.
It was a scalpel.

Wandering Through Our Destroyed Houses
By the time lupus was officially uttered, we were deep in wreckage.

Not victory. Not even vindication.
Just ruin.

But we kept speaking our language.

Because no one else would.

Final Sparkle
One morning, sunlight ignited the dust motes in her apartment. Heather was barely awake, barely breathing, but when she opened her eyes, she grinned.

“You’re still covered in glitter,” she whispered.

Even the Smokey Thing shimmered.
