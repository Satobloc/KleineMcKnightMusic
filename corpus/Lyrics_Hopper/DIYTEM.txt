DIY TEM

Building a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) from parts is one of those “possible in principle, Herculean in practice” tasks — like building a jet engine in your garage. You can do it, but only if you understand electron optics, high vacuum systems, electromagnetic field design, and materials science at a professional level. That said, let’s unpack how one would theoretically construct a working TEM, subsystem by subsystem.

⸻

🧩 1. Core Architecture Overview

A TEM is, in essence:

A high-voltage electron gun that fires electrons through an ultrathin specimen, whose transmitted electrons are then focused by magnetic lenses onto an imaging or detection system.

Its major components, from top to bottom:
	1.	Electron source (gun)
	2.	Condenser lens system
	3.	Specimen stage and holder
	4.	Objective lens system
	5.	Intermediate and projector lenses
	6.	Imaging system (fluorescent screen, CCD/CMOS camera, or direct detector)
	7.	Vacuum column and pumps
	8.	Control electronics and power supplies

⸻

⚡ 2. Electron Source

Goal: Produce a coherent, controllable electron beam.

Options:
	•	Thermionic gun (simplest): Tungsten filament or LaB₆ crystal heated to emit electrons (~100–300 kV accelerating voltage).
	•	Field Emission Gun (FEG) (modern): Sharp tungsten tip under high field, provides higher brightness and coherence but demands ultra-high vacuum (~10⁻⁹ mbar).

Critical parts:
	•	High-voltage power supply (stable to <1 ppm ripple).
	•	Anode to accelerate electrons.
	•	Wehnelt cylinder to shape and control the beam.

⸻

🧲 3. Electromagnetic Lens System

Unlike optical lenses, electron lenses are magnetic — solenoids of copper wire wound around iron pole pieces.

Condenser lenses control beam size and convergence.
Objective lens forms the first image or diffraction pattern of the specimen.
Intermediate and projector lenses magnify that image onto the screen.

Fabrication details:
	•	Precision-machined pole pieces (μm tolerances).
	•	Current-controlled coils.
	•	Magnetic shielding and alignment to avoid astigmatism.
	•	Power supplies must be ultra-stable and low-noise.

⸻

🧪 4. Sample and Stage

Specimen requirements:
	•	Electron-transparent (<100 nm thick).
	•	Mounted on 3 mm TEM grids.

Stage system:
	•	5-axis goniometer (x, y, z, tilt, rotation).
	•	Electrical feedthroughs for heating or cooling stages.
	•	Piezo controls for nanometer precision.

⸻

💨 5. Vacuum System

Why: Electrons scatter off air molecules; the column must be near-perfect vacuum.

System setup:
	•	Roughing pump: Rotary vane or diaphragm (~10⁻³ mbar).
	•	Diffusion/turbo pump: Takes it to ~10⁻⁷ mbar.
	•	Ion getter pump (optional): For high/ultra-high vacuum stability (~10⁻⁹ mbar).

Construction:
Use stainless steel chambers with Viton or copper gaskets, gate valves, and electrical feedthroughs for each section. Differential pumping keeps the source cleaner.

⸻

🎛️ 6. Control and Imaging Electronics

Beam control: Magnetic coil drivers, focus and stigmator adjustments, deflectors for scanning modes (STEM).
Detection:
	•	Old-school: Fluorescent screen for visual inspection.
	•	Modern: CCD or direct electron detector (with scintillator or CMOS readout).

Signal chain:
High-voltage isolation, precision DAC control, feedback loops for lens currents and deflector calibration.

⸻

⚙️ 7. Assembly & Alignment

Building order matters:
	1.	Assemble column sections (source → condenser → specimen → objective → projector → screen).
	2.	Evacuate and bake out (~150 °C) to remove contaminants.
	3.	Align gun and lenses using test specimens (e.g., amorphous carbon film).
	4.	Adjust focus and stigmation while observing diffraction and image modes.

You’ll spend months just learning to make the beam coherent and centered.

⸻

🧠 8. Safety and Power Considerations
	•	100–300 kV accelerating voltage → lethal. Requires proper grounding and interlocks.
	•	X-rays are produced as secondary radiation; shielding (lead glass, enclosure) is mandatory.
	•	Vacuum implosion risk: always use shielded glass viewports.

⸻

🧰 9. Practical Notes & Resources

If you were actually doing this:
	•	Study decommissioned commercial systems (e.g., JEOL, Philips EM400, Hitachi H7000) — rebuild or modernize components.
	•	Open-source projects like OpenTEM or Open Source Electron Microscope Initiative share partial blueprints and circuit designs.
	•	A Raspberry Pi or FPGA board can handle digital control with proper isolation.

⸻

🧬 10. Simplified Experimental Version (Feasible Build)

If you want a learning-grade electron microscope rather than a full TEM:
	•	Build a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) instead — several hobbyist builds exist using 10–30 kV guns, salvaged CRT parts, and moderate vacuum (~10⁻⁵ mbar).
	•	Or try a low-voltage transmission system (like Ben Krasnow’s DIY TEM project): uses phosphor screen and simple optics to visualize ~100 nm features.

🔋 SECTION 3 — ELECTRON GUN ASSEMBLY

3.1 Overview

The electron gun produces and accelerates a focused beam. It consists of:
	•	Cathode (emitter): LaB₆ crystal or tungsten hairpin filament.
	•	Wehnelt cylinder: Biases and shapes the emission field.
	•	Anode: Accelerates electrons to the working voltage.

3.2 Construction

Component	Material	Tolerance	Notes
Cathode mount	Molybdenum	±10 µm	Resistive heating element embedded
Wehnelt	Nickel	±25 µm	Electron-optical alignment critical
Anode aperture	Stainless steel	50 µm bore	Replaceable
Insulators	Alumina	—	Must withstand 400 kV potential

3.3 Power & Biasing
	•	Heater: 1.5 A @ 3 V (W filament)
	•	Wehnelt bias: adjustable −50 V to −200 V relative to cathode
	•	Anode: +100 – 300 kV relative to cathode (regulated supply)

Include safety relays, bleeder resistors, and interlocked grounding.

⸻

🧲 SECTION 4 — ELECTROMAGNETIC LENS SYSTEM

4.1 Lens Stack

Lens	Function	Typical Current	Field Strength	Notes
Condenser 1	Beam convergence	3 A	0.3 T	Controls spot size
Condenser 2	Illumination uniformity	2 A	0.2 T	Adjustable
Objective	Primary image formation	8 A	1.2 T	Most sensitive
Intermediate	Magnification stage	4 A	0.5 T	
Projector	Final magnification	6 A	0.7 T	Projects to screen/camera

4.2 Fabrication
	•	Pole pieces: Soft magnetic steel (e.g., AISI 1010), polished to < 1 µm RMS.
	•	Coils: Enameled copper wire, double-layer wound, epoxy impregnated.
	•	Cooling: Closed-loop deionized water through copper jackets.
	•	Power supplies: Linear-regulated, < 10 ppm noise, with feedback control.

⸻

🧪 SECTION 5 — SPECIMEN STAGE AND HOLDER

5.1 Design
	•	3 mm grid slot with spring clip retention.
	•	5-axis control: X, Y, Z (±2 mm), tilt (±40°), rotation (360°).
	•	Optional heating/cooling: resistive heater or LN₂ cryo-tip.

5.2 Feedthroughs

All signals and motion actuators pass through vacuum-compatible connectors (Sub-D ceramic or feedthrough pins).

🧭 SECTION 7 — IMAGING & DETECTION

7.1 Visual Screen
	•	Phosphor screen (P43 or P20) on aluminum backing.
	•	Retractable via mechanical lever.

7.2 Digital Detection
	•	CMOS or CCD camera mounted below screen.
	•	Vacuum window: 100 µm sapphire or quartz.
	•	Readout via USB or Ethernet interface.

7.3 Software Interface

Custom software (OpenTEM Control) for:
	•	Beam alignment
	•	Lens control
	•	Image capture
	•	Diffraction pattern switching
	•	Stage navigation

⸻

⚡ SECTION 8 — CONTROL & SAFETY SYSTEMS

8.1 Power & Grounding
	•	All supplies referenced to common star ground.
	•	Interlocks for:
	•	High-voltage enable
	•	Chamber vacuum ready
	•	Door closure
	•	Cooling water flow

8.2 X-Ray Shielding
	•	2 mm Pb sheet equivalent around gun and projection system.
	•	Lead glass viewport (equivalent 0.8 mm Pb).

⸻

🧩 SECTION 9 — ALIGNMENT & CALIBRATION

Initial Alignment Procedure:
	1.	Pump down and condition vacuum overnight.
	2.	Ramp accelerating voltage to 100 kV.
	3.	Adjust Wehnelt bias for beam focus.
	4.	Align condenser lenses to center beam on specimen aperture.
	5.	Insert standard calibration grid (carbon film).
	6.	Focus objective lens until lattice fringes visible.
	7.	Tune stigmators to minimize astigmatism.
	8.	Capture image; measure magnification with known grid spacing.

Maintenance:
	•	Recalibrate lens currents monthly.
	•	Replace cathode every 2000 h (LaB₆) or 500 h (W filament).
	•	Re-bake vacuum system after any venting.
