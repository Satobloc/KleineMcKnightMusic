Hellfire: Book One



Scene 1

Foochow stank of fish rot, coal smoke, and old piss. The alleys steamed. Dogs nosed at gristle-piles while laundry hissed overhead like lines of prayer flags. Li Wu stepped off the river ferry into that reek with a slight stoop in his shoulders—not from age or weakness, but habit. A man who listened first, moved second. His left hand toyed with the thumb-worn edge of his amber prayer beads, tucked half-concealed in his sleeve.

The foreigners had arrived two days prior, dragging crates stamped with stars and Latin abbreviations, tall under their fedoras, their language hard-edged and quick. Americans, someone had muttered at the dock, as if naming a species. A local fixer—one of the fat ones with government friends—had mentioned they needed a cook. Or a translator. Or both. It was never clear.

Li Wu wasn’t sure why he’d come. He told himself it was for the pay.

The tea shop they used as an interview room reeked of fried oil and scorched mint. Its windows were crusted with fly droppings, and someone had nailed half a Buddhist charm to the lintel. Superstition for prosperity, badly placed. A Western woman sat in the far corner, photographing a chipped bowl as if it were a Ming relic. Beside her, a man with angular shoulders and a field notebook tapped his pen on the edge of the table, rhythmically, like he was counting down.

Roy Chapman Andrews, Li Wu had learned. Spoke with the confidence of someone used to commanding rooms, but his Mandarin came out like wind-blown gravel. He asked questions as if they were levers.

Li Wu stood when called, and bowed faintly—neither servile nor stiff.

"Y-you r-r-require a man f-familiar w-w-with… city a-and i-inland d-dialects?" he said, each stumble carefully spaced, like stones set to slow pursuit.

Roy blinked. "Jesus. They weren't kidding. That stutter—well. Let’s see what you know. Where’s your English from?"

"Mission… school. K-Kunyang." He lifted his eyes slightly. "And… b-by w-w-work."

“You worked for Westerners?”

Li Wu gave a small nod. “S-some geologists. Missiona—missionaries. P-professors.”

Andrews turned to the woman—Yvette, the camera-woman, though no one had introduced her formally. She said nothing. Just tilted her head, observing. Li Wu noticed how her hand shifted to take notes—except she wasn’t writing. Only the muscle of it moved, lightly.

Roy grunted. “You know how to cook?”

“W-well enough to k-k-keep a w-white man’s st-stomach f-from c-c-collapsing, yes.”

That made the American bark a laugh. “Hell of a job description.”

They made him read a letter next, one written in formal French. A reference for some past employee, maybe. Li Wu read it aloud in Mandarin, then again in passable English. He omitted the insulting metaphor tucked between the lines—“obedient as a trained macaque”—because he’d already decided it wasn’t worth anyone’s time. Maybe that had cost him the job. If so, then the job wasn't worth his time either. The woman noticed anyway. Her brow twitched, just barely. She knew.

Roy closed his notebook. “You’ll ride upriver with us, Li Wu. We leave at dawn. You’ll be cook, translator, and when required, the man who keeps the rest of us from walking straight into a clan feud.” He gestured vaguely. “Wages every three weeks. Room, rations, some extra if we make it west.”

A lesser man might’ve bowed low. Li Wu inclined his head, hands folded. "Th-thank you. I—I underst-stand."

Later, alone in the back courtyard of the guesthouse they’d assigned him, he sat under a skeletal loquat tree, watching smoke rise from the kitchens. A boy chopped fish heads. A pig screamed somewhere in a slaughter alley, cut off mid-wail. In the dusk light, Foochow shimmered. Not with beauty exactly—but with something stranger. Multiplicity. Rot and wonder. Piss and prayer.

Li Wu rubbed the edge of his pen—steel-nibbed, lacquer-repaired—and stared at the ink pooling in his thoughts. He’d been hired. Not seen, not yet. But he would be.






Scene 2

The steamer wheezed against the current like an asthmatic mule, dragging itself upriver through brown water thick with ash from the hills. The Min twisted through velvet-green slopes, sheer and shivering in the heat, but Li Wu kept his eyes on the deck—on boots and baskets, on crates tied with wire, on where the ropes bled rust into the planks. He crouched low, cleaning a brass rice pot with coarse salt and duck feathers. The motion soothed his tongue.

A crewman hacked and spat. Somewhere below, a child cried—then fell silent, too suddenly.

Three foreign crates had shifted during the night—one of them labeled “Dry Plates. Glass. Fragile.” He’d retied them himself before sunrise, using fisherman’s knots his father taught him as a boy, when they still lived near salt flats and the gods hadn’t yet gone silent.

From the upper deck, laughter rang out—bright, distant. The American woman. Li Wu didn’t turn to look, but he heard her shutter click: twice, then a pause. Then three more.

Roy Chapman Andrews paced behind her like a mastiff on a lead. Always moving, always explaining something to someone. He wore that wide-brimmed hat like it granted him rank among the heavens.

Li Wu stood, wiped his hands on his trousers. Noticed a smudge of grease on his right knee. He bent to scrub it away. Behind him, the boy they’d brought on as a mule-boy squatted by the railing, eating sour rice out of a leaf cone. When their eyes met, the boy looked away.

The launch slowed for a bend. Then the scream came.

A splash, loud and sharp.

Then—nothing. No scrambling. No panic. Just the slap of the river closing back in.

Li Wu turned. The boy had stood now. A man on the crew shouted something flat and dismissive in Min dialect. Someone had fallen in—one of the coolies, maybe. A low voice muttered “Gone already” as if the words were smoke.

No one moved to help. No one threw rope.

The river churned lazily. A scrap of fabric—red or orange—spun on the surface like a severed prayer flag.

Li Wu stared. The body was gone. Pulled under by the silty weight. Gone like the pig’s squeal, like the gods. He felt nothing at first. Then something small and sharp.

Not grief. Not yet.

He reached for the rail with one hand, steadied himself. The child below had resumed crying.

A minute passed. Then two. The Americans said nothing. Yvette continued filming the riverbank. Roy lit a cigarette. From the front of the boat came the rattle of the engine straining again, as if it had never stopped.

Li Wu swallowed. The breath caught in his throat, folded like bad paper. He reached for his beads—counted backward, ten to one. When the breath unlocked, he let it pass through clenched teeth.

"F-fell in," he said quietly to no one. "N-no rope."

No one answered. There was no answer.

They passed a bamboo stand where a white heron stood ankle-deep in the reeds. The light caught its feathers just so—and for a moment, it looked silver. Almost unreal. Then it took flight, slow and wide-winged, carving across the water like an ideograph no scholar would admit to knowing.

Li Wu watched until it disappeared behind a bend. The pot at his feet still smelled faintly of duck grease. The crates hadn’t shifted.

He sat back down, resumed cleaning, as the river resumed not caring.


Scene 3

The streets of Yen-ping steamed with mule piss and crushed camphor. Vendors hawked sugar-bread and boiled duck tongues under torn parasols. Li Wu walked behind the cart, left hand hidden in his sleeve, thumb working at the lacquer seam of his pen. He could feel the city tightening. The kind of heat that brewed trouble. There had been rumors. Not shouted, just exchanged with the care of contraband: The Yung men had fired on a tax patrol. Blood at the south gate. Too many rifles in the hills.

Yvette had gone ahead with her camera and the stout missionary woman—Miss Hartford—who blinked too often and mistook dialects for decorum. Roy trailed them, shirt open at the throat, adjusting his revolver like a man arranging medals. His mood had turned lately—flintier. He hated delays, and Yen-ping was full of them: blocked roads, missing guides, bribes that needed the “right tea leaves.”

Li Wu stopped when he reached the gatehouse. The yamen wall was splashed with paper—bounties, obituaries, slogans in black ink scrawled over others in red. The layers gave it a texture like a wound scabbing over. One fluttered loose and caught against his leg: a tax edict, dated three weeks ago, now obsolete.

Inside, the foreign woman was speaking too quickly, her German-accented English rasping through the dust. “No, I said not today. No, the bridge is—never mind.” She turned, found Li Wu. “Could you explain? They’re saying we need a different pass.”

He stepped forward, bowed slightly toward the nearest guard. He recognized the uniform, the sour wax smell of dried sweat under brocade. Bandit-fighter turned city man. More gun than teeth.

Li Wu listened first. The words were wrong—not rude, but oily. The man wanted something. Respect, or face, or coin. Probably all three.

“Th-they s-s-say the roads a-aren’t s-s-safe today,” Li Wu translated, quietly. “He… s-says it would be… w-wiser for the f-foreigners to return b-by gatefall.”

Roy’s face twitched.

“Tell them we’re here under permit from Foochow. And from the Republic. I’ll be damned if we turn around because some farm boy got twitchy with a gun.”

Li Wu didn’t translate that. He offered a different set of syllables—polished ones. Described the expedition as scholars, harmless, curious about butterflies and river fish. He used the word yisheng, doctor, which flattered the guards’ sense of order.

The head man gave a snort. Not yes, not no. But they were allowed through.

Outside, the carts had drawn up short beside the temple square. A small crowd had gathered near the incense stalls. The smoke curled in chaotic threads. Children with cracked heels and dyed rags darted between the vendors.

Li Wu saw it before the others did.

A man, bound at the wrists, kneeling beside a chopping block. An official execution—quiet, legal. No ceremony. A petty brigand, someone whispered. Robbed the wrong storehouse.

Yvette had stopped, not photographing. Her mouth was a flat line. Roy stepped forward, but not close. Just enough to look. He tilted his head, studying it like a bad specimen. Miss Hartford had gone pale and was digging in her bag, as if searching for something that could unsee what was in front of her.

The sword fell fast. One stroke. Clean. The man’s body slumped forward, hands still tied.

The crowd murmured once, then stilled.

Li Wu didn’t blink. He had seen worse in the famine years—men flayed for stealing grain. But this, in the middle of the expedition, with foreign boots not five steps away—it sat wrong. Like finding mold inside rice you thought clean.

The blood seeped slowly, thick and dark on stone. A child tried to touch it. His mother slapped his hand away.

They passed by without speaking.

At the next corner, a camellia bush had bloomed out of season. Pale blossoms cupped in glossy leaves. Li Wu paused, touched one lightly with his fingers. His stutter vanished in that moment, silently, like breath in fog.

Then they walked on. The city behind them, already forgetting.


Scene 4


The rain had lifted but left its weight in the air. Steam curled up from the road, gathering under the eaves of the low-walled post station like smoke with nowhere to go. The mule’s hooves thudded against packed earth as they entered the compound—three walls of rammed earth, a tin roof bowed under moss, a clay urn catching drips from a leaking gutter.

Inside, it smelled of old ink, millet porridge, and the unmistakable sharpness of bird lime.

Li Wu moved past the front bench and knelt near the delivery crates. A half-sodden ledger sat open under a stone weight. He brushed back the flap and scanned the labels: one tin of medical alcohol. A crate of preserved specimens. Two parcels marked “delicate”—probably the glass plates. And a small oilcloth bundle with an English return label, already smudged beyond reading.

He lifted each one carefully, arranging them in order by weight. The rice-packing inside the smallest crate had gone moldy.

To his left, Roy was speaking briskly with the post clerk—an old man in thin robes, eyes like river stones. Behind them, another boy opened the pigeon loft. The birds were nearly silent. Just the occasional shuffle of feathers, a single low coo. No sudden movement.

Another boy opened the pigeon loft. The birds were nearly silent—save for one that stepped forward on its perch as the door swung wide, tilting its head toward Roy like it had been waiting. No command. No handler’s touch. Just movement, precise and timed.

Roy signed his name with a sharp flourish and handed over a small roll of thin paper.

“Message to Teng-yueh,” he said, loud enough for Li Wu to hear. “We’ll arrive in five days. Sooner if the terrain holds.”

The boy clipped the message to a leg-band without ceremony. He didn’t clap, didn’t speak. Just held the bird in both hands for a moment—gentle, but with purpose—and let it go.

The pigeon wheeled once above the yard, caught the wind clean, and vanished north.

Li Wu finished checking the straps on the chemical crate, paused at the last label. It had warped—writing lost to moisture. He picked up a stick of charcoal and rewrote the English phonetics carefully, each letter a shape he knew better by sound than meaning.

Yvette appeared beside him, unwrapping a letter with gloved fingers. Her face was unreadable, but her breath had gone shallow. She folded the letter twice, slipped it into her coat without a word, and helped him lift the crate.

A second pigeon slipped from the loft unbidden—no signal, no message. It landed on the edge of the desk, tapped twice at the lid of a message tube already lying there. The clerk nodded attached the canister to the pigeon's leg, and it flew off. 



Scene 5

The stove was rusted thin near the base, so Li Wu used a stone to wedge it upright. The kitchen shack leaned, roof patchy with broken tiles, but at least it had a chimney—unlike the last place, where smoke gathered thick as ghosts in the rafters.

He knelt on the hard-packed earth, rinsing river greens in a cracked basin. The water was already turning grey with ash from the firewood. A piece of old cabbage floated by. He nudged it away.

Outside, the expedition rustled into restlessness. Someone was shouting about misplaced traps. Heller, probably. Always precise, always irritated when anything shifted without permission. Roy had gone upriver to consult a boatman who might’ve seen the tiger—the tiger, the one with the striped ghost-pelt that haunted dreams and journal entries. And Yvette had vanished before dawn with her tripod and a map sketch she didn’t show anyone else.

Li Wu stirred the rice pot. It was almost ready.

The other cook—the one they’d called Da-Ming, the "Woolworth Building"—had disappeared two days ago. Illness, he’d claimed. An opium toothache. But Li Wu had noticed the sudden absence of the sour-bitter smoke, and the way the man had started talking to himself in the dark, counting coins that weren’t there.

Now it was just Li Wu, and the pots, and the pressure to feed seventeen mouths with nothing but salted duck and stubborn rice. And one dented tin of “Libby’s Roast Beef” Roy had insisted be saved “for emergencies.”

Footsteps approached—boots. Heavy. Roy.

“Smells decent,” the American said, pausing at the threshold. “That cabbage or—?”

“C-chou,” Li Wu said, not looking up. “Wild. N-not p-poisonous.”

Roy grunted. He ducked under the lintel and leaned against the wall, arms crossed.

“They said there was another village upriver. Abandoned. Tiger country. Or bandits pretending to be tigers.” He shrugged. “Same result.”

Li Wu tasted the broth, added salt. Dried shrimp for body. A few red haw thorns for color. He stirred without comment.

“You ever hear stories of a blue tiger?” Roy asked suddenly. “Not from missionaries. From locals.”

Li Wu didn’t answer right away. He adjusted the stove ring. The flame hiccupped, then settled.

“In s-some villages, they s-say it’s not a tiger,” he said slowly, “but a… w-wandering god. Or a curse t-t-tied to famine. It shows itself b-before… bad seasons.”

“Huh,” Roy said. “That sounds like superstition.”

Li Wu didn’t correct him. There was no point. He kept stirring.

“I need someone I can count on,” Roy said, after a pause. “It’s not just food. The boys listen to you. You see things. Keep people in line without making noise about it. That’s rare.”

Li Wu set the ladle down. His left hand found his beads again. He didn’t meet Roy’s eyes, but the words settled heavily between them. Not quite trust. But something approaching it, wrapped in utility.

“I w-won’t run,” he said, finally.

Roy nodded. Then left.

Later, as dusk fell and the fire snapped low, Li Wu carried the rice pot outside to cool. The smoke trailed behind him in threads. Midges danced in the last shafts of light. The hills rose black and velvet above the trees, and for a moment—just one—the call of a bird pierced the hush, clear and spiraling.

It sounded like sorrow. Or laughter. Or something older than either.

Li Wu watched the sky change color. He tasted ash on the wind. And though he did not believe in wandering gods, he understood why someone might.

In the morning, Li Wu awoke to the commotion of the Andrewses setting up a scene. 

The air in camp now was thick with the residue of ammonium salts, sulfur compounds, and burned gelatin—residue from the darkroom and flash charges. The goat grease had faded, replaced by something acid and artificial. Yvette adjusted the legs of her tripod with deliberate care, the soles of her boots silent against the dirt floor. A red flag dangled from the flash stand, motionless except for an occasional twitch of wind. Roy stalked between crates, pointing out alignments with a surveying rod, calling out distances to one of the coolies with theatrical ease. He squinted into the sun, lowered a hand, and waved for a lantern to be repositioned.

Li Wu poured tea behind the stove screen, half-watching. His thumb slipped rhythmically along the beads in his sleeve—amber to tin, amber to tin. The rhythm wasn’t prayer. It was thought. He noted how Yvette leveled her lens—two degrees higher than expected—compensating for the sun’s drift across the canvas backdrop. Roy shifted again, kneeling briefly, framing the shot with his fingers like a puppet-master conjuring ghosts.

Then—a sudden flick of movement. A bird, large and glossy, landed neatly on the thin cross-bar of the flash stand. Not a hawk. Not a magpie.

It turned once, flicked its tail, then steadied. Li Wu blinked once, slow and careful, taking in how it moved... like it had been carved from smoke and lacquer—long-legged, slate-backed, tail unfurled behind it in a fan of silvered filaments and rusted lace. The feathers shimmered not with brilliance, but with subtlety: a dozen shades of charcoal and ash, each edge veined with light like calligraphy too old to translate. Its throat pulsed with soundless tension, chest low, body forward, toes spread wide on the dirt like it had never learned fear. At rest, its tail curved slightly inward, like the edge of a question. In motion, it flared—a cathedral of threads, trembling. Not beautiful, exactly. Not strange. But exact. The kind of exactness found in echoes. Or lies.

He had seen such birds only twice before—once near a governor’s villa in Kiangsi, once again beside a salt mine west of Yung-chang. Never near human sound. 

Perhaps it was the red flag. Such things called to birds.

Yvette looked up from the lens, studied the creature a beat longer than most would. Her lips parted.

“Just arrived,” she said dryly, “what’s the news?”

The bird turned its head—paused—and then repeated her words, pitch for pitch, rhythm for rhythm. “Just arrived, what’s the news?”

Roy raised a finger as if to say something, then let it fall. He returned to his clipboard.

Li Wu did not smile. He did not speak. He placed the tea tray down with care, then turned to fetch the glass plates from the dark cloth caddy. His stutter did not stir; the words did not need to rise. But his gaze lingered.

He had heard mimicry before. Street hawkers, clever parrots. But this… this was something quieter. The kind of event that slips between words.

He watched as the bird preened, then lifted itself away with a fan of feathers and a click like a camera shutter. The air did not change, but the moment held its shape. Then, as if on cue, Yvette pressed the shutter release. Flash.

Li Wu retrieved the tea. He would not speak of the bird again. But it was added to the ledger in his head. Not the museum's. Not Roy’s. His.


Scene 6

The bells came first—distant, metallic, frantic. Then the smoke. Thin at first, a thread across the ridgeline beyond Yen-ping’s west gate. By the time it curled above the rooftops like a question unasked, Li Wu already knew: something had broken.

He dropped the fish knife.

The shouting began near the grain market. Fire warnings, urgent but vague, tangled with the crack of gunshots. Not firecrackers. Real. That flat, ragged sound that struck the belly before the ear.

He pushed past the doorway curtain, nearly colliding with Heller, who had just returned with a box of bird skulls and a nosebleed from the humidity. “What’s happening?” the American asked, already knowing the answer.

“R-riot,” Li Wu said, grabbing his coat. “No—n-no, n-not riot. Rebellion.”

That word landed like an old curse. Heller blinked once.

Yvette was already moving—camera strapped to her back, hands firm. She didn’t ask questions. Just looked once at Li Wu, as if his face held a compass. Then she vanished up the alley with that silent grace of hers, skirts hitched, boots sucking at the muck.

Roy barked orders behind the temple wall. Someone had seen soldiers. Nationalist militia. Others said bandits dressed in old uniforms. It didn’t matter. Civilians were bleeding either way.

Li Wu ran. He didn’t remember deciding to. He just did. The smoke was closer now, oily and black. Fire had reached the wooden roofs of the orphan house near the north quarter. Screams rose, disjointed, like birds fleeing a storm.

At the mission clinic, Dr. Trimble was already elbow-deep in blood. A boy’s thigh had split open from a bullet. Another child, barely ten, lay beside the doorway—alive but still, eyes wide like a fish gasping in a dry net.

Li Wu knelt.

“C-c-can he h-h-help you?” he asked in fast Min dialect.

The child blinked once.

“W-what’s your name?”

“Ming.” Barely a whisper.

The mother clutched the boy’s arm, her face paper-white and soaked in tears. She wasn’t speaking. Just mouthing something. A prayer or a curse.

Li Wu turned. Dr. Trimble looked up, face streaked with sweat.

“He needs that artery clamped now,” the doctor snapped. “I need the gauze from the blue satchel.”

Li Wu ran. He found the satchel. He held a basin. He watched a man’s life pour into cotton like ink bleeding into rice paper. He said nothing. No time for silence to become meaning.

Outside, the gunfire shifted—moved uphill, toward the yamen. Li Wu followed the path of it in his mind. The alleyways, the blind corners. He’d drawn maps like these before—when he helped the Belgian. When he’d lied to a scholar about who really controlled the tea tolls north of Futsing.

He stood in the courtyard, blood drying on his sleeves. Smoke turned the sky a bruised orange. In the distance, someone blew a conch shell—an old signal. From the hills.

Then stillness.

Li Wu walked past the mission gate. Ash drifted from the burning storehouse like snow. A half-dead ox stood in the road, tethered to nothing, foam crusted at its mouth. Its eyes were dull, almost blind. It turned toward him with that slow, pitiful grace only animals carry at the edge of ruin.

The path led down from the temple square, past a grove of willow-fig and then opened onto terraced farmland sloping gently toward the river. The air was damp, warmed by recent sun, thick with the persistent calls of frogs and invisible crickets. Somewhere farther off, a cartwheel creaked. Rice shoots waved in orderly rows, neat as a scholar’s script.

Two farmers moved along a stone-lined channel, adjusting sluices with worn cedar staves. One carried a basket of tools over his shoulder, the other an iron kettle swaying from a pole. Neither looked toward the road. They worked in rhythm, boots half-sunk in wet earth.

A field on the left was under fresh tilling—dark rows of soil fanned in deliberate arcs. At first glance, there was nothing unusual: the dull gleam of iron, the sway of a plough blade, the heavy breath of animals laboring through the wet soil.

There was no man with them.

The team of oxen—two, yoked shoulder to shoulder—moved steadily along the furrow. Their flanks were slicked with sweat. The larger, red-brown and broad-hipped, moved a half-step ahead and would occasionally glance left to correct their angle. The smaller one adjusted its gait accordingly. Their plough dipped, turned, bit into the soil again.

The team reached the end of the furrow. Both oxen halted, rested three beats. Then, without pause or signal, they pivoted with an unhurried precision and began the next row.

The harness was fitted clean, brass rings darkened with age. No ropes trailed. No handler walked behind. Only the steady sound of hooves, snorts, and the wet slice of iron.

Roy shaded his eyes. He had stopped on the road, just for a moment. Watching.

“Good stock,” he said to no one in particular. “That’s gonna be hard to find where we’re going.”

He adjusted his strap, picked up pace again toward the compound.

Yvette was farther ahead, camera packed away, attention caught by the shifting light on temple tile.

Li Wu lingered a moment longer. The oxen were mid-row again now, heads down, muscles working in tandem. Their horns were trimmed, not showy. From the look of the lines, they’d been at it since before first light.

He noted the field—flat, wide, stone-edged. A better-than-average yield. The barn at the hill’s foot bore fresh paint along the beam ends. The thatch was tight.

A well-kept farm, he thought. Not rich. But capable. Perhaps someone had sent a child to the city school. Perhaps they hired out their teams on holy days, when others drank.

The oxen turned again.

Li Wu blinked once, then followed Roy down the track toward the yard gate, his step measured and unhurried. Behind him, the plough bit into the earth once more, and the field breathed as it always had.

He stopped. Met the ox’s gaze. The animal swayed. Then moved on, hooves quiet against the cinders.

And there it was again—unexpected, unwanted—beauty. Even here.

Even now.




Scene 7

The rebellion had burned itself out by morning. Or been bought off. It was never clear which. The streets of Yen-ping held the aftermath in their teeth—charred doorframes, scattered shoes, the brittle hush of a city pretending not to flinch.

Li Wu stepped over the remains of a fruit cart, careful not to crush the half-melted abacus lying in the gutter. Flies moved like threads of threadless silk above a slick of blood that was already turning black.

He carried the tea kettle. Small, brass, dented from years of being knocked against mule saddles. The handle burned his palm where it had cracked, but he held it steady. He needed to bring tea to Yvette.

She’d slept sitting up, camera case across her knees like a shrine. In the chaos, she'd taken no fewer than twenty plates—he knew this because he’d helped rinse two of them in the pitch-dark backroom of the mission chapel, where the chemicals stank of pickled plums and rot.

Yvette had not spoken much. Just written tiny notes in her ledger. Light too hot. Shutter slow. “Man with bamboo splint. Missing thumb.” “Cleft-palate child, turning.”

She took pictures of what the Americans called “context.” Li Wu called it pain. But he didn’t say that.

He found her in the alley behind the chapel, bare-handed, pulling drying cord taut between two soot-blackened support beams. Her hair was pinned back, streaked with grease. Her face had that distant expression again—the one that made her seem older than her years, though she always stood straight as a lantern pole.

He approached slowly, steam curling from the spout.

“G-ginger t-tea,” he said.

She looked over. Took the cup without a word. Sipped. Her fingers were shaking slightly. She didn’t hide it.

Li Wu stood beside her. The alley opened behind them toward the slope of burned tenements. One had collapsed inward, its roof fallen like the folding wing of a dead bird.

Above it all, the sky was unreasonably beautiful. Pale blue, thin clouds like brushstrokes. The kind of morning poets wrote about, if they ignored everything beneath their sandals.

“Did you sleep?” Yvette asked, softly.

Li Wu shook his head.

She didn’t press. Just pulled a photograph from her pocket, not yet fixed—an exposure on glass, the shadows ghostlike. A woman with her mouth open in grief. No sound, just the shape of it. The raw void.

“I… should not h-have taken this,” he said, looking away.

Yvette didn’t answer. But she held the plate gently, as if it might break from shame alone.

Behind them, from the front of the chapel, came a burst of voices. Andrews and the missionary—Caldwell, perhaps—arguing again over logistics. Guns, or sermons. Traps, or peace. Always the same debate, just with different names.

Yvette exhaled. “They’ll want to leave soon.”

Li Wu nodded. “The b-blue tiger again?”

“The tiger, or whatever lies they need to keep walking.”

She sounded tired. Not bitter. Not quite.

A breeze lifted ash from the corner wall. For a moment, it looked like snow, drifting upward.

They stood in silence, drinking the tea. It tasted of smoke and ginger. And something else—something faintly sweet, like lotus root boiled too long.

Li Wu thought: We are all learning to carry the unspeakable. Some with film. Some with silence.

A crow called overhead. Sharp, insistent. The sound of endings. Or warnings.

He finished his tea. Then bent to lift the tripod case.






Scene 8

The mules reeked. Even in the high mountain air, their sweat clung to the caravan like old resin—feral, acrid, constant. The mafus spat often and cursed louder, snapping switches at the beasts with the boredom of men too tired to be cruel, but too proud to appear gentle.

Li Wu walked alongside the second cart, where the dried meat was packed. One strap had loosened in the night, and the crate had begun to tilt. He’d knotted it tighter with his lacquer-threaded twine, weaving it beneath the axle spokes while the American men debated route options ahead, completely unaware.

He didn’t mind. Let them be unaware.

The road was barely that—a thread of packed earth, broken by stones and mule droppings, winding between cliffs and stunted pine. Every few li, the path vanished into shade or rockfall. Once, they passed a village so thin and stilled it seemed carved from salt. No one emerged to wave. No dogs barked. Just the echo of their hooves fading behind.

Yvette walked sometimes, rode sometimes. She had a way of fitting herself around the motion of the mule, not quite riding like a man, but not not, either. She filmed less now. Drew more. Sketches of roofs, hands, outlines of trees bent against wind they hadn’t reached yet.

Roy, meanwhile, talked—always. To Heller, to Yvette, to the air. About terrain, about science, about progress. He carried his rifle even when they weren’t near game. It was always clean. Li Wu had noticed the habit: before every rest, Roy wiped the trigger guard with the edge of his handkerchief, as if afraid the journey might tarnish something essential.

That morning, they passed a ravine where mist hung in veils, trailing like prayer strips from the ridges. Beneath it, a pool had formed—still, black, perfect.

Li Wu paused to piss near a cedar stump and saw his own reflection: eyes sunken, skin dry, the stubble at his jaw dark against his hollow cheeks. He looked, for a moment, like the old charcoal-diggers he’d translated for years ago. Men who knew how to disappear.

A horn call ahead. The cart slowed. A broken axle. Not theirs.

They reached the blockage after a steep turn. A local caravan had collapsed—three crates spilled, one mule lame, its hoof cracked and bleeding. Two women, cloaked in patched linen, crouched nearby, holding a bundled infant.

The men in their group—Roy, Caldwell, Heller—stood at a remove, discussing in slow English whether the road was impassable. One of the mafus muttered something sharp: They could have cleared this last night.

Li Wu stepped forward.

One of the women noticed him and began to speak. Not Mandarin. Not even Min. Something older—he caught pieces. Lisu? No—some western dialect. He tried a Yunnanese trade-pidgin. She understood enough to show him the binding on the mule’s leg.

It had split clean open.

He knelt, unrolled a strip of tea-dyed cloth from his pack. He wasn’t a healer, but he knew the basics: pressure, bracing, dignity. That last one mattered more than anyone admitted.

The woman watched him with quiet eyes, not grateful. Not yet.

Roy came up beside him. “Wu, how long will this take?”

Li Wu didn’t look up. “N-not long. B-but the road… the road n-needs clear-c-c-c—” He clamped his jaw. “Clear path. We… w-we w-will m-m-move the… the heavy c-crate.”

Roy nodded, distracted. “Fine. Just don’t get blood on the plates.”

Li Wu smiled without smiling.

Together, with two mafus and one of the local men, they lifted the crate—a rough wooden box banded in copper. Inside, it clinked. Something metallic. Religious artifacts, maybe. No one asked.

As they shifted it off the path, Yvette took one photo. No staging. Just bodies bent, the muscles of it, the balance of strangers working together without a shared tongue. It wasn’t beauty, exactly—but something near it. Motion made visible.

Later, that evening, they camped by a cold stream. Li Wu cooked rice thick with ginger and taro. The stars burned high above—white specks so sharp they looked like they could cut.

He sat alone near the firepit, knees drawn to chest. The beads in his hand rolled smooth and warm from the heat.

From the edge of camp, the wounded mule groaned once, low and long. Then silence.

He whispered a blessing—not in words, but rhythm. Thumb to bead, breath to smoke. Then let it go.






Scene 9

The valley narrowed at Yuchi. Pines gave way to scrub willow, and the road became a game trail pretending otherwise. Li Wu knew the smell of ambush—sun-warmed stone, sweat-dried grass, the pause in birdsong. He walked slower, listened more.

The brigands didn’t arrive with shouts. No, these came with silence.

A bend in the path. Then five men, half-uniformed, rifles carried casually like farm tools. They didn’t block the road outright. Just stood there. Watching. One picked his teeth with a matchstick. Another tapped his boot heel with the stock of his gun.

Roy stepped forward like he’d rehearsed it. “We’re an American scientific expedition,” he said in crisp English. “Zoological. No interest in politics.” He gestured toward Heller, who nodded, holding a notebook full of dead mammals. The rifles didn’t lower.

Li Wu felt it in his spine—the space between misunderstanding and miscalculation.

He stepped forward. Bowed, shallow but deliberate. Not too low. Not servile.

"These men," he said in Mandarin, "are travelers. No threat. No officials. No cargo worth seizing. Just ink, paper, bones."

The leader—sharp-cheeked, too young to command—snorted. “Bones are worth plenty when they come from foreigners. And cameras are worse than guns.” His dialect was provincial, clipped, but clear.

Li Wu met his eyes. “What do you want?”

"Tax."

That word again. Always “tax.” Always at gunpoint.

Roy, behind him, shifted. “Wu. What’s he saying?”

Li Wu didn’t turn. “H-he wants p-payment.”

Roy exhaled sharply. “Tell him I’ll give him a receipt, if he can write.”

Li Wu said nothing. Not yet.

The gunmen were restless. One squatted, urinating without turning away. Another flicked a cigarette butt into the grass. Sparks hissed, caught briefly, then died.

Li Wu reached into his satchel. Slowly. No sudden movements. He drew out a pouch—not coin, but tobacco. Good leaf. Wrapped in oiled paper. He held it out.

“Smoke,” he said. “Better than anything you’ll find in these hills.”

The young leader took it, still suspicious. Unfolded the parcel. Sniffed. His mouth twitched. Not a smile. But almost.

"You talk like a scholar," he said.

Li Wu kept his voice flat. “I read when I have time.”

“Then you know the war’s already here. Just hasn’t reached you yet.”

Li Wu said nothing.

The man pocketed the tobacco. “You may pass. But if you come back this way—no gifts, no courtesy.” His eyes flicked toward Roy. “Especially not with him.”

Li Wu bowed again. Shallower.

The brigands stepped aside.

As the caravan rolled on, Roy caught up beside him. “What did you give them?”

“Tobacco,” Li Wu said.

“That all?”

“They wanted something,” he replied. “It d-doesn’t matter what.”

Roy grunted. “Smart thinking.”

Li Wu didn’t answer. He was watching the ridge behind them. One of the brigands remained at the top, silhouetted against the sky like a statue. Rifle slung across his back. Still.

Later, when they camped near a slope of wind-blown grass, Yvette came to him. She had seen the whole thing—hadn’t spoken then, hadn’t needed to.

She passed him a scrap of paper. A sketch: the leader’s face, captured in ten lines. Not cruel. Just tired. The angle of a boy trying too hard to be feared.

Li Wu studied it. Then handed it back.

That night, the wind shifted. Carried the dry sweetness of hawthorn blossoms from somewhere unseen. The stars, usually harsh, pulsed softer—muted through a thin veil of mist. Almost gentle.

And for the first time in days, Li Wu slept.






Scene 10

They reached Ta-li Fu under low clouds and mule stink.

The city announced itself not with towers or gates, but with the sudden presence of laundry—white sheets like flags of surrender, strung across second-story balconies. The streets were broader than Yen-ping’s, paved in places, though rutted from carts and rain. Dogs slept belly-up beneath the wooden eaves, twitching in their dreams.

Li Wu dismounted stiffly. His legs ached from the mountain pass, and his left hip had begun to catch. Too many nights sleeping on stone. He stretched once, eyes scanning rooftops. The air smelled of garlic smoke, plum brine, and wet charcoal. Familiar. Foreign. Neither.

The caravan pulled into the courtyard of a missionary guesthouse—stone-walled, flanked by two banyan trees with roots like unraveling robes. Miss Morgan, the foreign woman who ran it, emerged with her sleeves rolled and her hair knotted in a way that suggested she’d been managing something unpleasant five minutes earlier.

Her Mandarin was brittle but brisk. “We heard you were coming. You’ll want hot water, I imagine. And rice. Our cook can manage meat if it’s not too exotic.”

Roy introduced himself with his usual handshake—too hard, too confident. Morgan didn’t flinch. Yvette gave a tired smile, brushed the dust from her skirt, and immediately began scoping the light angles for the open-air kitchen. Heller had already disappeared toward the storerooms with muttered concerns about specimen rot.

Li Wu stood quietly near the cart. One of the missionaries’ staff—a young man with one ear scarred flat—offered him tea in a chipped bowl. Li Wu nodded his thanks, sipped. It was bitter and smoky. Not bad.

The afternoon was a blur of unloading. Tents came down. Crates were inventoried. The preserved rats were stashed in the cellar, the camera plates carried inside like contraband. Roy wanted to meet the local magistrate. Yvette wanted to find a view of the lake.

Li Wu wanted solitude.

But first—fire.

The kitchen was a lean-to beside the main house, roof of corrugated tin and old rice sacks. The hearth was ringed with cracked brick. Inside, an older man stirred something greasy in a wok, humming through missing teeth.

Li Wu nodded at him, rolled up his sleeves. There were eggplants and scallions to prepare, a tin of salted fish to soak. He worked in silence, hands precise, each movement practiced.

The local cook eyed him. “You’re not one of them.”

“No.”

“You’re not one of us, either.”

Li Wu didn’t reply. He ground ginger with the side of his knife until it wept fragrance.

They worked in tandem—an unspoken rhythm. He peeled garlic. The other man stoked the flame. Outside, the expedition talked loudly in two languages neither of them needed.

As dusk fell, the light hit the courtyard just right. Slanted, gold, catching in the water buckets and the edges of tin bowls. For a moment, the smoke from the fire rose straight upward, perfectly still, as if suspended in glass.

And Li Wu, hands red with chili, face slick with sweat, felt—very briefly—the shape of stillness inside himself. Not peace. Not exactly. But alignment.

Later, Roy would burst in, asking about dinner.

Later, someone would knock over a bucket of rice, and the cook would curse, and Yvette would quietly document a burned doorframe with her finger.

But for that one moment, the work was just work. And that, somehow, was enough.






Scene 11

The hills above Ta-li Fu were ribboned with terraces—green steps cut into the slope like God had paused mid-carving and left the lines. From below, they looked serene. Up close, they bled effort: broken sandals, callused feet, hoes dulled to stumps. Rice grew here like an apology.

Li Wu climbed the trail behind the others, the morning damp soaking through his collar. He kept a slow rhythm, not out of weakness but calculation. Let Roy surge ahead. Let Heller test his boots on the switchbacks. He’d carry what mattered.

Yvette walked somewhere between. Not at the front, not quite behind. She took no photos yet—only looked. The cloud cover was low, the light gray and even, the kind that gave shape without drama.

The trail turned sharp near a cluster of stone outbuildings, abandoned but still standing. A boy with a bamboo slingshot watched them pass. His mouth was red with haw jelly. He didn’t smile.

Roy barked a laugh at something Heller said about weasels. The sound scattered a clutch of crows from a nearby fig tree. They rose like oil, flapping loud, vanishing into mist.

The hunting site was farther up, just past the Buddhist shrine that had half-collapsed into the hillside. Only the pedestal remained—weathered stone and rusted incense cages, joss sticks stuck at odd angles like broken teeth.

The tiger traps had been set overnight. Pitfalls disguised with leaf mats and old netting. Li Wu knew better than to speak about their flaws. He’d seen these same tricks used by hill thieves catching tax collectors. They never held tigers.

The bait—a goat with one eye and too many ribs—bleated once, then fell silent again. It had given up trying.

Roy checked the site, hands on hips. “No prints. No scat. Damn it.” He kicked at a divot in the soil. “We’re close. Caldwell swears by this ridge.”

Li Wu crouched beside the pit. The edge was too sharp. The leaves still too green. A clever cat would smell the hand that set it.

He stood.

“I w-would n-not hunt h-here,” he said, voice low. “Too… t-too close to p-p-path. T-tiger knows.”

Roy waved him off. “We’ve got a limited window, Wu. I’m not in the mood for folk wisdom.”

Li Wu didn’t answer. Not with words.

He turned and scanned the slope. The trees leaned away from something uphill. Wind, maybe. Or memory. He stepped lightly toward a copse of spruce and bent low. There—a print. Not fresh, but wide. Imprinted into softened earth where a rabbit had dug once. Four pads. A drag mark from the tail.

He traced it with one finger. Then wiped it away. Let the forest keep its secrets a little longer.

He returned to the camp. Yvette stood beside the goat, her face unreadable.

“Don’t like traps,” she said.

“N-neither d-does the tiger,” Li Wu murmured.

They looked at each other a moment. Then she turned and walked back toward the pack mules, her sketchbook already in hand. She never sketched animals in cages. Only what tried to flee.

That night, they camped beneath the ridge. The fire snapped in the cold air. Moths battered the lamp glass. Roy told a story about a wolf he’d shot in Mongolia. Heller corrected his taxonomy.

Li Wu ate slowly, staring into the dark beyond the firelight.

He thought of the shrine, the goat, the print he’d erased.

And in the far trees, something moved—silent, watching.

He did not wake the others.






Scene 12

Rain came before dawn—not a downpour, but the kind that seeps, slow and stubborn, turning everything to paste. The tent ropes sagged. The mule dung softened underfoot until the whole camp smelled of wet leather, boiled millet, and rot.

Li Wu lit the fire with bark chips and patience. The matches were damp again. The kitchen tarp had sagged overnight, and one crate of salt fish had collapsed—wood swollen, brine leaking into the mud like something shameful. He salvaged what he could, slicing through the soft pack with his penknife. The oil slick on the surface caught the lantern light and shimmered, rainbow-slick, like oil on a prayer bowl.

A mafu tried to coax flame from a sodden cigarette. Another cursed the mule that had torn its rein and run half the night before getting tangled in a bramble. Its eye was torn now, milky and red-veined. The animal didn’t complain. Just stood there, shivering in its own shape.

Li Wu kept working.

Yvette emerged last. Her boots squelched. Her hair was pinned but coming loose, and a single black smudge ran across her cheekbone—soot, or charcoal, or maybe just the memory of it. She carried a wrapped plate under her arm. Not developed yet. She wouldn’t speak until the light was right.

Roy came next, already barking. He wanted an early start. He’d heard from a boy in a straw hat—some wild tale about tiger scratchings on a cypress stump above a burial grove. The boy had been maybe eleven, and half-starved, but Roy treated the story like a map.

“Wu,” he called. “Pack the field stove. We’re going light. Just me, you, and the camera.”

Li Wu nodded. Swallowed a reply.

They left before the tea had finished steeping.

The climb was steep, roots slick and loose. The clouds hung low, gray and heavy, pressing the air flat. The path narrowed until there was no path—just moss, stone, memory.

Roy carried his rifle with pride. Yvette, the camera in both arms, her face set like stone. Li Wu, slightly behind, carried the stove, spare plates, and a coil of rice flour bandages in case of injury. The tin case pressed into his spine with each step.

They reached the grove by midday.

It was smaller than expected. Five cypress trees, gnarled and leaning like old men, stood around a plot of half-sunken stones. Grave markers. The kind carved by poor hands—no script, just rough edges and rain-worn corners.

Roy circled the clearing. “Here,” he said. “Scratches. Three vertical, one diagonal.” He crouched. “Height of a standing cat, maybe. Thoughts?”

Li Wu looked.

The bark had indeed been torn—fresh. Not clawed. Not cat. The strokes were tool-made, deliberate. But they matched what Roy wanted to see, so Li Wu said nothing.

Yvette took a photo. Just one.

Then the silence deepened.

Li Wu felt it first. Not fear. Not awe. Just absence. The birds were gone. The wind had stopped.

The trees did not move. Not even the smallest needle.

He stepped lightly to the edge of the grove. Looked down into the valley. Mist clung to the hills like bruises. He could see the silver line of a river far below, winding through fields like a forgotten sentence.

A shape flickered at the edge of vision.

He turned.

Nothing there. Just the goat trail. Just the fog.

Just—

A sound, low, almost imagined. Not a growl. A breath.

Roy stood, stiff.

“Did you hear—”

Yvette raised her hand. Not to hush, but to hold space.

They waited.

Nothing.

But Li Wu felt it. Underfoot. In the back of the throat. A presence. Not watching.

Choosing.

He dropped his gaze, not in fear, but in respect.

“We should g-go,” he said quietly.

Roy hesitated. Then nodded. “Tomorrow,” he said. “We’ll trap this ridge.”

Yvette didn't speak. She packed her camera with ritual precision.

They descended in silence.

Behind them, the trees stood exactly as before.

But Li Wu knew—nothing was as before.






Scene 13

By the time they returned to camp, night had already folded itself over the ridgeline. The fire was low. The soup had scorched. One of the boys had broken the hinge on Yvette’s darkroom tent, and Roy was shouting—something about responsibility, about money, about how “Americans didn’t haul half a continent’s worth of gear just to watch it rot under native carelessness.”

Li Wu didn’t speak. He pulled off his pack slowly, knuckles cracking one by one.

The truth was, the boy hadn’t broken it. The wind had. The kind that comes down from high peaks, cold and sudden, and doesn’t care whose skin it touches. But Roy needed a target. So the boy, maybe sixteen, face raw with altitude rash and eyes too wide, took the blame. Said nothing. Stood there with his mouth half open and fists clenching in the hem of his tunic.

Li Wu stepped between them without being asked.

“I—I c-can repair it,” he said, voice dry. “J-joint’s not s-split. Just bent.”

Roy hesitated, nostrils flared, the way men do when they think they’re being challenged but can’t quite prove it.

“Fine,” he said. “Just make sure it’s ready by morning. I need Yvette working, not fixing.”

He turned and stormed off toward his tent, muttering about discipline.

The camp was quiet again.

Li Wu crouched beside the darkroom tent. It was a clever contraption—rubberized canvas over a pop-rib frame, part European and part field improvisation. The brass armature had twisted, but not snapped. He unthreaded the joint slowly, smoothed it with a flat stone, and reset the tension cord with a strip of boiled linen from his satchel. His fingers worked methodically. Thoughtlessly. Like prayer.

When he was done, he stepped back. The tent held its shape.

Yvette approached with her arms crossed, a wool shawl pulled tight against the chill. She didn’t say thank you. Just lowered herself beside the fire, close enough to the embers to watch them but not feed them.

“I should’ve tethered it,” she said.

Li Wu shook his head. “The m-mountain d-did that. Not you.”

She looked at him then. Really looked. Her face was shadowed, but her eyes caught the firelight like old glass. Behind them, laughter broke out from the mafus' tent—coarse, relieved. The kind that comes after near-violence.

“You watch everything,” she said.

Li Wu didn’t respond.

She leaned forward, warming her hands. “There’s a kind of tiger they talk about in Alsace. Black stripes on black fur. You can only see it when it moves.”

Li Wu nodded. “We have those, too. But we d-don’t talk about them.”

Silence again.

Somewhere above, on the far side of the ridge, a cry rose. Not a wolf. Not a cat. Something in between—hoarse, guttural, alive with distance. It cut the night like a line through wet ink.

The fire cracked.

Li Wu rose and fetched a pot of hot water, poured it into two chipped cups. The steam curled upward, ribboning the space between them.

Yvette took her cup with both hands. Her fingers brushed his—briefly, dry as bark.

They sat like that for a long time. Not talking. Not needing to.

The cry did not come again. But its echo remained, stitched into the sky like thread under skin.






Scene 14

The next morning, mist rose thick from the soil, curling between the tent ropes like ghosts in no hurry. The ground had turned to sponge overnight. Each footstep sank. The men cursed. The mules refused to move. A pack saddle had split. One crate of formalin jars had shattered—glass and chemical slicked with silt, bones soaked in a smell that clung to Li Wu’s throat like a scolding.

He carried the jars out one by one, hands raw, breath shallow. The opossum skulls were salvageable. The weasel had dissolved to pulp. Heller hissed when he saw it, then went quiet, squatting beside the wreckage with the reverence of a priest over a desecrated altar.

“Everything,” he muttered, “wants to rot.”

Roy was pacing. Too fast. He was thinking in loops again—about permits, about the Museum, about his name in a paper ten thousand li away. The tiger hadn’t come. The traps had sprung but held only mud, crushed ferns, and one half-dead crow with its wings snapped like ribboned cloth.

Li Wu said nothing. The mountain had spoken already.

He moved to the stream and knelt. The water ran cold and clouded with silt from the last rain. He cupped it in his palm, sipped, let it wash the stink from the back of his tongue. Above him, a kingfisher darted, its flash of blue so bright it left an afterimage in his eye.

He closed his eyes. Counted four beads between thumb and finger. Let the silence settle.

Footsteps behind.

Yvette.

She didn’t speak right away. Just stood near, pulling her shawl tighter. Her boots were caked to the ankle. Her face looked paler than usual, less from fatigue than restraint. Her hands were hidden in her sleeves.

“Roy wants to descend tomorrow,” she said.

Li Wu didn’t move.

“He says the tiger’s moved on.”

Li Wu opened his eyes.

“No,” he said.

Yvette tilted her head, curious.

“He’s still here,” Li Wu said. “But he’s w-watching us.”

She didn’t laugh. Most would have.

Instead, she crouched beside him. Not close. Just enough to see the water the way he did—rushing over stones older than names.

“I think I photographed him,” she said. “I’m not sure. The plate was underexposed. But… there was something. A shape.”

Li Wu stared into the stream. He imagined the image. A shadow among trees. Lines where no lines should be. Not proof. Not absence. A ghost between frames.

He said, “We should l-l-leave the m-mountain a g-gift.”

Yvette nodded. “What kind?”

Li Wu untied the pouch from his belt. Inside, a few preserved jujube fruits, a coin from the Kangxi era, and a folded scrap of scripture. Not religious—not anymore. But rooted.

He walked to the base of the nearest pine. Dug a shallow notch in the earth with his fingers. Placed the items in, slow and deliberate. Covered them. Pressed his hand flat on the mound.

Not for luck. Not even for safety.

Just recognition.

Behind him, the camp crackled with shouts. A mule had kicked a pannier off the ledge. Roy swore.

But the air around the pine stood still.

Yvette raised her camera. For once, Li Wu didn’t turn away. She didn’t take the shot. Just studied the line of his back, the slope of his hand on the dirt.

He stood, brushed his palms clean.

The mountain exhaled.

And somewhere between the trees, something unseen paused—and did not leave.






Scene 15

They began the descent in silence.

The sun rose late, lazy behind thick cloud, casting the valley in the kind of light that flattens distance—every hill the same dull gray, every tree a silhouette. The cold had softened, but the air remained wet, the sort that seeps into bones and memory. They moved single file, Roy at the front, Yvette near the rear with the smaller mule, Li Wu in the middle, where he could listen.

The trail had turned slick again. Mule dung mixed with clay until it became paste. One cartwheel had lost a spoke. Heller muttered to himself, inventorying the damage under his breath in English—"Two weasels crushed, one civet tail dissolved, three skulls shifted." He spoke like a man reading scripture backwards.

Roy was pushing them faster now. Word had come by courier—bandits active near the next pass. Rumors of rebels, deserters, monks with rifles. All equally unreliable. But reliable enough.

The American’s nerves showed in his hands—always fidgeting. Checking his rifle. Re-checking. Touching the strap on his binoculars as if it might vanish.

Li Wu carried the field stove and one of Yvette’s canvas rolls. His boots were wet. One heel had worn to the nail. He stepped carefully, choosing stones with the flat of his gaze before his sole touched down.

Just below the saddle ridge, they passed an abandoned hamlet. Three houses, roofless. A well, dry. A shrine split down the middle where lightning had kissed the stone. Roy paused, frowning, then waved them on. Yvette stopped just long enough to sketch the broken lintel.

Li Wu didn’t stop at all. He had seen too many like this. Places the Empire had forgotten. Or never remembered.

At midday, they halted by a spring, water running thin over slate. The men dropped their packs like weights flung from drowning backs. A few peeled oranges. One mafu lit a pipe. Heller lay flat on his back, staring upward like the sky had insulted him.

Li Wu brewed tea. The leaves were stale, over-used, but hot water softened everything. He handed Yvette a bowl. She drank, then passed it back without a word. Her fingers lingered longer than usual.

Then—movement.

A rustle on the far slope. All heads turned.

Just a deer.

Thin. Ragged.

Its legs trembled as it drank from the spring. Its ribs showed like brushstrokes under skin. But its eyes—its eyes were wide and steady.

No one moved. Not even the mules.

Li Wu watched the animal’s throat move as it swallowed. Watched its breath in the cold. It raised its head once—toward them, not away.

Then it turned, and vanished into the brush.

Roy exhaled first. “They’re starving up here. Nothing left to hunt.”

Li Wu didn’t reply. He thought of the shrine they’d passed. The offering he had buried.

Some things are not hunted.

He tipped the kettle, poured the last of the water into the soil.

A gift, again.

Not for luck. Not for safety.

Just acknowledgment.

They packed. Walked on.

The trail narrowed again, and the sky, at last, began to clear. For one brief hour, the mist lifted. Far below, the valley opened wide, gold-touched, raw and immense.

From this height, even failure looked beautiful.


Scene 16

The missionary outpost was built along a shallow bend in the pine ridge, its two long buildings joined at the corners by covered walkways and a low fieldstone wall. Whitewash flaked from the beams. Smoke from a woodstove curled from a brick pipe chimney. There was a water trough out front, a Bible verse etched in English into the soft stone. A line of laundry hung stiff in the morning wind, shirts and cassocks pinned side by side.

Roy had been speaking with the missionary—an older man, coatless in the chill, his Mandarin crisp with hill-accent. They were discussing trail conditions northward, and the best source of small game along the lake terraces. Then something shifted in tone. The missionary nodded toward the rear courtyard and said simply, “We’ve a good Belgian hound. Came to us half-starved with a trader’s party. Took to the Lord’s routine better than some of the boys. Yours, if you’ve use for him.”

Roy didn’t hesitate. “We’ll take him.”

The handover was efficient. A whistle, a word. The dog appeared at a side gate—low-built, broad-chested, brindled in slate and black, with eyes like rubbed glass. It stopped once, sniffed the air, then trotted to Roy’s side with the calm assurance of something long acquainted with purpose.

Roy crouched briefly, patted its flank. “Good stock,” he said, approving. “Should take to the traps fine.” He gave the dog a firm rub behind the ears. “You’ll like us. Plenty of work. Good boy... goood boy.”

Yvette stood just behind, camera case slung across her shoulder, her scarf caught at one corner in the wind. She watched Roy and the dog with a faint smile. Her eyes flicked from the curve of the hound’s shoulders to the specimens being unloaded behind the storehouse. The smile didn’t change, but it settled deeper in her expression.

The dog didn’t linger.

As Roy stood and returned to the freight ledger, the hound moved across the packed dirt toward the covered trays and canvas bags beside the gear platform. The expedition had taken in six birds that morning, plus a tin box of preserved frogs and two muskrats trapped beside the lower creek bend. The specimens were arranged in a rough cluster—some tagged, others not yet. The dog sniffed once, then began.

It used its snout sparingly, placing each nudge with deliberate care. Birds to one side. Mammals to the other. A quail wing placed atop a matching breast, not overlapping. The frogs were separated cleanly from the mammals and grouped beside the formalin jar. A broken jawbone fragment, half-tumbled near the base of the tray, was nudged neatly into line with the others. The hound stepped back, waited. Then adjusted the muskrats again—one half-pivoted so the tails aligned.

Li Wu, crouching nearby beside a mess tin and small register roll, watched quietly. His gaze didn’t linger, but it caught the movement, tracked the pattern. He’d seen such dogs before. In Yung-chang, one of the Belgian geologists had one that could find phosphorus deposits through cooked rice. Another, from Szechwan, had been trained to navigate through smoke.

Still, this one was precise.

He noted the balance. The lack of wasted motion. Then turned back to sealing the tin.

Roy had moved on to calculating rations. He passed behind the dog, glanced once, approving.

“Smart little bastard,” he said. “Knows his job already. Hell, better help than half the guides we’ve had.”

Yvette chuckled lightly. “You’ll start trusting him with the field notes next.”

Roy didn’t answer. He just looked down and gave the dog another long pat, hand firm, voice low. “We’ll get on fine, won’t we.”

The dog didn’t respond. Just stepped back, tail still, eyes on the next load being brought in from the cart. It waited. It would sort those too.

The camp moved on around it. A kettle clanged, someone cursed about boots gone missing. The missionary was already inside, probably back at his ledgers. Smoke from the stove shifted east.

The dog kept working.

The next morning brought frost. Not the thick, white film of mountain winter, but a glimmer just visible on canvas seams and mule hairs, fragile as spider silk. The breath of the animals steamed in slow puffs. Even the flies had gone still.

Li Wu rose early, as always. His body moved with the knowledge of cold—each joint measured, each exhale shallow. He rubbed warmth into his hands and reached for the kettle. The fire had died to embers, but he coaxed it back, breath and flint and whisper.

The dog had disappeared before dawn. But someone found a neat stack of utensils, sorted by size, beside the dying fire. The last unbroken specimen jar—previously left off to the side—was tucked in among the packed crates.

Li Wu did not rush. Not today.

Because the tiger had been seen. And now they would leave it.

Behind him, the camp roused. Roy emerged grumbling about bearings and angles. Yvette brushed frost from her lens case with the edge of her sleeve, careful, even reverent. Heller packed silently, coughing into a square of linen gone rust-colored.

There was a new weight in everyone’s movements. Not exhaustion—habit. The kind that comes after something has happened that no one knows how to speak about.

Li Wu ladled tea into tin cups. The steam curled upward, catching the early light, brightening in threads. He handed one to Yvette, who took it with a tired nod.

Roy approached, holding the map with both hands. The fold had cracked. He jabbed at a spot near a drawn river.

The dog wove through the loose line of mules, stopping now and again to sniff at each load in turn. When it came to the camera crates, it lingered longer, then moved on, satisfied.

“If we go south here, we can meet the traders in Teng-yueh by next week. Maybe pick up better pack animals. If the French haven’t closed the trail again.”

Li Wu glanced at the map. Then at the line Roy had drawn—bright pencil, over worn ink.

“That’s an old road,” he said. “Washed out. The l-locals don’t use it.”

Roy frowned. “It’s faster.”

Li Wu said nothing. He stirred the ashes, broke the last piece of charcoal into two.

Yvette stood. Her eyes met Li Wu’s—no question, no statement. Just awareness. Then she turned to Roy.

“Faster doesn’t mean safer.”

Roy didn’t answer.

By midmorning, the caravan moved. Downhill, toward warmer valleys, toward bureaucracy and border posts and telegrams. The tiger was behind them now. Or ahead of them always. Either way, it would not come again.

Li Wu walked with the second mule, fingers grazing the crate edge every dozen steps. He wasn’t thinking about the tiger. Not directly. He was thinking about the boy with the slingshot. The deer. The grove.

About what he’d seen and not claimed.

And about Yvette’s drawing—now folded beside his beads like a second, unwritten language.

They passed a ridge of wind-warped pines. Below, fog stirred in slow eddies. One of the mafus whistled an old opera tune, cracked and tuneless but strangely comforting.

Roy turned, once, to scan the treeline behind them.

Li Wu saw it: the flicker of doubt in his eyes, the need to name what could not be held.

He said nothing.

At the next bend, the trees opened wide—and for a breath, for a blink—the view spilled outward.

Mountains layered in gold and smoke. Terraces carved like calligraphy. A hawk wheeling so high it cast no shadow.

Beauty.

Undeniable. Unspoken.

Behind them, the dog paced along the cliff’s edge, stopping at a cairn that had been knocked askew. It nosed one of the flat stones back into place, then trotted to rejoin the group without a sound.

They moved on.



Scene 17

The rain came back hard as they reached the river crossing.

No bridge—just a dory roped to a leaning post and a line of wet-footed coolies arguing over weight limits. The ferryman’s oilcloth was shredded at the shoulders. One eye filmed white. He smoked a twisted roll of leaf so tightly packed it hissed in the rain.

Roy was already shouting. Not out of anger, but urgency—the kind that flares when leadership meets a lack of leverage.

“We’ll do three loads,” he snapped. “Two mules and the plates first. Then the rest.”

The ferryman nodded without looking. His price was scrawled in charcoal on a board nailed to the post: two silver dollars per beast, one for a man. Roy grimaced. Paid anyway.

Li Wu stood near the crate of color plates, his shoulders damp, the hem of his coat a soaked tongue dragging in mud.

A boy—maybe nine—stood barefoot near the dory, picking weevils from his cuffs. He watched the foreigners with his mouth half open, neither fearful nor impressed. Li Wu met his eyes. The boy held up two fingers.

Two what?

Then the boy grinned. Not cruel. Just knowing. And slipped away behind the grain sacks.

The river was high. Silt-rich. Fast. One of the mules refused to board and had to be half-dragged, half-pushed. The boat rocked like a gourd in soup.

Li Wu took the last seat on the first crossing. Across from him, Yvette balanced her case across her lap, both hands gripping it like a reliquary. Her hair was tied but soaked, plastered in dark coils. Her boots had come unbuckled at the ankle.

The current bucked once, violently. The boat listed. A rope slipped. The ferryman barked something no one understood.

Li Wu didn’t move. Didn’t breathe. One of the crate ropes had loosened.

He reached across, hands slick, and caught it just before it tipped. The box swayed, heavy with glass and the weight of unseen images. He steadied it with his palm, holding his breath as the ferry scraped through the middle run.

Opposite him, Yvette’s eyes met his. Not wide. Not afraid. Focused. Present. She mouthed something he couldn’t quite hear. Maybe “careful.” Maybe “thank you.”

They landed hard. The ferry jolted, bumped shore. Li Wu staggered off with the box still braced in both hands. Mud pulled at his boots. He didn’t fall.

Behind them, the river surged louder. The sky, briefly, cracked open with light.

And in that flash, through the mist on the opposite bank, he thought he saw movement—not mule, not man. Something low. Furred. Silent. Watching.

By the time he blinked, it was gone.

The second ferry never came.

The boatmen refused another trip. The water was rising. Roy cursed. Heller kicked a rock so hard he limped after.

They were split now—half the team on one side, half still stranded.

Roy paced the bank, demanding solutions. The mafus smoked, eyes down, waiting for rain to decide.

Li Wu found shelter beneath a cedar root, the crate dry beside him. Yvette sat across, wrapping her shawl around the exposed edges of her case. She looked up once and smiled. Not at him. Not past him. Just into the rain, like it was a thing she remembered loving.

For the next hour, no one spoke. The river did all the talking.






Scene 18

The mud on this side of the river didn’t dry. It clung. Everything smelled faintly of rot—old water, donkey sweat, sodden hemp. They had found shelter under an abandoned threshing shed, the kind built half into the hillside, with walls of woven cane and a roof patched with rusted tins.

Li Wu had boiled the last of the millet into a gluey porridge, bulked with dried scallion and what little salt they had left. It wasn’t food. It was weight. Fuel.

Roy refused a bowl. Too busy arguing with a map. He’d spread it across a broken packing crate like a commander planning a siege. “If we move west along the second ridge—this line here—we can bypass the worst of the gorge. Unless we lose another mule.”

They already had. A white one, snapped leg on the trail down from the pass. No one spoke of it now. No one wanted to remember the sound it made when it fell.

Li Wu sipped his porridge. It tasted like rope and smoke.

Yvette sat nearby, writing notes with her gloves still on. Her pen scraped softly in the gloom. She didn’t ask what the porridge was. Didn’t have to. She ate slowly, eyes scanning the edge of the shed as if expecting something to emerge—memory, maybe. Or meaning.

The rain hadn’t let up. Just softened. Thinned. A drizzle now, barely enough to ripple the surface of the washbowl beside the stove.

Outside, the trees stood dark and still. The world had gone into itself.

A cough broke the silence—Heller. His had worsened. Not a cold. Deeper than that. A wetness in the lungs that hadn’t been there two weeks ago. He waved off concern, but Li Wu had heard the rattle in the mornings. He’d seen the blood flecks in the handkerchiefs, folded neatly and hidden under folded maps.

Later, while the others slept or pretended to, Li Wu slipped outside. The air was sharp, filled with the clean bite of pine and damp ash. He climbed just far enough up the slope to be alone.

The hound was his only company this night; it padded over to the array of jars near the fire, nudging one that had tipped in the night gently back into line. Its nose twitched once at a beetle that had curled under the tarp, but it left it alone. Not its category.

There, beyond the ridge, the clouds had parted. Not much. Just enough to reveal a sliver of stars, thin and silver, like fish bones pressed into velvet.

He breathed through his teeth, slow and silent. He let the cold in, deep into the ribs.

And then—movement.

Not on the trail. Not from the camp. From the trees below, lower than the eye wants to look.

A glint. Pale. Blue.

Gone.

He didn’t chase it. Didn’t speak. Just stood, every nerve taut with the knowledge that something ancient had brushed the edge of the night.

When he returned, Yvette was still awake. Sitting upright, staring into the dark. Her sketchbook open in her lap.

He passed her, said nothing.

But in the morning, she’d drawn something she hadn’t seen with her eyes.

The rain had stopped two days before but the ground still breathed dampness, the smell of it rising through each bootfall—moss, ash, faint traces of mule urine. A kettle rattled near the cookfire. Someone was cursing about a mislaid specimen box. The canvas tops of the expedition tents billowed softly in the late mountain breeze, their lines twitching like threads caught in a loom.

As morning waned into early afternoon, Li Wu found himself crouched beside Roy near the gear cart, helping rebind the broken latch on the steel-edged trap case. He’d improvised a loop using an old brass button and two strands of yak-hair twine. His fingers moved with unthinking precision, cleaner now from long handling of alcohol tins and powder tins. Roy squatted beside him, chewing a stick of dried ginger root, the edge of his khaki sleeve damp from pine sap. Every so often, he’d grunt his approval, or point out a kink in the knot with the handle of his pipe.

“No—there,” Roy said, indicating a corner flange. “Tighter. You want the seal to hold if we’re crossing snow run-off.”

Li Wu adjusted it. His left hand, idle, had found a stray tent peg and was idly rubbing its pitted edge. He no longer carried his beads when he worked with Roy. It gave the wrong impression.

From the corner of his eye, he caught the flick of something descending—weightless, controlled. A shadow settled on the eastern tent post, next to a tiny dart of red cloth someone had apparently tied there overnight. It stood out from the other faded blue strips of cloth that had been tied to mark the pegging order, and left to weather. The pennant fluttered twice. The bird shifted once.

It was the same bird. Or near enough.

The tail curved in layered arcs. The chest shimmered, the curve of its throat still. It made no call at first, just stood watching.

Roy did not look up. He reached for the coil of twine. “No thanks,” he muttered absently, in response to something one of the mafus had shouted across the clearing.

The bird tilted its head. Then repeated, perfectly:

“No thanks.”

The words were Roy’s, intonation exact. Not just the sound, but the rhythm. A phrase placed back into the air like a stone returned to its shelf.

Roy blinked, glanced up once, exhaled through his nose, and went back to tying the bundle.

Li Wu said nothing. His hand stilled on the tent peg.

He looked at the red flag, the bird, and the way its claws clutched the canvas loop—not with tension, but with ease. There was something rehearsed in its balance. As if it knew this spot. As if it had practiced.

He thought of old sayings—mountain ghosts come in mirror-form, bird-shapes, to carry messages half-remembered. He thought, too, of wind catching a kite string. Of broken traps. Of the way memory echoes in a language not your own.

But the thoughts stayed unvoiced. His stutter, even internally, dissolved into silence.

He returned to the trap box, fastened the final knot, then brushed his hands on his trousers.

Later, when the pine smoke had thickened and the last tent line had been checked, he slipped into the supply shade and retrieved his pen. Not to write. Just to hold it. The lacquer thread was still intact.



Scene 19

The morning broke in streaks—light slit through cloud like the sky had been cut with a dull knife. Mist still hung low, dragging across the lower branches like a thief. The world smelled of old wood, mule dung, and fire gone out too soon.

Li Wu rose before the others. He always did. His body moved without asking. A knee twinged. His back clicked twice. He folded his bedroll with care, brushing away a single crushed moth, wings stuck flat like pressed paper. He whispered a prayer for it, out of habit, not belief.

The fire was dead. The stove cold. He coaxed it back with pine shavings and patience. His hands worked while his mind tracked the sounds of the camp: a mule snorting in its sleep. Heller’s wheezing cough. Roy’s muttered American pacing in circles inside the canvas tent.

The hound had sorted itself early—walking the perimeter, nudging a pan someone had left half-buried in the mud to face the right way up. It always returned things to where they belonged.

Li Wu fetched water from the spring. The trail was slick with green algae. On the way back, he passed Yvette already awake, standing barefoot in the dirt. She had taken her boots off to let them dry. Her toes were pale, raw at the edges. She didn’t look at him, just turned her face toward the trees and let the mist touch her.

He left tea for her beside the stump where she kept her sketchbook. She never asked. He never explained.

They broke camp quietly. No one said much. The trail ahead was narrow and steep—switchbacks across shale and crushed fern. The kind of road that didn’t like being called a road. A path made by need, not design.

Halfway through the second turn, one of the mafus slipped. The crate he carried—one of the photographic chests—lurched forward. Li Wu dropped his own pack and lunged, catching the handle before it tipped off the ledge.

The strain shot through his shoulder like fire. But he held it.

The mafu stood up, face pale. “Sorry. I—”

Li Wu only nodded. “Tie it b-better next t-time.”

The climb took hours. Rain began again, thin and sharp. At the ridge, they stopped.

From here, the land opened wide: a bowl of forest and fog, ridgelines layered like brushstrokes, each more distant than the last.

And there—across the valley—just for a moment—

A shape.

No sound. No breath.

Blue.

Not bright. Not glowing. Just the suggestion of blue in motion. The kind that lives in shadow and cannot be named.

Li Wu didn’t blink.

He didn’t speak.

He only stared.

And in that moment, he didn’t think of Roy, or science, or proof.

He thought of the boy with the slingshot. The way the old shrine split in two. The coin buried in the pine roots.

The tiger turned its head. Or maybe it didn’t. Maybe the forest turned with it.

Then it was gone.

He felt Yvette step up beside him.

“You saw it,” she said.

He nodded.

She raised her camera. But didn’t shoot.

The wind moved between them, soft as cloth pulled through fingers.

Below, the expedition stirred. A mule brayed. Heller called for help with the load.

Yvette said nothing more. Just stood beside him, both of them staring into the place where the trees had parted.

And Li Wu, hand on the packstrap, still breathless, still still, thought: It let itself be seen.

Not caught.

Not understood.

Just seen.




Scene 20

The mood that evening was brittle.

Not tense—tension would have required clarity. This was worse: the thick, gray quiet of too many thoughts left unsaid. The campfire was low, wet wood hissing. Roy sat apart, hunched over his notebook, drawing lines across a folding map already water-damaged beyond sense. His fingers twitched at the edge of each page like he wanted to erase something, but couldn’t.

Li Wu stirred rice in a dented tin pot. It had scorched again. There wasn’t enough oil left to prevent it, and no one had eaten fresh greens since two valleys ago. The only seasoning was smoke and salt rubbed from the sides of their own mouths.

He scraped the bottom anyway. Burnt rice could still fill a stomach.

Across the fire, Yvette was wrapping glass plates in oilcloth—slow, deliberate, almost ritual. The light caught on her lenses, flaring once. She hadn’t spoken about the tiger. Not once. And yet she’d spent most of the afternoon alone, near the ridge, sketching in a shorthand only she understood. She’d used three pages. Torn none out.

Heller was already asleep, wrapped in two blankets and coughing in his dreams. The mafus were rolling dice with coins that hadn’t been paid yet. The animals were restless. Even the night birds had kept to themselves.

Li Wu ladled rice into tin bowls, placed them on the stone ledge in order: Roy’s, Yvette’s, his own. He didn’t need to ask. Everyone ate in the same silence.

Halfway through the meal, Roy spoke.

“I think we should say we saw it.”

Yvette didn’t look up. “We did.”

He nodded, slowly, like swallowing something sharp.

“Then we say we tracked it. We say we found prints, baited sites, and we sighted the animal—large male, blue-gray, moving along the upper ridge.”

He didn’t say the word proof.

Li Wu sat still, chopsticks in hand.

Near the fire, the dog had begun pulling a misplaced cloth pouch from under the table with one paw, dragging it until it clicked back into line with the other gear. No one had noticed but Li Wu.

“You agree?” Roy asked him.

Li Wu didn’t look away. He let the quiet settle. Then answered, not with evasion, not with force.

“I saw s-something that d-doesn’t want c-cages,” he said. “That d-doesn’t want our n-names.”

Roy shifted. “We’re not caging it. We’re documenting it. This is science. The Museum will want—”

Li Wu raised a hand. Not rude. Not loud. Just steady.

“We c-came for a thing that m-might not want to b-be f-found.”

Roy stared at him.

Then, unexpectedly, said nothing.

The fire cracked.

Yvette stood, took her bowl to the water basin, rinsed it in silence. As she passed Li Wu, her sleeve brushed his. He didn’t look up. But he felt it—small, deliberate, real.

Afterward, he walked to the edge of camp. The wind had picked up—dry, high-altitude wind, smelling of resin and stone. The stars were fewer here, thinner. But sharp. Above the ridgeline, clouds moved like slow animals.

He pulled his coat tighter. Reached into his satchel and pulled out a folded slip of paper—one of Yvette’s sketches.

She’d left it in his pack. Or placed it. It showed a line of trees, two human figures, and beyond them—a suggestion of motion. Barely inked. Mostly implied. A shape defined by what wasn’t there.

Li Wu stared at it.

Then, slowly, he folded it again. Tucked it beside his prayer beads.

He did not need to understand it.

Only remember.


Scene 21


Rain had needled the camp since just before dawn, turning from steady soak to a thin, petulant drizzle by late afternoon. Now, past dusk, it had stopped. But mist clung stubbornly to the spruce, like breath caught in high branches. The ground under the oilcloth fly still pulsed with wetness—low drops falling in a rhythm so even it almost passed for thought.

Li Wu knelt by the fire-ring, a piton braced against his thigh, the flat of a carpenter’s chisel working at its twisted point. It had bent during a mule-slide on the northern ridge, two days ago—caught under stone, nearly cost them the load. Twelve strokes, then breath. Twelve more. A patience he’d learned from language, not tools.

Iron filings sparkled briefly on his fingers like salt.

Beyond the firelight, two of the boys scrubbed Oneida traps in the streambed—half-laughing, half-shivering. Their voices rose in bursts, brittle in the damp air. Yvette, under the fly’s edge, hunched over her camera kit, clearing grit from the Autochrome filters with a small camel-hair brush. Every time a strand of hair dropped across her face, she puffed it aside, not bothering to fix it. The gesture struck Li Wu as strangely tender—like someone brushing aside a fly without meaning to kill it.

Heller swore in his usual undertone beside a cracked tin of jewel beetles—half of them had escaped when the lid warped. He coaxed one back with an envelope and the rim of his palm, muttering syllables that walked the edge of science and frustration. Nearby, Roy sat cross-legged with a dull pencil, re-labeling the spirit jars. The paper had smeared from rain; the alcohol had lifted some ink entirely. Amphibia, maybe. Insecta? He frowned, guessing from shape, not from memory.

The dog arrived without a sound.

Black-muzzled, wiry-coated, loosed by rain and distance. A gift from the Yung-chang station—a missionary’s hound, half shepherd, half something else. He circled once, nose down, steam rising from his wet back.

Li Wu watched as the hound moved between the scattered jars. Not sniffing in the usual sense, but… sorting. One jar nudged left. Another shifted forward. Vertebrates pulled nearest the fly’s edge, arthropods clustered center, soft-bodied creatures—snail, pale lump of sea cucumber—placed furthest from heat.

The dog paused, stared at a twitching scarab. Then placed a paw on it gently, lifted it by the backplate, and dropped it into Heller’s tin.

Heller exhaled. “Jesus. Field assistant with four legs.”

Yvette reached into her satchel, pulled out a strip of venison. Offered it. The dog took it like ritual.

Li Wu laid down the chisel. Wiped the filings onto his trouser knee.

“K-Kiangsi had dogs for p-papers,” he said quietly. “M-mandarin scribes. Tr-trained to order… m-m-messages. By w-weight. By f-folds.”

Roy looked up, bemused. “You’re telling me the mandarins had filing dogs?”

Li Wu shrugged. “Not l-logic. S-smell. Ink. O-oiled silk. E-enough… t-times, and they l-learn.”

He wasn’t trying to explain it. Only to remember aloud.

The last jar clicked into place beside its neighbor. The dog stepped back, sat. Tail curled. Fire popped once. Mist coiled through the trees like a low dream.

Roy marked something in the field log:
specimens resorted by dog – sequence correct – labels pending.

Li Wu slid the repaired piton into his satchel. Sat back on his heels. The air smelled of scorched cedar and wet iron. Above them, the first stars emerged faint and uncertain.

The hound blinked once. Then curled into himself, snout resting on his own flank.

Li Wu rose, shook out his legs, and moved to bank the fire.

Some order, he thought, arrives without command.

And some silence rearranges the world just enough to be seen.





Scene 22

The trail south narrowed again, a scratch in the hillside barely wide enough for two mules. On the left, the mountain rose steep, littered with slate. On the right, a drop. Not sheer—but long. The kind of fall that doesn’t kill outright. Just ruins.

Li Wu moved carefully. Every step was weighed. His boot soles had thinned weeks ago, and now he felt each pebble like a small truth underfoot.

Below, riverlight flickered through fog. The valley was still waking, sun not yet touching the fields. Smoke rose from distant hearths in soft blue plumes. Everything about it looked peaceful. Which meant it wasn’t.

Behind him, Heller muttered again about the chest of preserved squirrels—how the straps had shifted, how one jar had cracked, how the tail of the rarest specimen had bent like wet string.

Roy didn’t answer. He was too busy scanning the ridgeline ahead, chin high, fingers twitching at his hip as if willing the tiger back into view.

But the tiger had gone. Or never been.

It was Yvette who broke the silence. She walked beside the mule, one hand on the pack, eyes on the path.

“You ever been to Yung-chang?” she asked, quietly.

Li Wu blinked at the question. Then: “Once. W-when I was… younger. With a geologist. Bel—Belgian. We hired Lolo men there. T-their dogs could track through smoke.”

She nodded. “We’ll be near there soon.”

He could hear the question behind her voice. Not “Have you seen tigers there?” Not “Will there be safety?”

But something simpler. Can we keep going?

He didn’t answer it. Not directly.

“There’s a f-festival in the valley this time of year,” he said. “Lanterns. Pigs roasted in pits. Firecrackers too old to be legal.”

Yvette smiled faintly. “Photographable?”

Li Wu shrugged. “Not everything wants to be remembered.”

They rounded a bend. The trail leveled. A broken signpost stood at the fork—one arm pointing toward a burned village, the other toward the southern route. The wood was splintered, but someone had painted new characters over the char.

Roy stopped, squinting.

“Which way?” he asked.

Li Wu didn’t answer right away. He stepped closer to the post. The paint was fresh. Still sticky in the center. Not official. Not reliable.

But true enough.

He pointed. “Right fork. Lower valley. Safer footing. More villages.”

Roy grunted. “Good. I want roads again. Civilization.”

Li Wu looked away.

They passed a field of stunted maize. Children watched from behind a crumbling wall, their faces unreadable. One held a feather tied to a stick, spinning it like a prayer wheel. Another clutched a dog with eyes clouded white.

Yvette raised her camera—then lowered it. Too far. Too fast. Not now.

Li Wu watched her.

That choice—the restraint—it said more than the photograph would have.

As the sun finally crested the eastern ridge, it lit the dust on their boots, the frost-melt on the pack ropes, the dull sheen of rust on the buckles. Everything broken gleamed for a moment. Not repaired. Just seen.

Li Wu exhaled, slow.

He felt the tiger’s absence like a weight that no longer pressed—but had left its imprint.

They walked on.





Scene 23

They reached the salt market at Yung-chang under thin, dry light. No rain for two days. The dust clung to everything—boots, crates, cloth, skin—until the world took on a dull ochre sheen. Even the air tasted powdered.

Li Wu led the last mule into the yard of the courier house. The beast was limping again. The same left rear leg. He unbuckled the load slowly, palms raw beneath his gloves. One of the barrels had shifted. A hairline crack in the wood leaked brine down the canvas wrap. The scent of dried fish and formalin had grown too familiar.

The market noise spilled over the yard wall—vendors shouting, pigs squealing, the creak of overloaded carts. It was a relief, somehow. Life in excess. The chaos of people who weren’t waiting for proof, only price.

Roy bargained for fodder in broken trade dialect, waving a silver coin like it had power. It still did. But only just.

Heller had gone to find wine. Not to drink. To trade—for gauze, for bandages, for cough syrup that might have real opium. His cough was worse now, deep and slow, like a fire collapsing inward.

Yvette had vanished after lunch. No words. Just gone, sketchbook in hand. She did that sometimes—slipped into the city like a stone into water. Not to disappear. Just to see what the current carried.

Li Wu didn’t worry. Not anymore.

He brought water to the mule, then sat on a crate in the shade of the entry arch. For the first time in days, he unwrapped his pen. It was still there—steel nib, lacquer chipped, ink cartridge half dry. He drew a single line down the page of his pocket book. Then a second. Then paused.

There was no story to tell. Not one that could be told yet.

But there were fragments.

A claw mark, real or imagined.

A deer, half-starved, staring back.

The hush before Yvette lifted her camera, and the moment she did not.

A boy with a slingshot and two fingers raised—not peace, not warning, just knowing.

A tiger’s blue shadow, shaped more by what it wasn’t than what it was.

He wrote none of it. Just let the words sit, unsaid, behind his teeth.

A breeze stirred through the yard. Carried the smell of peppers frying, horses, boiled silk. Somewhere nearby, temple bells rang—thin, off-tune, perfect.

And then she returned.

Yvette stepped through the gate like a ghost becoming flesh. She looked sunburned, her collar loose, the sketchbook under one arm. Her fingers were smudged with graphite. She handed him a folded page.

He didn’t open it. Not yet.

She sat beside him, neither of them speaking. Just watching the sunlight shift across the yard wall.

A dog barked. A coin clattered. The day, despite everything, continued.

Eventually, he unfolded the page.

A mountain slope. Pine silhouettes. One figure, kneeling. One watching. Between them: a shape. Not drawn. Just negative space. A curve of absence.

He closed the page.

Tucked it back into his satchel.

The tiger was gone.

But they still carried its outline.






Scene 24

The inn at the edge of the western road was nothing more than a clay rectangle with a roof like a sagging spine. But it had four walls, a stove, and a well that hadn’t run dry. That was enough.

Li Wu boiled water first. Not for tea. For washing. His hands had started to crack between the fingers, skin split from weeks of damp cold and mule ropes. He soaked them in a tin bowl and let the heat creep up his wrists like memory.

Outside, the light was fading—the kind of sunset that doesn’t blaze but withdraws, line by line, until everything turns the color of worn cloth. The others were eating. Roy louder now, full of plans again: routes west, maps to redraw, reports to draft. Heller silent beside him, shoulders hunched, coughing into the bowl between bites.

Yvette had taken a spot near the wall, back to the plaster, sketchbook on her knees but unopened. She wasn’t drawing tonight. Just watching. Every so often she glanced toward Li Wu, but never long enough to ask a question with her eyes.

He appreciated that.

He finished the wash and dried his hands on the edge of his coat. Then went to check the mules. One had bitten through its lead rope. Not out of fear. Just boredom. He tied it back, gentler than he needed to.

The yard was quiet. Someone nearby was playing a bamboo flute—halting, unsure. Not for performance. Just practice. The notes slipped between the trees like mist. Wrong sometimes. But honest.

Li Wu sat on the low stone beside the trough and looked up.

No moon yet. But the stars had returned, faintly—threadbare dots behind the last shreds of cloud. He found the familiar ones: the ladle, the northern peak, the crooked arrow the elders called “the path home” and the army called “the emperor’s cloak.”

He thought of Foochow. The docks. The first step. And how far it was now.

Not in distance.

In difference.

Behind him, footsteps.

Yvette. He didn’t turn.

She stood for a moment. Then sat beside him. No words. Just presence.

After a while, she handed him something—a packet of preserved ginger, wrapped in wax paper. From her bag, probably. From Hong Kong. A taste of a world not covered in smoke and mud.

He took one. Let it soften on his tongue.

“Do you think,” she said, “it was ever really ours to see?”

Li Wu let the question settle.

Then: “No.”

She nodded. “But we saw it anyway.”

He didn’t speak. But the ginger burned in his mouth. Clean. Sharp. True.

Somewhere in the dark, the flute faltered, then found its note.

They sat until the stars thickened.

Until the mules stopped shifting.

Until the outline of the tiger—not caught, not claimed—curled quietly into the folds of memory, and remained.






Scene 25

Two days from the Burma border, the road vanished.

Not metaphorically. Not in some poetic way. It simply ended—washed out by a landslide months ago, perhaps longer. A wall of shattered stone and red earth spilled over the track like the mountain had sighed in its sleep and forgotten to breathe in again.

Li Wu stood at the edge of it, hands at his sides, jaw tight. The path ahead had been their fastest route—marked by Roy in pencil, confirmed by a local official with a half-smile and ink-stained hands. But it was gone now. No markers. No detour.

Behind him, Roy cursed. “Son of a—” He snapped the notebook shut. “Well. That’s three days lost, minimum.”

Yvette said nothing. Just adjusted her pack and turned to study the incline. She was chewing something—a dried apricot, maybe—and her eyes moved slowly, tracing the cut of the ridge like she was sketching it in her head.

Li Wu crouched near the runoff. The dirt was still damp beneath the sun-dried crust. Too soft to walk. Too unstable for mules.

They would have to go around.

He stood. “T-there’s a g-g-game trail east. D-down through the g-gully, then up the other s-side.”

Roy stared at the slope, doubtful. “Are you sure?”

“No,” Li Wu said. “But it’s b-better than w-waiting for the mountain to m-move again.”

The detour took them low—past thorn thickets, through groves of dwarf bamboo, across stone so hot it pulsed underfoot. Mules balked. One fell. They unpacked it, lifted the load on their shoulders, walked on.

Li Wu moved ahead, breaking path with a branch he’d stripped clean. His hands bled where the thorns caught. He didn’t stop. Not when his shoulders ached. Not when sweat poured from his chin into his collar. Not even when Heller began wheezing behind them and Roy fell quiet with fatigue.

Late afternoon. They crested the ridge.

And saw it.

A plain—not wide, but still and green, dotted with old farm plots gone wild. Millet stalks growing without rows. Pomegranate trees tangled in fence posts. In the center, a ruined temple. Small. Weatherworn. Perfect.

The sky above it was clear. No mist. No tricks.

Just light.

Yvette raised her camera.

Then lowered it.

Li Wu stepped past her. He walked to the temple alone, the others waiting behind.

Inside: no statues. Just a stone basin, cracked and dry. A single offering still lay in the corner—half a gourd, shriveled to its husk, and beside it, a child’s drawing: ink faded, but clear enough to see the shape of a cat. Large. Stripe-marked. Blue pigment in the eyes.

Li Wu touched the edge of the paper. Dust rose.

He didn’t take it.

He stood, let the silence gather around him. Not heavy. Not empty. Just wide enough to hold what had been left here and not taken away.

Outside, the sun dipped. The shadows stretched long across the plain.

He returned to the others, slow and quiet.

Roy didn’t ask what he saw.

Yvette didn’t need to.

They camped beside the temple that night. The stars came quickly. The wind carried nothing but itself.

And in his sleep, Li Wu dreamed of movement—slow, wide, careful steps through undergrowth. He did not see the tiger.

But he felt its passing.




Scene 26

The path into Burma began not with a gate but with a rope.

Frayed. Sagging. Strung between two bamboo poles set in sun-cracked earth. No guards. No fanfare. Just a worn sign with characters half-erased by wind and time. On one side, the world they had crossed. On the other, a country they did not know.

Li Wu stood just before the rope. Dust clung to his ankles. The last stream they’d passed was half-dry and bitter with minerals. One of the mules had refused to drink from it.

Roy had gone ahead to speak with a British officer—an angular man in linen with a moustache that moved when he frowned. Heller was asleep beneath a tree, coughing in bursts between dreams. Yvette was sketching again, though her eyes moved slower now, the pages filling with more white space than ink.

Li Wu didn’t cross.

Not yet.

He turned, looking back. The hills behind them—so recently feared—seemed far away now, gentle and blue at the edges, like brushstrokes on porcelain. A place once thick with sound and ache now softened to a shape in memory.

He sat beside a fallen fence post. The wind here smelled different—drier, laced with pepper and iron. He pulled out the folded sketch Yvette had given him days ago. The tiger still wasn’t there. Not exactly. Just a presence. The negative space.

He ran a thumb over the fold. Not to flatten it. Just to feel the grain.

A boy approached from the other side—dark hair, long shirt, bare feet. He stared at Li Wu openly, chewing a green stem, then pointed at the sketch.

“Dog?” he asked, in halting Mandarin.

Li Wu smiled. Shook his head. “No. T-tiger.”

The boy squinted. “But no eyes.”

Li Wu nodded. “That’s h-how you know it’s real.”

The boy laughed, ran off.

Yvette joined him soon after, sketchbook under her arm, hands streaked with charcoal.

“They’re ready,” she said.

He didn’t move.

She sat.

For a long time, they watched the wind blow dust in circles at the edge of the rope line. The world felt paused—like the mountain still breathed behind them, like the tiger still circled somewhere out of frame.

“I keep thinking,” she said, “we’ll forget it. The sound of it. The weight.”

Li Wu didn’t look at her. Just said, “W-we won’t.”

“How do you know?”

He lifted the sketch. Held it between them. Light passed through the crease.

“Because it c-changed us.”

Roy called from the other side of the rope. “Let’s move! Burmese side’s expecting us before dark!”

Li Wu stood, slowly. Brushed dust from his knees.

He folded the sketch. Tucked it into his coat. Adjusted the strap on his satchel.

Then, without drama, without ceremony, he stepped forward and ducked under the rope.

The border did not resist.

The tiger did not follow.

But something stayed behind in the hills.

Something that did not need to be taken to be kept.


Scene 27

The way station at Ma-li-pa appeared just after the fourth bend in the coastal road—a shallow rise above a cove gone to gray sand, and a yard cluttered with broken wheel-ruts, damp rice sacks, and a scattering of dried fish skins that curled like old punctuation. It wasn’t a port, not exactly. But it had the scent of one.

The expedition caravan pulled in slow and creaking, mules shuddering under their loads, ankles caked with brine-dusted mud. Local workers with rolled sleeves and blackened fingernails moved through the motion of it all—hooking ropes, shouting brief instructions in dialect, checking crate seals as if the foreigners were simply another variable in an old, solved equation. A rusted bell clanged once from a second-story veranda, and then silence again, save for the click and knock of the unloading rhythm.

Coal smoke curled low in the air. The smell of salt clung in the margins.

Roy barked something to Heller, then began directing the specimens offloaded from the mules. Yvette had already stripped off her hat—red, wide-brimmed, frayed at the edge—and looped it lazily over a line strung between two cracked timber posts. It swung once. Settled.

A few foreign syllables floated from somewhere inside the post house—French, maybe, or Italian, but gone again before they could form meaning. The fishmonger’s boy spat and kept sweeping.

On the fence rail just beside the pigeon loft, five birds sat in uneven stillness, their feathers glossy, their eyes too knowing. They shifted only when the crate lids slammed.

From the ridge-beam, something dropped lightly.

A bird—familiar in shape, plumage, manner—landed soundlessly on Yvette’s hat.

It stood still for a long moment. Then three soft clicks. Not a call. Not speech. Just sound, placed deliberately.

The bird waited. The hat swayed once.

Then again: three clicks. Like a signal held up against the dull rhythm of the station.

Yvette, crouched beside the packing trunk, looked up. Her expression stayed flat—neither surprised nor pleased.

“Oh! I see…” She stood slowly. “Just my hat. Sorry.”

The bird remained for a beat longer. Then—four clicks, even and patient—and it flew off into the yard’s haze, past the pigeons, into the air that still smelled faintly of seaweed and engine grease.

Li Wu had seen it from the edge of the courtyard, where he stood noting which crate had split along the binding seam. His hands were at rest, thumb tapping the edge of the load register. He made no remark. No gesture. Only followed the bird’s departure with his gaze, then glanced once at the red pennant knotted beside the hat—same flutter, same shade.

It had happened again. But not really again. Only differently. Properly. As it should, here, just before departure, on the border between the old road and the mapped one. The sound had been right. The air had held it.

The form matched the absence. Or at least, the shape of something once missing.

He turned back to the crate. Adjusted the cordage. Later, perhaps, he would take out his pen—not to record, but to place it in his palm, weightless and known.

The afternoon pressed on. The clatter of a misplaced tin. A mafu cursed the pigeons. Coal smoke thickened near the door.

The way station settled back into itself. Nothing strange. Nothing paused. The line moved forward.

The British compound stood like an afterthought—low stone walls, whitewashed corners, and a square little flag that fluttered as if embarrassed by its own presence. Inside, a young officer with sandy eyebrows and polished boots greeted them with tea too sweet and questions too clipped.

Li Wu didn’t sit. He stood by the door, fingers curled loosely around the strap of his satchel, eyes taking in every detail: the rifles stacked in corners, the servant boy’s eyes (quick, wary), the worn edges of the Union Jack pinned above the desk. The whole place smelled of polish, boiled wool, and paper gone brittle in the heat.

Roy was explaining. Something about the collection. The tiger. The journey. His voice had taken on its official tone—measured, certain, imperial in the way even Americans didn’t quite realize. Yvette stood near the window, her camera bag beside her boot. She hadn’t spoken since they crossed the rope.

The hound, wet to the ribs from a rice-cart gutter, had taken up a position beneath the spirit crates stacked by the wall. Every few minutes it would nose one back straight if the porters brushed too close.

Li Wu watched her from the edge of the room. She looked out toward the hills.

He knew what she saw.

Not the path. Not the tiger.

The space left by its going.

Captain Clive—light-haired, blue-eyed, just old enough to pretend wisdom—asked them about danger. Brigands? Disease? Trouble from the Chinese side?

Roy answered easily. “All handled. Our man here kept things smooth.” He gestured to Li Wu. “Couldn’t have done it without him.”

Clive raised his cup. “Then he earned his pay.”

Roy chuckled. “He earned more than that.”

Li Wu said nothing. Not even to correct the past tense.

He was still earning it.

That night, they slept in real beds. Springs. Sheets. Mosquito netting folded crisp. It felt wrong, the way silk feels wrong on a body used to hemp. Li Wu lay awake, staring at the ceiling. There was a crack in the plaster shaped like a river. He imagined following it with his finger, through valleys and ridges, back toward the hills.

He didn’t sleep long.

Dawn came pale and loud—birds, boots, clatter of breakfast trays. Roy was already shaving with borrowed soap. Heller had vanished into the latrine. Yvette was walking alone in the compound garden, the sketchbook open again, but she wasn’t drawing.

Li Wu stepped outside.

The compound wall cast a hard line on the ground—shadow, light. China, Burma.

He stood just inside it. Pulled a folded scrap of paper from his coat. The last sketch. The tiger—not drawn, not named, just suggested.

He held it in both hands.

Then, slowly, carefully, tore it in two.

The breeze took the pieces, lifting them like ash. They fluttered past the garden hedge. Yvette saw, but didn’t move.

He didn’t need to keep it.

What mattered was already inside him.

Not the tiger. Not the trail.

But the shape of absence. The space that had changed its edges.

Roy clapped him on the back as he passed. “Ready to get to the coast, Wu?”

Li Wu nodded. “Yes.”

He was ready to go.

But he would not forget where he had been.



Scene 28

The steamer downriver bore the shape of modernity—iron-bellied, smoke-throated, painted white in the British style, though streaked now with rust and river soot. It sat low in the brown water, groaning softly with the weight of crates, trunks, rifles, and men trying not to admit they were tired.

Li Wu stood near the bow rail, sleeves rolled, eyes fixed on the churned wake behind them. Burma receded slowly, in layers: first the stilted huts, then the cotton fields, then the last row of trees leaning inward like they were whispering to each other.

He did not look forward. Not yet.

Roy had disappeared into the captain’s cabin to discuss export permissions, specimen valuations, and the possibility of publication. Heller was asleep under a pile of wool blankets, coughing even in dreams. Yvette stood by the opposite rail, camera slung but untouched. Her hair was tied back loosely, and the wind played with a strand near her ear. She wasn’t sketching today.

Li Wu reached into his satchel and pulled out the journal he hadn’t written in for days. The page was blank, the ink faint with age. He uncapped the pen. Then paused.

There was too much to say, and too little that could be said.

So he wrote one word:
留。
To stay.

Not in a place. In a moment. In the space that still hummed between breath and memory.

He capped the pen.

The river curved ahead, and with it came the smell of brine. Seabirds circled now, their cries sharper, more certain than mountain calls. Somewhere ahead, ships waited. Customs officials. Telegrams. A train to Rangoon, a ship to Hong Kong. And from there, Foochow. If he wanted.

If he returned.

He did not know if he would.

Yvette moved beside him. Not close, but enough to share space.

“They’ll want stories,” she said. Her voice was soft, nearly lost in the wind. “At home.”

Li Wu nodded. “Th-they won’t b-believe them.”

“No,” she said. “But they’ll still want them.”

He watched the river. A floating log drifted past—half-submerged, moss-veiled. Not a tiger. But it made him look twice.

“I’ll leave things out,” she added, more quietly.

Li Wu turned. “Why?”

She gave the faintest shrug. “Because some things don’t belong in cages. Not even words.”

He smiled. Not wide. Just enough.

Behind them, the sun cracked the clouds—a shaft of gold across the water, sharp as a blade but warm. The light hit the surface and scattered.

He remembered the goat’s cry. The cold teeth of mist. The print half-erased by his own hand.

And the shape of something blue, glimpsed and gone.

Yvette raised her camera.

He didn’t flinch.

The shutter clicked once.

Then silence.

The ship groaned forward, carrying them farther from the place where they’d not found what they came for, but still left changed.

Li Wu closed his eyes.

And let it take him.






Scene 29


Hong Kong burned with light. They had arrived at the port just after first light, the sky still colored with salt-rose and coal ash. It was cooler than inland—air moving off the sea, steady, steady, smelling of damp rope, iodine, coal tar, and something else. Something warm and animal. A low, unbroken breath threaded through the place, not loud but everywhere.

The dock stretched in segments, iron-linked and orderly, with painted lines, signs in English, French, and phonetic syllables marked with colored pennants. The shipping company’s compound sat just back from the quay—a red-roofed leviathan with brass-lettered signs, broad awnings, and clean water pumps that hissed with punctual rhythm. There were fences, yes, but they were more for routing than protection. The order of it all was familiar to Roy.


Gas, oil, lantern. But light, everywhere. On signs, in windows, on the metal shoulders of trams and the pressed glass of teahouses. Even the streets glistened with it, as if the city sweated brightness the way inland towns exhaled dust.

Li Wu stepped down from the hired cart slowly, his boots stiff against the smooth stone of the quay road. The harbor spread behind him—junk sails, coal steamers, the distant iron hull of a foreign dreadnought hulking over native skiffs like a god too big for its temple.

The noise was unbearable.

Children shouted in three languages. Men in English uniforms barked orders. Rickshaws clattered, wheels shrieking on wet turns. A monkey leapt from a fruit stall and was slapped for its trouble. Somewhere, someone played a phonograph too loud, the trumpet notes warbling from under a tarp.

Roy was already ahead, talking to port officials in a burst of practiced charm. His English had sharpened here—more clipped, more certain, like the Empire had refilled him on arrival. Yvette stood beside a crate marked FRAGILE—CAMERA PLATES, her fingers curled around a leather strap. She said nothing. Just watched.

They were making arrangements. A steamer to San Francisco. Letters to New York. Customs. Declarations. All the necessary rites of reentry.

Li Wu was not going with them.

Not to the Americas. 

Yvette stood near the ramp where the crates were offloaded. Her hat was tucked into the crook of her arm. Her hair, though neatly pinned, was caught by the sea wind now and then, just enough to remind one of its weight.

The stevedores were already at work.

Most of them were apes—great apes, uniformed, red-collared, taut across the shoulders and thighs, their movements smooth and practiced. They carried cargo two or three at a time, heads forward, avoiding eye contact. Their coats were trimmed short at the wrists and joints. One wore a brass insignia on its left shoulder. Another held a clipboard in one hand while guiding a winch-ox team with the other.

No one stared. Not the other travelers. Not the merchant clerks. Not the boys offering steamed buns and pear vinegar near the freight arch.

Roy stretched his back, flexed one hand over the other, and exhaled. “It’s good to be back,” he said aloud. “Be even better to get back home, right Yvette?”

She nodded slightly. Her expression held no surprise—only tiredness, held carefully.

A shriek of sound rippled across the square—not harsh, but layered, tonal, almost harmonic. Then another.

A lyrebird.

No, not one. Five. Ten.

They stood behind a kiosk shaped like a fan of red pennants, each bird perched at attention on a lacquered rod. One spoke—a fluent string of Portuguese, then a Mandarin phrase, clipped with accent. Another replied with an English passage: “Customs office located at dock level three. Departure manifest will be verified.” Their voices layered, distinct. Then one broke into Cantonese, mid-sentence.

Passengers approached, leaned in to listen. Then answered. Some offered tokens or notes. The birds repeated the responses aloud. The coordination was perfect. A third bird repeated a woman’s request in three languages. Another took dictation in tonal Burmese.

Li Wu stood beside the crates. His eyes passed over the birds, then the kiosk, then to a young man pinning new tags to the outbound manifests. He looked down. A red mark—a pennant again, this one etched into the baseboard.

The breathing sound in the air—he recognized it now. Not one animal. Many. Not chaos. Function.

Roy tapped the edge of a crate with his boot. “Beats shouting into a megaphone,” he said, smiling. “We’ve come a long way since Kansas stockyards.”

Li Wu didn’t answer.

He turned instead to Yvette. She was watching the birds—not photographing them. Watching.

Their eyes met briefly. He gave a faint nod.

She returned it. Small. Measured. It passed between them without comment.

Roy had moved on, directing the porters toward the gangway, already estimating the hold space. Heller was inspecting the canvas-wrapped bundles. A crate of rifles had been sealed under signature.

The apes kept working. The birds kept speaking.

Li Wu adjusted the strap on his case. He looked back, just once, at the fan of pennants. Then down again at the scuffed stone beneath his feet. The air smelled of sea oil, sweat, and cardamom. Not home. But something adjacent.

He said, to no one in particular, “Their dogs in Yung-chang… c-could track through s-smoke.”

No one answered. He didn’t expect them to.

The bell rang once. Boarding call.

He stepped off of the gangway with a slight hesitation.

Behind them, the birds still sang.

He hadn’t said it aloud. Not yet. But he knew he would not see them again. Not Roy. Not Yvette. 

An ape runner passed, shirtless and winded, carrying a message scrawled on thin paper. Another boy hawked baozi from a tray, steam rising around his bare arms like incense.

He heard his name.

Yvette, coming back down the ramp.

She approached with something wrapped in oilcloth.

“Don’t open it yet,” she said.

He didn’t ask. Just took it.

She looked at him for a long moment. Not soft. Not sad. Just seeing.

“You’ll write?” she asked.

He gave a slow nod. “M-maybe.”

She half-smiled. “If you do, leave things out.”

He nodded again.

Roy shouted from the gangway. Something about schedules. Something about time.

Yvette turned. Walked away.

Li Wu stayed.

Later, under the frayed canopy of a noodle stall near Sai Ying Pun, he unwrapped the parcel.

Inside: a sketch. Ink. Charcoal. Watercolor. And something else—a smear of blue, delicate and deliberate, across a shape that wasn’t drawn but implied. Shadow made real by absence.

And below it, in tiny English letters:
“You were never invisible.”

He folded it carefully.

Tucked it inside his coat.

The noodles came. Too salty. Just right.

He ate slowly, listening to the chaos around him. Watching the light shift across chopsticks and tin bowls.

He wasn’t going back.

But he hadn’t left the trail either.

Not really.

It lived in him now.

This city had no edge. No inside. Only layers.

He turned from the dock and looked up the hill—past the shops, past the roofs, toward the faded tiled wall of the old quarter. That’s where he’d go. For now.




Scene 30

The ferry to Kowloon was half-empty.

Men smoked in silence. A woman with a bandaged child stared at the sea without blinking. The wind blew low and steady across the deck, tasting of rust and salt and something sour that clung to the inner harbor’s skin—coal oil, maybe. Or old fish.

Li Wu stood at the rail. One hand on the brass, the other in his coat. The folded sketch pressed against his ribs like memory. He had not looked at it again. He didn’t need to.

On the horizon, Victoria Peak hunched like a sleeping animal, its spine veiled in haze. Beneath it, Hong Kong Island bristled—cranes, masts, telegraph wires, every edge built by the same hunger.

He was going across to the mainland, to the train station. From there, north. Home.

Or something like it.

His last letter had gone unanswered. Perhaps his sister had moved. Perhaps she hadn’t forgiven him. Perhaps the revolution had come down the alleyways too fast and left the postman dead in a ditch.

He didn’t know. He didn’t expect to.

But he would go anyway.

The engine coughed. The ferry pulled forward, cutting through the brown-green chop with a shudder. The city moved behind them, not growing smaller, just harder to look at. Like a wound with lights.

A young man stood near the stairs—slick hair, Western suit too tight at the collar. He looked at Li Wu’s clothes, then looked away. Not mockery. Just dismissal. The kind city men learn young.

Li Wu didn’t mind. The tiger had looked through him once, and nothing since had felt like erasure.

A gull landed on the rail for a moment. Its wings were ragged. One eye clouded white. It blinked slowly, tilted its head, then launched off again with a clumsy grace. The wind caught it. It vanished into sky.

Li Wu closed his eyes. Felt the ferry move beneath him—wood, water, iron. All of it temporary. All of it real.

When he opened them, the mainland was closer.

A boy on the dock waved. For no one. For anyone.

Li Wu didn’t wave back.

But he watched.

The sketch pressed against his ribs again.

Not a burden.

A compass.






Scene 31

The train pulled north with a sound like breath held too long and finally released—slow, rattling, metallic and human all at once. The compartments smelled of kerosene, boiled peanuts, ink, and the long fatigue of travel.

Li Wu sat by the window.

Third-class. Hard bench. No cushion. His pack tucked between his feet, the sketch wrapped again and pressed into the fold of his coat. Outside, the land unspooled—a scroll of flat fields and low villages blurred by speed. Every so often a water buffalo lifted its head as they passed, unbothered.

The car was full, but quiet. Families clustered around tins of congee and old bundles of rice cakes. A monk chewed on a stalk of sugarcane. A man in a military jacket, missing one sleeve, snored gently into a coat balled under his chin.

Li Wu watched the horizon.

The fields gave way to forest. The forest to low hills. The hills to riverbanks pocked with trash and forgotten nets.

No tigers.

Only dogs. Cranes. Children knee-deep in irrigation channels, laughing with their whole throats.

He closed his eyes once. Opened them to a new landscape. Then again.

Memory blurred with motion.

He thought of Yvette. Of the camera never raised. Of the moment shared in silence.

Of Roy’s boots scraping the temple stones. The goat. The eye of the deer. The half-burned map folded in Roy’s coat.

He thought of the smell of pine and blood and tea steeped too long.

He thought of the blue shadow, seen once—never explained, never proven.

It did not matter now if it had been real.

What mattered was that it had been believed.

The train hissed. Slowed. A station appeared—bare concrete, red banners peeling at the corners. No name on the platform, just a painted character half-smeared by rain: 福.

Blessing.

The car emptied. Then refilled.

An old man with hands like carved wood sat beside him without a word. Their shoulders touched. The warmth passed between them without apology.

Li Wu looked down at his own hands—fingernails split, knuckles calloused, the small pink scar where the mule’s rope had burned deep weeks ago.

He would arrive soon. Or not soon. It didn’t matter.

He was still moving.

The train clattered on, wheels speaking a language no one needed to understand.

And Li Wu listened.

END
