HELLFIRE, BOOK I




The heat was the first thing. That swampy, throat-coating kind that made it hard to tell breath from steam. Li Wu stepped off the gangplank with one hand pressed flat to his left thigh—trying to still the tremor that had nothing to do with the heat.

“Missie, this way! Too much crowd, they grab!” A coolie’s voice rose through the chaos. Everyone was shouting. Everyone was carrying something.

Boxes. Crates. A piano, half-wrapped in burlap. American crates with museum seals. Tinned butter. Books. Guns. Cameras in leather satchels. Everywhere, cargo moved like water—faster than the people who owned it.

Roy Chapman Andrews stood a full head above most of the dockside tangle, khaki hat tilted back, boots braced on the jetty. He was pointing with a pipe. “No, no, not that one—Wu! Wu, tell them that one’s got film plates, careful.”

Li Wu moved forward, limping slightly, head down. He wove through the melee, then tapped the forearm of the coolie holding the wrong crate.

“N-not that b-box. That’s—photo s-s-stuff. Put it—low. Beneath.”

The man blinked at him once, shrugged, and shifted the crate with practiced indifference.

Roy caught up, smiling like they were already in the mountains and not half-crushed by humidity and humanity both. “God, listen to this place.” He paused, one hand on his hip. “You ever seen a stuttering Chinaman give orders to twenty yelling coolies?”

Li Wu didn’t answer. He knew the line wasn’t a question. He simply adjusted the strap of his lacquer-repaired pen case and gave the white man a tight, unreadable nod.

Behind them, Yvette Borup Andrews stepped lightly down the gangway, lifting her skirt just above the mud-slick planks. Her boots were polished, but already the canvas at the seams was dark with moisture. She wore a narrow-billed hat pinned back above her ears, and carried a field notebook under one arm, open to a page she was still writing on.

She didn’t look up when she spoke. “How many are staring at me now?”

Roy grinned. “All of them. But they were staring at me before you got here.”

Yvette said nothing, but her pen paused—just once.

Li Wu watched her walk past. Her eyes moved like his did. Sideways, quiet. Watching everything. She didn’t write everything down. Just the parts that wouldn’t explain themselves.

Up on a hill above the dock, the green-black bamboo trembled in the late sun. Somewhere farther inland, the hills curled like a sleeping animal, hiding teeth.

Li Wu adjusted his pack again. His left thumb tapped reflexively over his right. He could already feel the river humidity drying into dust on his neck.

One of the porters shouted, and a domino line of reply echoed down the dock.

A small wooden crate slid from a shoulder—landed hard—and split at the corner.

A flash charge tin clattered out.

And onto the upturned rim of the crate, without sound, landed a bird. Small. Chestnut wings, barred with black. Tail as long as a man’s arm, curled into ornate trailing filaments. A red pennant was tied to one leg.

The bird bobbed once, tilted its head, and spoke in a perfectly clear female voice:

“Just arrived. What’s the news?”

Yvette glanced over. “That’s mine,” she said softly. “Just arrived. What’s the news?”

The lyrebird repeated her again, then flew off into the green without another word.

No one commented. Not Roy. Not Yvette. Not Li Wu.

The shouting resumed. The boxes kept moving. The bird was gone.

Li Wu exhaled, not quite realizing he’d held the breath in the first place.

So much noise, he thought.

So little quiet.

And not a single sound out of place.

[End of Scene 1]








Li Wu had never liked boats.

Not because of the motion—though the Min River swayed like it couldn’t decide whether it wanted to flow or breathe—but because boats meant waiting. Waiting on water. Waiting for wind. Waiting while coolies yelled directions in six dialects, and still managed to row in three.

He crouched at the prow of the lead sampan, one hand around a chipped enamel teacup that had been rinsed but never washed. The steam off the surface smelled faintly of dried mushrooms. Behind him, the hull creaked under the weight of the gear: tins, crates, rifles, flour sacks, butterfly nets, rolled canvas tents.

Farther aft, Roy was trying to shave.

He’d strapped a cracked mirror to the mast and was working over his jawline with a straight razor while the boat rocked. Shirtless, one foot braced, he grinned as he caught Wu’s glance.

“Not bad for a drifting barge, huh?”

Li Wu didn’t answer, but Roy didn’t expect one.

“Couple more days of this,” Roy said, slicing cleanly under his chin, “and we’ll be past the worst of it. Caldwell says Yen-ping’s got tigers. Real ones. Not man-eaters, not yet.”

He grinned like that was an invitation.

Behind him, Yvette had her camera open on a folding tray. Her fingers worked in quiet ritual: plate in, slide down, cap off, angle, note. She said little, but the men always made space around her—perhaps because they couldn’t tell whether she noticed them or not.

“Can’t believe they gave her that much dry plate stock,” Roy muttered under his breath. “She’s gonna out-shoot the museum record.”

Li Wu’s gaze followed the flash of silver light along the riverbank. Morning fog clung low to the water, lifting like incense between the rice terraces. Bamboo groves leaned in close, like they were eavesdropping on the river’s secrets.

Up ahead, a bend in the current. A village crouched low at the base of the slope, like it had been ashamed to settle too high. Roofs of tile and moss. Smoke trailing sideways from breakfast fires. Already, the ferry men had begun their morning shout.

The boatman pointed with his paddle and said something in Min dialect. Li Wu caught only half, but it was enough.

“B-brigands,” he said flatly. “Said th-there were two last week. Took an Am-merican woman’s ch-chair.”

Roy raised an eyebrow. “Chair? Not the woman?”

“Too m-much trouble,” Li Wu replied.

They rounded the bend slowly. On shore, a boy—naked but for a red sash—ran beside the boat, pointing at the boxes. Another man spat in the dirt and shouldered a pole. They weren’t hiding their interest.

Roy rested his hand on the butt of his revolver. “No shots unless they start it,” he said, mostly to himself.

Yvette was already crouched behind her lens.

Click.

Click.

Click.

She didn't ask anyone to move.

By midday, the boats had pushed past the outpost. No attack came. Just silence. And eyes.

In the narrowest part of the river, where the stone cut high and the current slowed, a narrow bridge crossed overhead. Two oxen were there, yoked in pairs. Their plow lines dragged behind them.

No driver.

No man.

No rope.

The oxen moved in perfect unison. Left, then right. Turned precisely at the edge of the terrace. Began again.

Roy shaded his eyes. “Good stock,” he muttered. “That’s gonna be hard to find where we’re going.”

The words hung for a moment.

No one else spoke.

Li Wu watched the plowline cut the earth, smooth as a signature.

Then the oxen turned, perfectly again, and kept going.

[End of Scene 2]








It was the woman’s laugh that startled him.

Not because it was loud—it wasn’t—but because it was wrong. Joyous. Out of place in a place like this.

Li Wu stepped through the creaking gate last, one hand unconsciously tracing the amber bead on his left wrist. The courtyard was cracked tile, fringed with moss. A threadbare vine slumped across a trellis that hadn’t held a gourd in years.

She stood in the middle of it all, hands dusty from a clay pot she’d just dropped. Tall, for a foreigner. Hair pinned back in gray-blonde wisps that didn’t quite obey the rules of her bun. Face flushed, sleeves rolled to the elbow, revealing raw scrapes and calluses on both arms. She wiped her hands on her skirt, leaving a brown streak, and smiled as if the gesture could erase the last twelve years of her exile.

“You must be Mr. Andrews,” she said, addressing Roy with a nod. “And Mrs. Andrews, I assume. Thank God. I haven’t spoken English with a woman in eight months.”

Yvette didn’t smile back, but she stepped forward and held out her hand. “I read about you in Foochow. Someone called you the ‘last Hartford.’”

“Oh? Not the ‘flower of Yuchi’?” Miss Hartford’s eyes twinkled. “I prefer that one. Rarer. Drought-tolerant.”

Roy laughed, too loudly. “They say you’re the only missionary north of the river with her own rifle.”

“I do,” she replied, wiping her forehead. “But I also keep a stack of primers and a foot-washing basin. I choose my battles.”

Li Wu hovered near the rear column of the porch, already calculating the wind direction, the height of the inner wall, the firewood pile against it. Noticing. Always noticing.

There were three exits. Two usable. One locked with a rusted chain that had clearly not been opened in months.

He whispered something to the cook, who immediately moved toward the kitchen shed with his crates.

Miss Hartford watched the exchange. “He’s yours?”

Roy opened his mouth, but Li Wu replied first—quiet, but clear:

“N-not theirs.”

She raised an eyebrow, looked at him properly for the first time.

“Fair,” she said. “You cook?”

Li Wu nodded once.

“Good,” she said. “I’ve been eating boiled grain and virtue for too long.”

Yvette was already inspecting the main hall. “Where do you sleep?”

“I don’t, mostly,” Hartford said. “But I have a cot in the schoolroom when it isn’t flooding.”

She led them inside, brushing cobwebs from the doorway with a worn broomstick.

Outside, the town buzzed with sunset sounds—clay pots thumping on thresholds, pigs whining under wicker baskets, voices echoing through crooked alleys. Someone set off a single firecracker near the temple gate.

In the corner of the courtyard, a shrine glowed faintly with its single red candle, wax puddled into a broken bowl. An ancestor tablet leaned askew behind a crooked incense holder. It had not been lit that day.

Li Wu turned, slowly, and noticed the boy.

Small. Hair cropped close to the scalp, mouth open slightly in a frozen breath. He stood behind the gate, barefoot, watching the foreigners with the wide, expressionless stare of one who’d never seen their kind and might never again.

Li Wu stepped toward him. The boy didn’t move.

Only when Li Wu reached the gate did the boy shift. Not away. Not toward.

Just one step to the side.

And then, soundlessly, he pointed up.

Li Wu followed the boy’s finger.

Perched on the lintel above the courtyard gate was a white pigeon, tail marked with two black stripes. A ribbon tied to its leg fluttered faintly in the dusk.

Then the boy was gone.

Li Wu blinked. Once.

He turned back inside.

Roy was unpacking rifles. Yvette was wiping dust from the desk with her sleeve. Miss Hartford had found a candle stub and was lighting it with a splinter from the stove.

Everything ordinary.

Everything as expected.

Outside, the pigeon flew.

[End of Scene 3]








The river narrowed at Yen-ping, tightening like a noose.

Li Wu crouched beneath the canvas canopy of the sampan, fingers folded around a dented tin of boiled tea. The boat creaked with the current’s pulse—slow, steady. In the stillness, even the water had a voice.

Above them, the city rose in layers: stone walls silvered in moonlight, temples perched like old birds above the narrow alleys, red lamps bobbing faintly behind wooden shutters. Every window felt like an eye.

RCA sat on an upturned crate at the bow, sketching something in a weatherworn notebook. “Looks different from up here,” he said, not looking up. “Prettier. Less smell.”

Yvette didn’t answer. She was focused on a photograph, trying to scratch notes into the margin without smearing the ink. She wore her field boots unlaced, resting one foot on the gunwale. Wind tugged at the hem of her oilskin coat.

Downriver, a fire cracked. Not a campfire. Closer. A controlled burn? A warning?

Wu glanced at the boatman, who muttered something in half-dialect and cast a wary glance toward the far bank.

“C-c-caldwell said bandits still near the east gate,” Li Wu said quietly. “T-two weeks ago, they took a goat cart. No one stopped them.”

Roy shrugged. “They ever take a boat?”

“Not yet.”

“Good,” Roy muttered, and returned to his notes.

The current tugged harder as the river steepened toward the gorge. Stones loomed out of the black water like sleeping heads. The boatman grunted, shifted the tiller with his knee, and spat once into the river.

Farther ahead, the lights of Yen-ping dimmed.

There was a sound then—a thrum, not unlike a flute—but lower, vibrating, like something heard through water.

Yvette looked up. “That again,” she said.

Roy’s pencil paused. “Crickets?”

“Not like any I know.”

They listened.

The thrum came again. This time with a faint metallic undertone. Then silence.

The cook whimpered.

Wu looked toward the rear sampan. “They heard it t-too.”

Roy stood. “All right,” he said. “That’s enough mystery for one night.”

He turned toward Li Wu. “Think there’s space in that priest’s compound? The one we stayed in last time?”

Wu nodded. “We can reach it by—” he hesitated, stutter catching on a hard consonant— “b-by the alley through t-tea street. No soldiers there.”

“Lead the way then. You’re the only one they don’t shoot at on sight.”

Wu gave him a look. Not angry. Not surprised.

Just tired.

The boats slid into the dock one by one. The men worked fast and silent, gear shifting from wood to stone like a breath being held.

Across the water, the sound returned.

This time, it had a rhythm. A language.

No one spoke.

A child on the shore pointed toward the wall above them, then vanished into the shadows.

Li Wu didn’t look up. He didn’t want to see what might be perched there.

He just tightened his grip on the lacquer-bound tablet case under his arm and began walking toward the gate.

Behind him, the river kept speaking.

[End of Scene 4]


Ok, Scene 5





The city gate didn’t open. It leaned. Like everything in Yen-ping, it gave the impression of being closed while actually offering very little resistance.

Li Wu found the loose board by touch. He tapped it twice, waited, then ducked under. The others followed—quiet, single file—carrying nothing that clanked.

The narrow alley beyond the gate was damp, thick with the sweet rot of fish brine, sandalwood ash, and laundry dried too slowly. Above them, scraps of colored cloth fluttered between crumbling windows. Laundry or prayer flags—it was hard to tell in the dark.

No one spoke.

A chicken scuttled past Yvette’s feet and flung itself into the shadows with an offended squawk.

Wu led them by memory. Turn, left. Turn again, under a split curtain. Past a stone lion with a pig’s skull tucked under its paw.

No one asked questions anymore.

Eventually, they reached the temple.

A ruined compound, square and low, set slightly above the street. Banyan roots choked the walls in a slow-motion embrace. The outer gate leaned open with the quiet invitation of long disuse.

Li Wu rapped the doorframe twice. The old rhythm.

A voice called softly from within. Then footsteps. Then nothing.

Roy was about to speak when the door opened halfway. A boy of ten stood there, lantern in hand, hair matted to one side with sleep. His shirt hung from one shoulder. He stared at them, blinked once, and said:

“They already knew you were coming.”

Roy looked at Wu.

“He means the p-priest,” Wu muttered.

“No, I don’t,” said the boy. Then he turned and walked back inside.

They followed.

Inside, the main courtyard opened like a forgotten prayer. Cracked stone tiles. An empty brazier. The smell of centuries, faintly burnt.

The priest stood at the altar steps, hands folded, eyes unreadable. “You are late,” he said. “She is already here.”

Yvette stepped forward. “Who?”

The priest smiled, slow and wide. “The woman who sees light.”

Yvette opened her mouth. Then closed it again.

Behind the altar, a side door creaked open. An old woman shuffled out, wearing a blue robe with a red sash and a necklace of coins that had long since lost their emperors.

She held a bird in her hand.

A lyrebird.

Not caged. Not tied. It sat calmly in her palm, tail draped like silk, one leg lifted slightly as if waiting for music.

She looked at Roy. Then at Yvette. Then at Wu.

And smiled.

“Messages,” she said. “So many lately. I keep them. For when someone comes to listen.”

She lifted the bird to her shoulder. It blinked twice.

In Li Wu’s own voice, it said: “N-not theirs.”

Wu’s mouth went dry. He had said that—in Yuchi, three nights ago, in front of Miss Hartford.

But only once.

And never here.

No one moved.

The priest gestured to the empty temple floor. “You may sleep here. Until sunrise.”

Roy rubbed his jaw. “Do we pay?”

The priest laughed. “She already paid.”

Yvette looked at the old woman. “Who is she?”

The priest looked puzzled. “She was here last time.”

Yvette’s eyes narrowed. “I’ve never been to Yen-ping.”

The priest shrugged. “Some people are always arriving.”

The lyrebird gave a trill. Then in Roy’s voice, it said: “Good stock. Hard to find where we’re going.”

They didn’t unpack.

They just lay down where they stood.

And listened.

To the bird.

To the boy.

To the temple that never quite forgot.

[End of Scene 5]


Ok, Scene 6





Smoke still hung over the eastern rooftops when Li Wu stepped back into the street.

It hadn’t lifted. It just changed shape. Became the haze of burning rice stores and half-cooked breakfast fires, of incense lit for the dead and not-yet-dead.

Down the alley behind the temple, a trail of muddy footprints led to a red stain on the stone. Someone had swept the blood, but only lightly. The bristles had just spread it out, thinner.

Wu adjusted the strap of his pen case and turned the corner.

Roy and Yvette were ahead of him, already moving at a pace too fast for what was left of the city. They had their cameras, of course—Roy slung his rifle too—but it was Yvette who had taken the lead.

Her boots clicked against the stones with the even, deliberate rhythm of someone determined not to flinch. She hadn’t spoken since they left the temple. Just nodded when Roy said, “Let’s take a look.”

At the hospital courtyard, Dr. Trimble was stitching a man’s leg with a chicken feather needle. Not because he had to, but because he could. “All we had left,” he said, not looking up. “Brigands came through yesterday. Left most of the tools. Took the cases. Fancy French leather, I guess.”

Roy grimaced. “Who’s he?”

Trimble nodded at the unconscious man. “Local porter. Got caught between a wall and someone trying to shoot through it.”

“Friendly fire?”

“In Yen-ping?” Trimble laughed, once, without humor. “There’s no such thing.”

They stepped past him.

In the side chamber, six men lay on makeshift pallets—three breathing, one groaning, two covered with sheets of coarse muslin. The smell of camphor and blood was thick in the warm air.

Outside, a cart rolled past the alley mouth. A child pushed it. There was a man inside—head wrapped in bandages, shirtless, unmoving.

Li Wu watched as the child guided the cart carefully around a mound of broken brick.

No one stopped to help.

No one asked whose child he was.

Yvette raised her camera and took a single photograph.

Click.

Then lowered it again, slowly.

They moved downriver, past the old market quarter, to the charred shell of the telegraph office.

A line of birds perched on the fallen roof beam. Swallows. Or something like them. They made no sound, but each turned to face the group as they passed.

Roy stepped over a broken message drum and kicked at the remains of a satchel.

“Empty,” he said. “Or looted.”

Wu squatted beside a half-crushed bamboo basket. Something glinted inside. A steel comb, wrapped in red cloth.

Someone’s dowry.

No one claimed it.

Up the slope toward the barracks, an execution was finishing.

The body hadn’t fallen yet. It hung, caught mid-collapse, still warm. The crowd watched in a half-circle, respectfully distant. No shouting. No protest.

Yvette looked away. Roy didn’t.

Wu just stood still.

He’d seen a hundred like it. But he never forgot the first.

A bell rang in the distance. Temple hour. Nine. Maybe ten.

No one moved.

Then—

A rustle.

A shift of wings.

And on the broken archway over the alley, another bird.

Chestnut brown. Filament tail. One foot marked with a red ribbon.

It bobbed once.

Then sang in a child’s voice:

“She already paid. She already paid.”

Yvette stared.

Roy exhaled. “Did we bring it with us?”

“No,” Wu said.

The bird hopped once.

Then flew.

This time, no one said a word.

[End of Scene 6]








The letter arrived wrapped in oilskin and pinned to a gourd.

That alone would’ve been unusual in any city but Yen-ping.

The gourd had been tied to the belt of a soldier, who was half-asleep on the mission compound steps when Li Wu found him. He stirred only when Wu reached down and unknotted the rope.

“Caldwell?” Wu asked.

The man blinked once, looked up. “Said give to you. Said don’t drop it.” Then he closed his eyes again, as though his part in the story was already over.

Wu peeled the letter from the oilskin, careful not to crack the brittle wax seal.

The script inside was hurried, blotched at the corners. Written in Roy’s hand. But there were edits—places where the ink grew dark, slashed, rewritten. Someone had been pacing while they wrote it.

He read it silently, lips barely moving.

Wu—

We’re leaving the city at nightfall. Caldwell’s sent word from upriver—there’s movement again. Something odd. People disappearing, not dead. No bodies. Just gone.

He says it’s the tiger again. Not the blue one. Something else. A man-eater that doesn’t leave a mess.

Yvette thinks we should go. She says the lyrebird came again. I didn’t see it. I believe her anyway.

You’ll know how to find us. You always do.

Pack light.

R.

Wu folded the letter twice, ran a thumb over the seal remnants, and tucked it into the hidden flap of his pen case.

Behind him, the courtyard was empty. Yvette’s tent already folded. Roy’s rifle was missing from the rack.

The gourd still hung from the soldier’s belt. When Wu lifted it, he found it empty—but for a feather. Fine, chestnut-brown. Not from a chicken. Not from any bird he knew.

He dropped it inside again and tied the neck shut.

By the time he reached the dock, the river had changed.

The water ran faster. Not deeper. But with more insistence. Like it knew something the city didn’t.

A skiff was waiting. Untied. Facing north.

He stepped aboard.

The boy holding the pole didn’t speak. Just nodded once and pushed off.

They moved in silence past the sleeping quarters, the ruined telegraph hut, the market where someone had hung silk to dry despite the blood still in the drains.

No one waved.

No one stopped them.

Only one figure watched from a doorway.

An old woman in a red sash.

She held nothing.

But Wu knew what she’d say if she did.

“She already paid.”

The river narrowed.

The jungle thickened.

The expedition was moving again.

And whatever was watching—it had already followed.

[End of Scene 7]








They reached it before full dark.

Three boats, unloaded in silence. No flags, no firecrackers. The last light slanted through the ravine like a blade of brass. High above, the hills twisted into stone ridges carved by something older than wind.

Caldwell met them barefoot at the edge of the rice paddies. His beard had gone more white since Wu last saw him, and the Bible he usually carried was now wrapped in oilcloth and shoved beneath his belt—next to a knife.

“You brought him,” Caldwell said, nodding toward Wu. “Good.”

Roy didn’t reply. Just passed him a sack of cartridges and pulled out his binoculars.

“Something’s moving out there,” Caldwell said. “But it’s not tigers. Not in the way we know.”

Yvette stepped down from the rear boat. Her boots sank an inch into the muddy berm. She looked up toward the slope. “This is where we found the footprint,” she said.

Caldwell nodded. “Same shape. But no claw marks.”

“Human?” Wu asked.

“Larger. And lighter.”

That silenced even Roy.

They made camp without tents. Just bedrolls and canvas tarps. The fire stayed cold, unused. Roy cleaned his rifle. Caldwell paced the clearing in circles, muttering to himself, occasionally glancing toward the high path.

Wu stood near the edge of the trail, eyeing the way the vines shifted in the dusk. They weren’t swaying. They were parting. Like something had already gone through—and come back.

He turned his gaze upward.

Just visible at the crest of the ravine, where the last fingers of sunlight met the forest edge, stood an ox.

Alone.

No yoke. No rope. No farmer.

Its muzzle was damp with soil.

It moved slowly, methodically, toward a plow that wasn’t there.

Then it turned.

And walked backward.

Perfectly.

In its own hoofprints.

Wu felt the hair rise on his arms. He blinked. The ox was gone.

“Roy,” he said, softly. “Did you—”

But Roy wasn’t looking up.

He was kneeling beside a mound of earth.

Digging.

With his hands.

Yvette knelt next to him. “What is it?”

Caldwell was already walking toward them. “It was buried there,” he said. “I saw it yesterday. Gone this morning. I marked it with a cross of twine. Something uncovered it and took the twine.”

Roy kept digging.

Then stopped.

He pulled something from the soil.

A flash charge plate. Split in half. The kind Yvette used on glass.

And a feather.

Not brown.

Black.

Filamented.

Barbed with gold.

Yvette reached for it—but Wu grabbed her wrist.

He didn’t know why.

She looked at him.

And for the first time, didn’t pull away.

They left the feather in the dirt.

Night fell.

And the jungle stopped breathing.

[End of Scene 8]








They tethered the goat just after moonrise.

It bleated once—sharp, startled—then lowered its head and began to chew the grass like it had already forgotten it might be bait.

The ravine rose behind it in five stepped terraces, each one darker than the last. Bamboo closed in like ribs around a black lung of undergrowth. Somewhere up in the hills, a nightjar cried out. Then silence again.

Roy checked the sights on his .405. “No wind,” he muttered. “Too quiet.”

“Same trail as last time?” Yvette asked.

Caldwell nodded. “It always comes down left, then angles across. Quarter past ten, if it shows.”

Li Wu adjusted his crouch behind the thorns, suppressing the urge to clear his throat. The damp was setting into his knees. His hand drifted to the beads at his hip.

The last time they’d tried this, the goat had stopped screaming after the third step. That was the one Yvette sketched afterward from memory. She never developed the plate.

The dog, the Belgian one, was curled beside the gear. Alert. Eyes fixed on the edge of the clearing. Every so often it would move—three steps left, sniff, back again—then sit. Tail wrapped neatly around its paws like punctuation.

Wu whispered, “He’s t-thinking again.”

Roy grinned. “Hope he’s smarter than we are.”

“Smarter than me,” Caldwell said. “I’m the one who keeps showing up.”

They waited.

Ten minutes.

Twenty.

Moonlight shifted.

The goat raised its head.

Stopped chewing.

Its ears angled left, toward the ravine. Not toward the path. Toward the thicket above it.

Wu felt it first—not a sound. A pressure. Like someone whispering against the back of his neck without words.

The bamboo swayed once.

Then a shadow emerged.

It was not a tiger.

It had no color. It was as if the moonlight bent around it.

It moved low—too low—dragging no tail, leaving no visible track.

Roy didn’t fire.

Neither did Caldwell.

Yvette held her breath, camera in lap.

Wu realized he was digging his fingers into the mud.

The dog whined once.

The goat, impossibly, turned toward the thing.

Not away.

Toward.

Then it opened its mouth.

Not to bleat.

To speak.

In a clear, high voice, it said:

“She already paid.”

The shadow vanished.

The goat collapsed. Not bleeding. Not broken.

Just empty.

Roy finally raised his rifle.

But there was nothing to shoot.

Caldwell stood, slowly. “We’re not hunting anymore,” he said.

Yvette wiped her hands on her skirt. They were shaking. She didn’t say why.

Wu got to his feet last. His legs were numb. But not from crouching.

No one touched the goat.

They left it in the clearing.

By morning, there would be nothing left.

Not even hoofprints.

[End of Scene 9]






The counting never stopped.

Crates of biscuits, labeled in two languages and none. Kerosene tins. Tent pegs sorted by length and fate. Canned butter stacked in pyramids like offerings to some strange, metallic god of digestion.

Li Wu knelt in the shade behind Lane & Crawford’s warehouse and tallied the week’s worth of calories, weights, and labels in looping vertical script. One crate—marked in red chalk—held nothing but tins of marmalade. Another was full of .405 cartridges, packed tight and silent like iron seeds.

Roy called them “unit boxes.” Seventy pounds each. Enough to feed three people for seven days “without losing our minds.”

“How long would you last on just flour and sentiment, Wu?” Roy had asked that morning, grinning through his hat brim as he thumbed open a can of evaporated milk.

Li Wu didn’t answer. Instead, he’d traced the faint stutter at the edge of the label’s English script with his thumb. Product of Brooklyn. Then turned back to weighing rice sacks.

Now it was late afternoon. The harbor sweat rolled in sheets off the bay and pooled between crates. The air was molasses, buzzing with flies and ambition.

Yvette sat cross-legged beneath the canopy of an overturned crate lid, sleeves rolled, examining a black velvet-lined tin. Inside: rows of glass plates, hand-coated, not yet exposed. A pencil lay beside her notebook, the point perfectly blunt.

“They didn’t believe me when I said I wanted them sealed,” she said. “Tin-lined, then soldered. Climate’s too wet inland.”

Roy snorted. “Just like a battlefield.”

“It is,” she replied, not looking up. “But slower.”

Nearby, a portly Chinese merchant was trying to sell them a phonograph. “Music box, no wires. Spring inside. All newest ones—Caruso, Verdi, Sousa march—American band, very famous.” He opened the crate for emphasis, as if that would soften the price.

Roy held up a hand. “No thanks. Nothing we can’t hum.”

Li Wu checked the list again, tapping each character as he muttered it under his breath.

tent (3)
rifle (.405)
rifle (250)
camera (field)
darkroom tent
plate caddy (YBA)
unit boxes (11 complete)
surgical roll (sealed)
ammonium alum (1 crate)
net (bat, gill)
handcarts (not suitable—leave)

The handcarts had failed within minutes of real use. One had crumpled sideways on a tram rail; the other simply vanished—last seen heading north under two yelling boys and a stolen sack of millet. Roy had laughed, then paid them both anyway.

“Fastest exit I’ve seen in months,” he said. “Might hire them for mule recon.”

From behind a tower of bedrolls, Wu heard the tail end of an argument.

Yvette’s voice, flat: “I’m not leaving the darkroom tent behind.”

Roy’s reply, easy: “We need space. What if it rains on the specimens?”

“It will rain on the men,” she said. “I’ll be in the tent.”

The silence that followed was long and not impolite.

Wu felt it, though.

In that pause, he knew how much wasn’t being said.

He turned the page of his inventory sheet and drew a single glyph in the margin: 霧 – fog.

He didn’t know why.

But it felt right.

That evening, just before the shipping foreman locked the warehouse gates, a small bird flew in through the high window. It circled once, noiseless, and landed on a crate labeled ETHNOGRAPHIC — NOT TO BE OPENED IN CHINA.

Wu looked up.

The bird had a red pennant tied to one leg.

It opened its mouth.

Nothing came out.

Then it blinked twice, and flew away.

No one else saw it.

But Wu made a mark beside the crate’s serial number anyway.

A small circle.

No label.

Just a circle.

[End of Scene 10]








The riverbank at Haiphong was ankle-deep in mud and bureaucracy.

Dock clerks barked names in four languages—none of them accurately. Porters hurled crates like insults. Somewhere, someone had dropped a basket of chickens and no one was pretending to care.

The S.S. Yun Ho sat tethered awkwardly against the jetty, half-loaded and already sweating tar. Yvette leaned against a pile of tarpaulins, watching two boys roll a barrel of salt pork like it owed them money.

She didn’t look up as Roy shouted, “Where’s the manifest?”

“Here,” Wu said, stepping forward with the bundle of papers. His thumb held it open at the precise line where the weight of the ammunition was recorded. Crate 22-A: .405 express rounds, waterproof packed.

Except it wasn’t anymore.

From the far end of the dock, there came a hollow splash, followed by a string of Cantonese that sounded anatomical. A second later, a thin plume of silt-brown water arced into the air.

Roy was already moving.

So was Wu.

By the time they reached the edge, a ring of onlookers had formed. Dock workers. Boatmen. One small child with half a coconut and no expression.

In the water: a slowly spinning wooden crate, slipping just beneath the surface. The oil stencil read “22-A.”

Wu felt his stomach drop, but said nothing.

Roy muttered, “Twelve hundred rounds,” then turned to the crowd. “Anyone here swim like you’ve got rent to pay?”

No one moved.

Then a boy stepped forward. Shirtless, lean. Twelve, maybe. Wu pressed a coin into his palm before Roy could say another word. The boy grinned and dove.

He came up forty seconds later with a rope, soaked but triumphant.

They hauled the crate onto the dock. The bottom slats were blown out—two dozen cartridges spilled across the wood like fallen teeth.

Roy cracked one open and cursed. “Wet through. Saltwater.”

Wu said, “Brass is p-p-pitted already.”

Yvette walked over, crouched beside the spill. Picked up a single round and rolled it between her fingers like a stone from a grave.

“Could be worse,” she said.

“How?” Roy asked.

“We could be using them on something we can’t see.”

Roy didn’t laugh.

Neither did Wu.

That evening, the rounds were dried over charcoal embers in a shallow tin pan, rotated every fifteen minutes like duck eggs.

Wu sat beside them, notebook open, logging each by feel.

On the last row, he paused. Tapped one casing with his pen.

Faintly, just below the primer cap, someone had etched a character into the brass.

Not a number.

Not a mark from the factory.

It was the word for listen. 聽.

Yvette was already asleep in her tent.

Roy was drinking with the engineer.

Wu looked at the round for a long time.

Then placed it back in the pan, primer up.

And turned away.

[End of Scene 11]








It was not the heat that made the office unbearable. It was the paper.

Papers stacked on papers. Papers weighted with stones, papers folded into triangles, papers pinned to the wall with brass tacks that had rusted and bled into the margins.

Each one bore a date, a stamp, a name misspelled in French.

Li Wu stood motionless between two filing cabinets, pen in hand, waiting for a man named Chemin Dupontès to finish arguing with his own assistant. The assistant had one ear missing and the expression of someone who had lost more to paper than war.

“No tunnel,” Dupontès was saying. “Collapsed outside Lao Cai. Four dead. Line is closed until further notice.”

Roy stepped forward. “We only need passage to Yün-nan Fu.”

Dupontès sighed. “That is the tunnel. You want to go through it, you need mules. You want mules, you need permits. You want permits, you need the Commissioner’s good mood. You want that…” He trailed off and made a drinking motion.

Roy grinned. “We brought bourbon.”

“Good,” Dupontès said. “Then you’ll get your mules.”

Wu passed the signed manifest forward, careful not to smudge the seal. Dupontès glanced at it once, then stamped it with a mechanical thunk that seemed far too final.

“Train leaves at dawn. Baggage on the forward car. Passengers behind. Do not get out between stations. Do not speak to the guards.”

“Why not?” Roy asked.

Dupontès shrugged. “Because they have guns and no wages. I don’t hire saints.”

He turned to Wu. “And you—Monsieur Wu Hung-tao, yes? You’ll translate for the station crew?”

Wu nodded. “If they l-l-let me.”

Dupontès looked at him a moment too long. “They’ll let you.”

As they exited the office, Yvette pulled her sketchbook tighter under one arm. Her blouse clung to her shoulders, the fabric streaked with salt and dust.

“I liked him,” she said, without looking back.

“You like people who don’t like Roy,” Wu said.

She didn’t answer.

They passed a group of laborers unloading crates from a French military truck. The side was stenciled with a date: 1914. A full two years before now. Wu couldn’t tell if that meant they were ahead or behind.

One of the crates cracked as it hit the ground. Inside were wire spools, glass tubes, rubber sheets—surplus of some forgotten frontier.

Yvette paused beside it.

“Do you know what that’s for?” she asked.

Roy shook his head.

Wu did.

He’d seen diagrams once. Folk diagrams. Devices that hummed with ghost-light and whispered to the dying. Electrical boxes, used in temples before the Revolution. Half-science, half-sorcery. He said nothing.

Later, back at the rail platform, the team spread out their gear under a tin awning while the sun dropped behind the low green hills.

Wu began sorting the packs again.

Yvette joined him, setting her case beside his.

She glanced at the manifest and asked, “What did you write beside crate 17-B?”

He looked down.

The word was small, circled once.

靜 — quiet.

He didn’t remember writing it.

Yvette didn’t ask why.

She just nodded, and began unpacking the camera plates.

[End of Scene 12]








Yün-nan Fu did not rise.
It sprawled.

Low clay walls, whitewashed with lime and cracked like tired teeth. Courtyards choked with vines and laundry lines. Foreign houses elbowed into old compounds like awkward guests who didn’t know when to leave.

Li Wu stepped down from the train into a wash of highland air that felt almost clean. Behind him, coolies unloaded crates with a mixture of reverence and resentment, careful not to tip the ammunition tins, but quick to drop the camera boxes.

“Easy,” Roy barked. “The glass is worth more than your grandmothers.”

Wu didn’t translate. He just reached for a box before it slipped.

Across the platform stood a white man in a stiff hat and silk waistcoat, flanked by a Chinese officer with a clipboard and the eyes of a bureaucrat who’d been promised a raise for ten straight years.

“Howard Page,” the white man called. “Standard Oil.”

“Roy Chapman Andrews,” Roy replied. “American Museum.”

They shook hands like men who already expected trouble and respected that in each other.

“This is Mr. Wu,” Roy added, gesturing toward Li Wu. “Interpreter, cook, logistical backbone.”

Page gave Wu a polite smile. The officer beside him did not.

The Chinese Commissioner stepped forward. “You have papers?”

Wu offered them. Signed. Sealed. Smudged with train soot.

The Commissioner read them aloud—slowly, as if testing each character for fraud. Then he nodded. “You may proceed to Ta-li Fu. But you must understand: the road is not safe.”

“Brigands?” Roy asked.

“Men,” the Commissioner replied, “with guns and no land. And some with land, and too many guns.”

He signaled behind him. Four soldiers emerged from the alley. Dusty, lean, barely uniformed.

“Escort,” he said.

Roy raised an eyebrow. “And if we don’t want them?”

“Then we cannot guarantee the return of your… research.”

The pause was just long enough to imply: or your heads.

Yvette stepped forward. “We’ll take the guards.”

She didn’t say please.

Later, in the foreign quarter, Wu watched as Yvette sketched the outline of the German consulate from a rooftop ledge. Her hair had come loose. She tied it back with a strip of waxed cloth meant for binding snake bites.

“I’ve never seen a city so quiet in daylight,” she murmured.

“It d-doesn’t want to be seen,” Wu said.

She looked at him.

He didn’t explain.

Down in the compound courtyard, Roy was arguing with a man over mule feed while Page opened a crate marked LUBRICANT, FOR MILITARY ONLY and handed out cigars to no one in particular.

Inside Wu’s notebook, under the list of delivered items, he drew the character: 盲 — blind.

Then beside it, he drew another: 路 — road.

He didn’t circle either.

[End of Scene 13]








The road from Yün-nan Fu began with ceremony.

Not from the locals—but from the foreigners, who tied bandanas over their mouths like bandits in their own adventure novels and posed beside the mule train for Yvette’s lens.

She snapped a single exposure, dryly noting the light as “heroic with overtones of farce.”

Li Wu sat sideways on the smallest mule.

The animal had one bad ear, a temperamental gait, and a name that translated roughly as Third Daughter of Disappointment.

Wu bounced with every step, teeth clenched to keep from groaning.

“I thought you were used to field work,” Roy called back.

“I c-cook,” Wu replied. “St-stove has no legs.”

Roy laughed and turned back to the trail.

Yvette rode farther behind, notebook in her lap, sketching without looking—recording the curve of the ravine, the line of muleteers, the way Wu hunched forward like a monk on pilgrimage.

They climbed for hours.

Past tea terraces and pine copses, through stone-cut switchbacks and empty watchposts. Every third turn revealed another shrine choked in vines or plastered with decades of inkless prayers.

By midday, they stopped to rest near a gully where the water ran clean but slow.

The guards squatted beneath a tree, chewing pickled radish and smoking damp tobacco. None of them spoke to Wu, but one offered him a plum. He took it without thanks.

Roy knelt by the gully, washing his face. “Smells better than the city already.”

“Until you h-hit the c-caravan trail,” Wu said.

“What’s wrong with it?”

“Rot.”

Roy raised an eyebrow.

“Flesh rot,” Wu added. “From the p-pack horses. Too much waiting.”

Yvette sat nearby, sketchbook open, eyes on a line of ants moving in perfect formation across a fallen prayer tile. “Do ants know when they’re walking on forgotten gods?” she asked, mostly to herself.

No one answered.

They moved on.

By late afternoon, the road narrowed into a pass. The cliff rose on one side; the drop on the other was unkind and absolute.

That’s where the trouble began.

The lead mule refused to move.

Not balking. Not tired.

Just… still.

Wu climbed off his own mule and stepped forward.

The beast’s ears twitched.

Its eyes were open but unfocused, as if listening to something no one else could hear.

Roy reached for his rifle.

“Don’t,” Wu said, low.

He moved closer, placed one hand gently on the mule’s flank, and whispered a single word.

The mule shivered.

Then walked forward.

The line resumed.

Roy stared at Wu.

“What’d you say?”

“N-nothing,” Wu replied. “She just remembered.”

“Remembered what?”

“How to walk.”

Roy looked unconvinced.

Yvette just kept writing.

Farther up, a hawk circled.

But it didn’t cry.

It just watched.

As if counting.

[End of Scene 14]








They made camp in a half-ruined temple three days out from Yün-nan Fu.

The gods had moved out long ago—if they’d ever been home to begin with. Their images were weathered to the hips, heads worn smooth by sun, names stripped from tablets by rain.

Roy called it “romantic.” Yvette didn’t call it anything.

The roof still held in two corners. That was enough.

Li Wu laid out the stove stones beside a broken incense burner and set a kettle to boil. His knees still ached from the mule. His spine throbbed like a struck bell. But his hands moved without complaint—rice, ginger, jerky, a sprig of mint snapped from the courtyard wall.

The guards lounged beneath the lintel, smoking. One was sharpening a knife that was already sharp.

Yvette stood at the altar steps, sketching a half-fallen relief of Guanyin with vines for hair. The face had worn away, but the gesture remained: one palm raised, fingers soft.

“She’s not angry,” Yvette murmured. “Just watching.”

Roy paced the perimeter, checking lines of sight. He paused near the back wall where bats chattered in a dark seam above the prayer niche.

“Think they’ll come down?” he asked.

Wu shook his head. “T-too cold. They’ll st-stay in the cracks.”

He didn’t mention what he’d seen. Not yet.

Earlier, when gathering wood, Wu had found a second altar stone behind the main one—displaced, hidden. On its surface: a single glyph carved deeply into the granite.

鳥 — bird

Nothing else. No offering bowls. No ash. Just the stone and the word.

He’d reburied it. Without telling the others.

Roy unslung his rifle and sat on a flattened step. “Temple like this? Must’ve seen everything. Rebellions. Famines. Maybe even the Mongols.”

Yvette smirked. “I doubt the Mongols took the side trail.”

Wu served them tea without a word. He placed Yvette’s cup on a flat stone beside her notebook, careful not to spill. She nodded thanks, then returned to drawing.

The shadows grew long.

A wind shifted through the open arch. The fire flickered sideways for a moment, then straightened.

From the bat seam above, a single winged shape fluttered down—slow, deliberate—and landed on the corner of Roy’s tarp.

Not a bat.

A lyrebird.

Its feathers were dusted in ash. A red pennant hung from one leg like a monk’s prayer slip.

It looked around.

Settled.

Then, in Roy’s voice, said:
“No thanks.”

And again, softer:
“No thanks.”

Then it flew into the rafters and disappeared.

No one said anything.

Roy took a slow sip of tea.
“Well,” he muttered. “Guess even the birds are polite here.”

Wu didn’t laugh.
He reached into his satchel and, without looking, pressed his thumb against the page in his notebook.

When he lifted it, the print remained.

Dark. Oval. Unlabeled.

[End of Scene 15]








The morning mist was thick enough to chew.

The caravan wound upward in silence, mules panting through their bits, guards too tired to posture. Stone steps gave way to clay and then to root-choked ruts, each one slicker than the last.

Li Wu walked now. Not by choice—his mule had refused to carry him after yesterday’s descent. Something about the smell of moss on stone had spooked her, and now she kept one eye on him as if he might apologize.

Yvette said nothing. She walked too, boots laced high, skirt tucked into field trousers. Her satchel thumped gently at her side with each step.

Roy, still mounted, looked back once. “We’ll rest at the next ridge. There’s a—”

He stopped.

Wu looked up.

The trail opened onto a small clearing. At the far side, beside a splintered milestone marker, stood a weathered tent post. Still upright. Still roped. No canvas.

On it perched a bird.

Chestnut plumage. Tail filaments curled like brushstrokes.

A red pennant tied to one leg.

Same bird?

Impossible.

But it watched them.

Yvette stepped forward, slowly. The bird tilted its head.

Then it opened its beak.

And in Roy’s voice, clear as sunrise:
“No thanks.”

It hopped once, turned a quarter-circle—then said it again:
“No thanks.”

Roy dismounted. “Is that supposed to be me?”

Yvette didn’t answer. She was already pulling her sketchbook out, but not to draw. She just held it—open, blank—toward the bird. As if waiting.

The lyrebird looked at her. Then at Wu.

And flew.

Not into the trees.

Straight up.

Disappearing into the pale sky like it had punched a hole through it.

The wind shifted.

Not hard.

Just enough to make the trees answer back.

Roy let out a breath. “Guess he didn’t like the accommodations.”

Wu didn’t move.

He looked at the old tent post.

At the rope still tied there.

Frayed.

But not rotten.

It had been used. Recently.

He touched it once.

Then turned to follow the others.

Yvette fell in step beside him.

“Have you ever seen one that close before?” she asked.

He shook his head.

She waited.

Then asked, more quietly, “Do you think it knew it was mocking you?”

Wu thought of the way it had looked—not at Roy, but at him.

And the word it chose.

“No thanks.”

He thought of the dog from the mission.

The goat in the ravine.

The boy who had pointed, wordless, and vanished.

“I don’t think it was mocking anyone,” he said.

Yvette nodded.

And closed the sketchbook without writing anything down.

[End of Scene 16]








They found it tied to a gatepost behind the mission house—a black-and-white mongrel, medium-sized, with one ear chewed to a perfect crescent.

It didn’t bark when they approached.

Just stared.

Not curious. Not scared. Just…watching.

Reverend Doyle wiped his hands on a tea-stained cloth and gestured toward it. “Came from a Belgian hunter, four months back. He couldn’t keep it—said it was too clever.”

Roy chuckled. “That’s usually a compliment where I come from.”

“Not from Belgians,” Doyle said.

The dog blinked once, then rose to its feet.

It padded forward and sat squarely in front of Wu.

Wu, who hadn’t spoken.

Wu, who hadn’t moved.

He looked down.

The dog tilted its head slightly. As if checking for alignment.

Then it nodded.

Roy whistled low. “Well, look at that.”

“What’s it trained to do?” Yvette asked, stepping up beside Wu.

Doyle shrugged. “That’s the trouble. No one knows. Doesn’t fetch. Doesn’t point. Doesn’t bark at strangers. But if you let it loose in a specimen room...”

He hesitated.

“What?”

“It sorts things.”

Roy raised an eyebrow. “Sorts?”

“By type. Or maybe genus. Bones over here. Skulls there. Teeth in a pile. Hair in a different one.”

Yvette’s face went blank. “And you saw this?”

“I’ve seen it twice,” Doyle said. “No command. Just... does it. Once, it corrected me.”

Roy laughed. “You serious?”

“As a vow of poverty,” Doyle replied.

Wu stepped forward and knelt. The dog didn’t flinch. He reached out, slowly, and pressed two fingers to its chest. Warm. Steady.

“Good b-boy,” Wu whispered.

The dog looked at him.

Then at the crates behind them.

And wagged its tail once.

Yvette leaned down. “What’s its name?”

Doyle smiled faintly. “We called it Thomas. For the doubter.”

Roy patted his thigh. “C’mere, Thomas.”

The dog didn’t move.

Instead, it trotted past him and sat beside the smallest crate—unlabeled—near the wagon.

It pawed the lid once.

Then looked back.

Roy sighed. “That one’s full of tinned butter.”

The dog didn’t blink.

Wu said, “I’ll ch-check it.”

They opened the crate.

Inside: butter, yes.

But also—beneath the last row—a folded cloth satchel, frayed at the edges.

Inside that: a sealed tin of preserved bat wings, misfiled weeks ago in Hongkong.

Roy whistled again.

Yvette knelt beside the dog. “He’s not sorting,” she said.

“He’s remembering.”

Doyle crossed his arms. “I told you. Too clever.”

Roy nodded. “Good boy.”

That was all he said.

That was all anyone said.

They took the dog with them.

He never barked.

He never fetched.

But he never misplaced a specimen again.

[End of Scene 17]








Ta-li Fu rose from the mist like something trying not to be found.

A city on a lake, wrapped in stone and silence, where the gates looked less like fortifications and more like resigned boundaries. Even the guards barely glanced at their permits—just waved them through as if the effort of resistance had expired seasons ago.

Roy hated it immediately.

“Too quiet,” he muttered, scanning the rooftops. “Too polite.”

Li Wu didn’t disagree.

Inside the southern quarter, narrow lanes twisted through markets that smelled of ginger and coal smoke, duck fat and chrysanthemums. People stared, as they always did—but not with awe. With calculation.

Then, like a cut in the stone, there she was.

Miss Morgan stood in the middle of her garden courtyard with a small trowel in one hand and a teacup in the other. Her boots were caked in mud. Her apron was embroidered and spotless. Her expression said she had already read the telegram Roy hadn’t sent.

“So this is the expedition,” she said. “God help me.”

Yvette stepped forward. “We were told you’d know how to navigate the mandarins.”

“I don’t navigate them,” Miss Morgan said. “I dance with them. And sometimes pretend to faint. Come in.”

The garden wasn’t large, but it felt vast. A single gnarled plum tree grew from the middle, limbs pinned to bamboo scaffolding like an aging acrobat. Beneath it, four wooden chairs, none matching. A teapot steamed quietly over a charcoal brick.

Roy glanced around. “Is it safe?”

“From brigands? Probably. From boredom? Never.”

Li Wu sat last, after offering to help serve and being promptly ignored.

Miss Morgan poured tea without asking. “I’m told your interpreter is the cook.”

Roy smiled. “He’s both. And other things, depending on how many legs the problem has.”

She turned to Wu, her eyes sharp. “Which are worse—animals or people?”

Wu hesitated. “P-people. Animals d-don’t lie.”

She laughed once. “Fair enough.”

From behind a curtain, a maid appeared with a tray of dried persimmons and salted plums. She did not speak. Her left sleeve was pinned up where a hand should have been.

Yvette bowed slightly. The girl nodded once, almost imperceptibly.

Roy gestured toward the tea. “What’s the local gossip?”

Miss Morgan sat back. “There’s talk of soldiers moving west, but slowly. The mandarins are pretending not to know. The missionaries are pretending not to care.”

“And you?”

“I make jam.”

They stayed two nights.

On the second, after supper, Miss Morgan took Yvette walking along the lakefront, leaving Wu and Roy with brandy and dried ginger on the garden bench.

“She doesn’t seem like the missionary type,” Roy said.

“She isn’t,” Wu replied.

“Then what is she?”

Wu looked up at the plum tree. “Useful.”

Roy laughed, once, but said nothing else.

When Yvette returned, she had a single flower pressed in her sketchbook. “It only blooms at night,” she told Wu.

“Name?”

She shrugged. “She wouldn’t tell me.”

Wu wrote a symbol in his notebook anyway.

未 — not yet.

[End of Scene 18]








The road to Li-chiang rose into clouds that did not part.

They left half the gear behind in Ta-li Fu, stored under Miss Morgan’s tarpaulin shed and guarded by a boy with a wooden sword and perfect posture. Roy promised silver upon return. The boy didn’t blink.

By the time they crossed the second ridge, the mules were half-starved and the soldiers no longer sang. Wu rode with his eyes half-closed and one hand always resting on the dog’s back.

Then, as if summoned, the mist broke—

And the temple stood before them.

Not carved, but grown.

Stone wrapped in vines. Arches cracked by roots. Petals drifted across the courtyard like ash. A single camellia tree bloomed in the inner cloister—red, without scent, its blossoms cupped upward like open hands.

“Temple of the Flowers,” Roy read aloud from his notebook. “Named by a French missionary who died of tea poisoning.”

Yvette dismounted without waiting for help. She walked straight to the gate and touched it—not to test, just to remember.

Inside, the courtyard had been swept.

Wu froze.

Not from fear.

From familiarity.

Someone lives here.

In the main hall, a priest appeared. Not young. Not old. Face like wet paper, eyes like river rock. He bowed once.

“You are late,” he said. “The flowers are early.”

Yvette replied, “We won’t stay long.”

The priest shrugged. “They never do.”

They camped in the garden. There were no insects. No bird calls. Just the quiet, steady sound of petals falling onto stone.

The next morning, the people came.

One by one. Without announcement.

A boy with a basket of white rabbits. A woman holding a pigeon with mottled wings. An old man with a box of Maltese cats, each asleep.

And, wrapped in linen, a baby girl.

Alive.

Quiet.

Held forward like an offering.

“No,” Yvette said gently, stepping in.

The woman nodded once. Not offended. Just practical.

Roy watched, saying nothing. The dog circled the cats and sat.

“They sell what they can spare,” Wu said later, while cataloging the rabbits. “Even hope.”

That night, Wu sat under the camellia tree with his notebook open and the dog at his side.

A blossom landed on the page. No wind had carried it.

He picked it up.

Pressed it flat.

Then wrote:

生 — life.

Across the courtyard, the baby cried once.

Then was quiet again.

[End of Scene 19]








It was the smell that gave it away.

Not the rot of old hides or the musk of preserved fur. No, this was something sharper—like rain on rust, or secrets pulled from wet stone.

Wu noticed it when he stepped into the altar room after breakfast. The offerings were untouched. The incense was dry. But something had shifted.

The priest met him there.

“You sleep well?” he asked, without looking up.

“I slept,” Wu replied.

They stood in silence.

Then the priest stepped behind the altar and knelt.

He pressed one hand against a loose floor tile—smoothed not by water or time, but by repetition.

It lifted clean.

Beneath it: skins. Folded tight. Rolled with care. Fox, civet, goral. A snow leopard pelt, edges scorched. At least twenty in total, stacked like prayers.

The priest didn’t speak. Just laid a silver coin beside the opening.

Not into it—beside it.

“For the silence,” he said.

Wu didn’t nod. Didn’t flinch.

He simply wrote it down later.

In the courtyard, Roy was arguing with a muleteer. Yvette sat by the camellia tree, drying a glass plate in the shade, her hair pinned back with a brass clip that caught the sun like a blade.

“I didn’t think they sold here,” Roy said when Wu returned.

“They don’t,” Wu replied.

Yvette glanced up. “What did you find?”

“Nothing.”

She stared a second longer, then turned back to her plate.

Later, while packing, the dog nosed a satchel and whined.

Wu opened it. Inside: a fox pelt, curled tight, already tagged with Roy’s handwriting.

But Wu hadn’t seen Roy carry it out.

He looked toward the altar room. The door was closed.

He did not open it again.

That night, he sketched the layout of the temple from memory. The location of the trapdoor. The curve of the steps. The exact number of tiles on the altar floor.

Then, on the last page, he drew a coin.

Not dropped.

Placed.

Next to the character:

值 — value.

[End of Scene 20]








He arrived without warning, just after the noon bell.

One moment: silence, heat, and the slow drone of flies circling the grain sacks.
The next: a cough, the sound of boots on stone, and a voice like dry leaves—“Am I interrupting the century?”

Roy looked up from his specimen case.

Yvette paused mid-swipe of her darkroom cloth.

Li Wu stood, slowly, pen still in hand.

The man who entered was tall, thin as cane reed, with a beard more tangled than deliberate and a satchel made of patched wool and optimism. His trousers were Austrian military cut, twenty years old and mended with Chinese string. Around his neck hung a lens—cracked. Around his wrist, prayer beads not his own.

“Franz Haendel-Mazzetti,” he said, bowing slightly, hat pressed to his chest. “Botanist. And involuntary Buddhist, depending on the week.”

Roy shook his hand. “I thought you were north, near Tibet.”

“So did the Austrians,” Franz said. “But the passes are too hungry this year. I came down instead. Flowers are less particular.”

He nodded toward Yvette. “And you must be the woman they said had eyes like daguerreotype shutters.”

Yvette didn’t blink. “I’ve been called worse.”

Franz smiled—genuinely—and sat without being asked.

He ate half a lunch without pause: rice, pickled radish, and something Wu hadn’t named out loud. He drank weak tea like it was wine. Then, just as abruptly, he stood.

From his satchel, he produced: a pressed flower in oilcloth, a crumpled railway map with no rails left on it, and a molar. Human. Polished.

“Gifts,” he said. “None are cursed. Except possibly the map.”

Roy took the flower.

Yvette took the tooth.

Wu took the map.

“You’ll want that when you reach the ghost river,” Franz said. “The current runs backward. But only when watched.”

He said it like weather.

Then he turned, bowed to no one in particular, and walked out of the courtyard into the mist.

Roy stared after him. “Is he always like that?”

Wu nodded. “Worse, when he’s clean.”

Yvette held up the tooth to the light. A faint line etched around the root.

A symbol. Not one she recognized.

She handed it to Wu.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then wrapped it in muslin and placed it in his tablet case.

Later that night, the dog found Franz’s footprints beyond the temple gate.

But only heading out.

None coming in.

[End of Scene 21]








The air changed first.

Not colder—cleaner. Thinner. Like every breath now had to be earned.

Li Wu felt it in his ears, in his ribs, in the way sound carried too far or not at all. The mountains had closed in around them like ribs around a heart. And within that heart, the Moso waited.

They did not greet the caravan with fanfare. No drums. No open arms.

Just eyes. Dozens of them, half-hidden behind trees, under eaves, across fields.

Watching.

Roy dismounted with confidence, boots hitting the earth like punctuation.

Yvette stayed on her mule until a young girl stepped forward and offered her a plum. Then she smiled and stepped down, careful not to spook the moment.

The first to speak was a man in a goatskin vest, bow slung over his shoulder, crosshatched tattoos fading into the hollows of his collarbones. He said something in a dialect none of the soldiers recognized.

Wu stepped forward. He didn’t understand all of it either. But enough.

“He says we may stay three nights. No weapons in the shrine. And no questions about their dogs.”

Roy raised an eyebrow. “What dogs?”

Wu didn’t answer.

They camped at the edge of a terraced slope, below a ring of trees hung with antlers and carved prayer stones. The Moso brought millet, honeyed roots, and slabs of dried fish that glistened like bronze.

In return, Roy offered salt, matches, and a copper magnifying glass.

Yvette gave nothing but attention—and they gave her everything in return.

By the second night, they let her sketch the children.

By the third, they let her photograph the dogs.

Not one barked.

Not once.

Each dog moved through the village like a monk on ritual. They lay beside the sick without touching them. Herded chickens without chasing. One even sorted firewood into neat stacks—dry, green, damp.

Roy whispered to Wu, “That’s not training.”

“No,” Wu replied. “It’s inheritance.”

That night, around the fire, an elder placed a fur-wrapped bundle beside Wu and spoke six syllables. Quiet. Intentional.

Wu opened it.

Inside: a flint blade, a carved bone needle, and a scrap of paper with three characters written in a child’s hand.

他們記住 — They remember.

Yvette leaned over his shoulder. “Who does?”

Wu didn’t speak.

Instead, he closed the bundle, tied it shut, and placed it gently beside his pack.

The dog—the Belgian one—lay nearby, eyes open.

Watching the fire.

Not the flames.

The shadows.

[End of Scene 22]








It was nearly dark when they brought in the goral.

Shot clean through the shoulder by one of the Moso hunters, carried down in a bamboo sling, its legs stiff but eyes still open. Blood soaked through the sling like spilled lacquer.

Roy crouched beside it with practiced admiration. “Perfect pelt. No tearing.”

“Male,” Yvette observed, kneeling opposite him. “Young. Maybe two years.”

Li Wu stood back, hand on the Belgian dog’s collar. The dog didn’t growl, didn’t strain—just watched.

The goral twitched once.

Roy stepped aside, reaching for the skinning tools.

That’s when it moved.

Not violently. Not convulsively.

Deliberately.

It pushed itself up—front legs shaking, mouth slack—and staggered toward the sorting table.

Yvette rose, stunned.

The goral reached the far corner—where the specimens were grouped by classification: rodents in one pile, birds in another, ungulates in a third.

It turned once, as if confirming.

Then laid itself down atop the correct pile.

Its ribs fluttered once.

And stopped.

No one spoke.

The Moso hunter crossed himself. A gesture not his own. Something borrowed. Something remembered.

Roy finally broke the silence.

“Good instincts,” he said, softly.

Yvette stared at the goral, then at Wu.

“He chose,” she said.

Wu said nothing. Just stepped forward and closed the goral’s eyes with two fingers.

The dog didn’t move.

Later, while the others skinned and tagged, Wu opened his notebook.

He drew the goral.

Then, beside it, wrote a single word:

歸 — to return.

He underlined it once.

Twice.

Then turned the page.

[End of Scene 23]








The snow had come without warning. Not a storm, not even a flurry—just an overnight decision by the sky to forget the season.

Li Wu woke to silence and light. The air thin, the world blanketed in white that hadn’t existed the day before. The trees had stopped breathing. Even the dog walked quieter.

They broke camp with gloved fingers and reluctant mules. Yvette packed her glass plates as if sealing relics. Roy said nothing as he checked his rifle; for once, there was no joke.

The path narrowed as they climbed—a long cleft of stone between high pine and wind-bent rock. Wu led now. The guides had turned back two days earlier. The Moso said it was beyond their map.

They reached the ridge by midmorning.

There, in the snow—
Tracks.

Not many. Just four or five, but clear. Crisp.

Too large for wolf. Too narrow for bear.

No claw marks. No heel.

Each print was shaped like an open palm.

Roy crouched beside them. “Not human,” he said, too quickly. “Not even close.”

The Belgian dog didn’t growl. It sat. Alert. Ears back. Waiting.

Yvette photographed the prints without speaking. Then she handed the camera to Wu.

“You take the last one,” she said.

Wu looked down.

He had seen tracks like this before.

In a village near Yen-ping, years ago.
On the clay floor of a house that no longer stood.
In a dream he hadn’t admitted was memory.

He raised the lens. Focused.

Then hesitated.

And did not press the shutter.

Instead, he knelt. Pulled a sheet of paper from his tablet case.

Laid it gently over the largest print.

And traced.

When he stood, he did not speak.

He folded the page twice and slipped it into a lacquer envelope.

Roy stood. “Could be anything.”

Yvette replied, “Or everything.”

They turned, leaving the tracks behind.

But the dog stayed.

Just for a moment.

Then it, too, walked on—without looking back.

[End of Scene 24 | End of Act II]




The path narrowed as they climbed.

Not by erosion, but by design—hewn by hands that had long since turned to dust, bordered by pine roots and crumbling stones etched with forgotten prayers.

Li Wu led the second mule now, the Belgian dog walking just ahead, nose low, tail neither high nor tucked. The creature no longer barked. It hadn’t in weeks.

Behind them, Roy rode with his boots unbuckled, hat crooked, arms loose on the reins. He looked like a man satisfied with the arc of a job not quite finished.

Yvette rode alone. Her satchel, heavy with plates, rested on her lap, and she’d tied her hair back with the last clean strip of linen from the surgical kit.

The sky was a bruised silver, low with cloud, high with silence.

They passed the last of the Moso hunters near a curve marked by weather-polished cairns. One nodded in farewell. Another simply tapped the butt of his bow against a tree.

Then the party was alone.

Alone, except for the quiet.

And the mule that stopped.

It wasn’t dramatic. No snort, no stumble. It simply froze mid-step, one hoof lifted like an unfinished thought.

Wu approached carefully. Checked its legs. Its pack. Its breath. Nothing wrong.

The dog stepped forward.

It didn’t sniff. Didn’t bark. It just looked.

The mule lowered its hoof.

And continued.

No one spoke.

Later, when they stopped for a midday rest beside a stream so narrow it didn’t appear on Roy’s map, Wu wandered ahead. Not far—just far enough to be alone.

That’s when he saw it.

A moss-covered stone, no taller than his knee, half-buried beneath the roots of a leaning pine. It bore four characters, carved with care but worn with time.

Three were standard.

The fourth—no.

The fourth was one he’d seen before.

On the red pennant tied to the leg of a lyrebird.
In the pressed flower left by Haendel-Mazzetti.
In a page of his own notes he didn’t remember writing.

It wasn’t a word. Not exactly. Not yet.

He reached out. Touched it lightly.

The stone was warm.

When he returned to camp, Yvette handed him a tin of dried plum. Roy was already asleep under his coat, back against a stump.

The dog sat beside the gear. Watching.

Wu sat down slowly, opened his notebook, and drew the character again.

He didn’t label it.

He didn’t need to.

[End of Scene 25]








Laundry lines strung between two weathered prayer poles fluttered like faded flags, each one bearing the story of a hard season. Shirts stiff with salt. Trousers patched at the knees. A chemise with one sleeve gone entirely.

And on the end of the line: a red felt hat. Yvette’s.

Roy had teased her when she bought it in Hanoi. “You planning to impersonate a colonial widow?”
She hadn’t answered then. She hadn’t worn it often since.

Now it swayed gently in the mountain wind, tethered by a single wooden clothespin.

The caravan had stopped for water—a small spring trickling through a cleft in the stone. The soldiers sat with their boots off, soaking socks and bruises. Roy squatted by the fire circle, sketching a map from memory and muttering distances.

Yvette reached for the next pair of trousers on the line.

That’s when it landed.

Chestnut feathers. Curled tail. Filament wings that caught the sun at strange angles.
A lyrebird.

It perched lightly atop the red hat, talons barely creasing the felt. Its head tilted. Eyes fixed forward.

No one moved.

Yvette looked at it. Calm. Even.

She said, “Just my hat, sorry.”

The bird bobbed once. Then repeated:
“Just my hat, sorry.”

Its voice was hers. Perfectly.

Then it turned, leapt into the air, and vanished beyond the trees.

Roy didn’t look up from his sketch.

The soldiers laughed at something unrelated.

Wu continued drying the rice tin, then turned it upside down and patted the lid twice, as he always did.

Yvette clipped the next item to the line.

Then stood for a moment. Hand on the red felt brim.

Not smiling.

Just steady.

The wind shifted.

And carried no sound.

[End of Scene 26]








The supply tent had never been tidy.

But this morning, it was… ordered.

Not clean—nothing in the mountains stayed clean—but arranged. Harmonized. Tin crates in rows by function, not size. Netting folded into perfect thirds. The coils of rope set down like brushstrokes.

And at the center of it all: the ants.

A stream of them, winding between the crates, carrying nothing. Just walking.

Wu crouched in the entrance, elbow on one knee, watching as the ants split at a junction and formed a perfect ring around a scattered pile of sesame seeds—left from breakfast.

The ring tightened, then opened.

The seeds slid into two clusters.

One pale. One dark.

He said nothing.

Yvette appeared behind him, quietly, holding a fresh glass plate wrapped in cloth.

She stood beside him a moment, then knelt and pulled a soft pencil from her pocket.

Not to sketch the ants.

To trace the lines they weren’t walking.

Negative space.

She titled the page: Exclusion Drift – Li Wu’s Tent.

The Belgian dog padded into the tent and sat beside Wu, alert but untroubled. Its head followed the ants for a few seconds. Then it looked up and turned toward a box labeled ETHNOGRAPHIC – MISC.

It nudged it with one paw.

Wu rose, walked over, lifted the lid.

Inside: wool shawls, carved charms, a wrapped cylinder of unknown origin. And in the corner, tucked beneath a weather report no one had packed—

A red pennant.

Unfolded.

Untied.

Yvette didn’t react. She folded her cloth back over the plate.

Wu replaced the lid.

The ants filed out through a tear in the canvas flap.

The dog followed.

Roy’s voice called from outside: “They’ve got mule space for two more boxes. Pick your favorites.”

Yvette stood.

“So many choices,” she said.

Wu folded the pennant and slid it into his tablet case without a word.

Then followed her into the light.

[End of Scene 27]








The trading post at the Burma border buzzed with the rhythm of departure—ropes hauled taut, crates thudding against planks, calls in three languages bouncing between walls whitewashed with heat.

A brass whistle shrieked once, unnecessarily. Nothing changed.

Li Wu stepped down from the caravan wagon and immediately felt the shift.

Not in weather.

In precision.

Every movement in the depot followed pattern. The workers moved not just with discipline, but harmony—mirroring, coordinating, adjusting as if to music no one heard.

And some of them—

Were apes.

Not monkeys. Not dressed-up pets.

Large. Muscular. Clothed in matching khaki shirts, red armbands, and brimmed caps.

Each carried a clipboard.

Each wore gloves.

They moved as if born into the work—one lifted a specimen crate onto a cart with delicacy; another helped coil a shipping rope with one smooth gesture. A third walked past Wu, made direct eye contact, then continued without pause.

Roy stepped off the wagon behind him, nodded toward the bustle. “Finally. A crew that doesn’t complain.”

Yvette stepped beside them, untying her scarf with one hand. “Efficient,” she murmured. “Nothing wasted.”

One of the uniformed apes approached. Held out a gloved hand.

Roy placed a coin in the palm. Without hesitation.

The ape saluted.

Pocketed the coin.

Moved on.

Wu watched it climb the ramp to the cargo barge, crate balanced on one shoulder like a temple relic.

The Belgian dog tilted its head, watching.

But didn’t bark.

Didn’t move.

At the end of the pier, a red pennant snapped in the wind.

No one looked up.

No one asked.

Roy pulled a cigar from his coat and lit it with a match struck on his own boot.

“Back in civilization,” he muttered. “Thank God.”

Wu didn’t speak.

He just adjusted the strap on his pack.

And stepped forward into the line.

[End of Scene 28]








The depot square shimmered under the late sun—stone clean from wear, not care. Barrels of dried fish stacked like fortifications, carts laden with salt bricks, pigeons flitting from beam to beam like punctuation.

A brass bell rang somewhere above the telegraph office, but no one turned.

Li Wu followed the group past a tea stall and toward the shipping office when he saw it.

The booth stood just to the left of the customs gate. Modest, with red lacquer trim and a sign painted in English, French, and poorly-spelled Chinese:

MESSAGES – LYREBIRDS

Inside the booth sat four lyrebirds on open brass perches. Each bore a red pennant tied neatly to one leg. Their feathers shimmered—copper, smoke, ink—each tilt of the head catching different parts of the afternoon.

A man stood at the booth counter, speaking into a large conical horn.

“Tell her we’ve left the preserves at the post office,” he said. “We’ll collect them by dusk.”

The bird closest to the horn blinked.

Tilted.

Nodded once.

Then flew.

No one clapped.

No one stared.

The clerk adjusted a knob on the horn and gestured to the next in line.

Yvette stepped past the kiosk without glancing at it. Her fingers trailed against the edge of her notebook as if deciding whether to write. She didn’t.

Roy adjusted his collar and muttered, “Nearly done.”

The Belgian dog paused by the booth.

Looked up at the three remaining birds.

Then kept walking.

Wu lingered. Just a step.

One of the birds looked directly at him.

Its beak opened slightly.

But no sound came.

Then it turned.

And preened.

Wu moved on.

The red pennants fluttered gently in the warm breeze.

From the far side of the square, a pair of uniformed porters—one human, one not—hauled a crate marked FRAGILE – SPIRITS into the belly of the freight line.

Roy smiled.

“They’ve figured it out,” he said.

Yvette replied, “They never lost it.”

But no one said what it was.

[End of Scene 29]








Inside the customs hall, time had texture.

The air was dense with ink and tobacco, the ceiling fan unmoving and decorative. A clock chimed the hour, but its hands disagreed.

Li Wu stepped lightly across the worn tiles, clipboard in hand, guiding a crate of Yvette’s glass plates through inspection. Roy trailed behind, checking the shipment list against the manifest. Yvette was already negotiating a table near the window—better light, she said, for verifying film integrity.

The clerk—round-faced, thick-glassed, suspicious of everything but bribes—took their papers without comment.

Then the floor shifted.

Not physically.
Rhythmically.

Mice.

Not rats. Small. Sleek. Brown and gray and dappled. They emerged from beneath cabinets and behind desk legs in twos and threes, weaving through the customs hall like runners in an invisible race.

Each one bore something:
— a stamp stuck to its back
— a painted stripe
— a tiny brass ring

They ran in arcs and loops. None collided. None slowed.

One mouse passed over Wu’s foot with careful precision, adjusted course, and continued without pause.

No one said a word.

The customs clerk stamped the crate documentation and slid it back across the desk.

A second mouse delivered a folded note to a narrow chute behind the sorting counter.

The dog stood just outside the threshold, ears perked.

Watching.

But not chasing.

Yvette snapped one photograph—without flash. Then lowered her lens and sealed the plate. She didn’t title it.

Roy tapped the tip of his pencil against the side of his boot. “Just want this all packed and dry before we hit the river.”

The clerk nodded absentmindedly, then whistled.

Three mice paused.

Turned.

And disappeared beneath the west wall, single file.

Wu placed the paperwork in his tablet case and turned toward the exit.

Behind him, the clerk began typing on a manual machine with no ribbon.

The keys clicked anyway.

[End of Scene 30]








The loading yard shimmered with the rhythm of completion.

Crates lined the platform in rows too exact for accident—each stenciled, sealed, tin-lined, and marked with codes from three languages. Paper tags flapped like tongues in the wind, inked with weight, content, destination, and the occasional smudge of blood or tea.

Li Wu stood beside the largest shipment manifest of the expedition, a clipboard pressed to his palm, eyes scanning the last column.

Mammals: 2,146
Birds: 812
Insects: Count: lost in transit.
Reptiles: (still under dispute)
Plant Specimens: Approx. 3,000, preserved in folded wax paper.
Photographic Plates: 428 dry, 112 colorized, all sealed.
Motion Film: 10,000 ft, tin-spooled, waterproofed.

The dog lay beside the final stack, tail over paws, watching nothing and everything.

Roy oversaw the stevedores—half men, half something else—guiding them through the loading with the pleased fatigue of a man who’s carried his dream to the edge of reality.

“Label that one fragile,” he said, tapping a crate that weighed more than a man. “Let them figure out why.”

Yvette stood at a folding table, wrapping the final set of plates in oilskin, hands moving with the detachment of ritual. Her coat was off. Her sleeves were rolled. Her silence had changed: it was no longer observation. It was withdrawal.

Wu approached the customs officer with the manifest.

The man glanced at it once, then stamped it with a brass seal shaped like a plum blossom.

Wu paused.

The box at the end of the row—marked 34-G—had no label.

No number.

Just a glyph. Carved, not inked.

He had never seen it added.

Not on any packing list.

He hesitated.

Then stepped forward.

Signed the ledger.

And said nothing.

Behind him, Yvette looked up.

But only briefly.

Then she turned to her camera case.

The wind shifted. A stack of straw wrappers blew across the lot in perfect alignment—straight line, no arc.

The dog lifted its head.

Then lay back down.

Roy exhaled through his teeth and said, “That’s that.”

Wu folded the manifest in half.

And let the clipboard hang at his side.

[End of Scene 31]








One year, to the day.

They stepped through the southern gate of Teng-yueh under a sky as blank as before they left. Dust rose beneath their boots, familiar and unremarkable. The city had not changed, though the shadows now leaned differently.

Lanterns swayed from the same cracked poles. The same shopkeepers dozed in the same doorways. A child ran past with a wooden wheel, laughing like nothing in the world had teeth.

Roy let out a breath through his nose. “Back in one piece.”

Yvette offered a ghost of a smile. “If not in the same order.”

The Belgian dog walked beside Li Wu. It no longer bothered to check behind them.

The hotel at the city’s edge took them without a glance. Their names were still in the ledger, written in a different ink, under a different date.

Upstairs, electric lamps hung from the ceiling. Dead.
No one noticed. The kerosene ones worked fine.

Wu unpacked in silence. Not because there was nothing to say—but because he no longer knew which words still belonged to him.

Yvette stood at the window, notebook in hand, watching the river as if expecting it to rise.

Roy returned with news: the crates had been seen at the docks. The manifest had been received in Rangoon. The museum had wired something about a gala.

“Doesn’t feel finished,” he muttered.

“Nothing ever is,” Yvette said.

She tore a page from her book. Looked at it. Then crumpled it and let it fall.

Wu waited until they were asleep.

Then he walked.

No lantern.

No boots.

Just the soft press of foot on stone.

He passed the old tea house, the closed apothecary, the mission chapel that had once traded a dog.

At the city’s edge, near the first milestone marker, he stopped.

A telegraph pole leaned slightly to the right.

Atop it: a bird.

Not perched.

Balanced.

Filament tail curled around the wire. Feathers dull with travel. One leg raised, red pennant tied just below the knee.

It said nothing.

And neither did he.

Wu bowed his head once.

Turned.

And walked back into the city.

[End of Scene 32 | End of Act III]





Epilogue Scene – Not Listed in Manifest


The ship left port under a sky without clouds, the kind of sky that made Li Wu suspicious. Too clean. Too undecided.

He stood alone on the upper deck, away from the others. The museum men had boarded two hours earlier, shaking Roy’s hand with just enough pressure to remind him who paid the shipping bills. Yvette had vanished below decks with her notebooks, claiming seasickness. The dog was nowhere to be seen.

But Wu knew where it was.

Below.

With the crates.

In the hold.

He pulled the manifest from his coat—creased now, almost soft from folding. Ran his finger down the list until it reached the end.

Box 34-G.

No description.

No origin.

No destination.

Just the carved symbol.

It had no meaning in any dictionary. Not even in the older glossaries, the ones Wu had copied by hand when he was still a tutor’s son and thought truth was something you could archive.

But he had seen it now.

Etched into a milestone. Whispered in bird mimicry. Folded into goral bones and missorted butter tins.

He’d seen it in silence.

He’d seen it in return.

He replaced the paper.

Stepped to the railing.

Below, the sea hissed against the hull like breath.

Far behind them, somewhere inland, the jungle closed around an empty trail. Somewhere east, oxen plowed a field without orders. Somewhere north, a lyrebird said a thing no one heard and waited for no one to answer.

And in the dark, in the lowest hold of the ship, Box 34-G sat still.

But not silent.

Inside: a soft scratch.

Not urgent.

Not loud.

Just enough to suggest that something, somewhere, remembered.

And would continue to.
