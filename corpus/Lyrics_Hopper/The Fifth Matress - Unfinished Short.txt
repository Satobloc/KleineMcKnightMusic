

TITLE: The Fifth Mattress (unfinished story)
(c) 2005 by Shannon A. Rampe & Nathan McKnight



Fact: Five is the minimum number of mattresses required to survive in Antarctica.

I woke numb. My bones were cold steel rods, chilling me from the inside out. I thought for a moment that I was blind until I realized that my eyelids had frozen shut. 
I huddled in a tiny lean-to of mattresses. A few dozen meters behind me, my lifeboat bumped against the ice. I'd narrowly escaped the prison ship, the Carnival Sunshine, but in the end, I'd reached my destination. Antarctica.
I shifted one of the mattresses. The sky was an inky pool. An animal nudged its head into my cubby and I drew back, startled. It was a penguin.
“Hey, Bub. You need another one,” it said, speaking with a thick Brooklyn accent.
“Wh- what?” My lips were numb, my tongue half-frozen. 
“You need another mattress,” repeated the penguin. “Four won’t do. They’ll keep out the wind, but you need something to insulate you from the ice. By the look of it, that coat ain’t gonna do the trick. If you want to last, you’ll need to find one more mattress.”
“Leave me alone,” I said. “I’m perfectly capable on my own.”
The penguin shrugged, raising its flippers as if to say suit yourself. “Don’t lay down on the ice again or you won’t wake up. You gotta keep moving until you find the fifth mattress.”
I liked animals, but I wasn’t accustomed to accepting advice from them. Still, the penguin was right. Antarctica was my refuge, my new home. If I intended to survive and thrive, I needed a properly constructed and insulated fortification.
A crusty foot nudged my forehead. I hadn’t realized that I’d shut my eyes again. 
“Hey, human. You should see this.”
I took a long, icy breath. “See what?” I said at last.
He kicked me hard this time. “Behold!”
I sat up, rubbing my forehead, and the mattresses fell away.
There was light on the horizon. Not the faint pink hues of dawn. The Sun wouldn’t return for months. This was a ghostly green swamplight, waves shimmering across the sky like the sea of the gods lapping up against the shores of heaven.
“Aurora Australis.” I stared, mesmerized.
The penguin gestured behind me. “Looks a little like your mattress.”
My mattress. My mattress.
It was dirty, stained with green and pink and yellow magic marker.

As long as Will could remember, Luke had tormented him. 
His earliest memory was of coloring with markers. Will had a coloring book, each perforated page with the outline of an animal. There were walruses, penguins, sea lions, fish, tortoises, gulls. It was his favorite coloring book. He couldn’t spell their names yet, but he could color them. He loved to color, and Mom had given him a brand new set of markers for his fourth birthday. 
He loved animals. And he loved to color.
Will removed each page from the book, being careful not to tear them or otherwise damage the pictures. Then he spent hours in his room, painstakingly filling in each picture, never straying outside the lines, always selecting the nicest colors—the honey yellow, the sea foam green, the astral blue. As he finished each one, he wrote his name at the bottom, a difficult act but one which made him proud. He was smart.
Rain battered the windows, darkening the sky and frightening Will, but as long as he focused on the animals, he could almost imagine that he was outside, in the sunshine, playing with them. He clenched his jaws and focused on each shape and line, shutting out the rest of the world.
By the time he completed the pictures, the rain had eased up. He had colored every picture in the book. He ran into the low-ceilinged living room where Mom was smoking cigarettes and watching game shows. “Look, Mom! Come see what I did.”
There was an uneasy look in her eyes. “What did you do, Will?”
Will’s smile faltered. Was Mom scared? “I made pictures. Come see.”
She followed him down the hallway. The rain and thunder had come back, letting the darkness creep in through the windows. 
He pushed open the door to his room, trying to recall the joy and excitement he’d felt just moments before. He froze.
His bedroom was a scene of carnage. Luke had destroyed everything. Each perfect page had been scribbled on with black markers, ruining Will’s careful lines. In giant red strokes, Luke had scrawled his name over each page and then torn them in half. He’d even written his name up the walls and onto the ceiling. Luke. Luke. Luke. 
“Oh, Jesus protect us,” whispered Mom. “What did you do, Will?”
“Luke did it,” Will said. He began to cry.
Mom put him in bed and ordered him to stay there. She fled the room, closing the door behind her. Will was left crying in the dark with the rain shaking the windows. And Luke was there, hiding under the bed, laughing. 
Will pulled the sheets up over his head and clutched his markers close. He cried until his tears ran down into the markers and ink got all over him and all over his sheets. The ink would soak through and leave the mattress stained with blots of bright color. 
After that, mom told Will he was to old for coloring and gave him Grampa Benny’s old books to read instead...Darwin, Wallace, Shackelton.

The cheese plate was replete with stink as the other prisoners danced slowly around the heaving seaborne ballroom and the guards jabbed them with cattle prods. 
Somehow, Luke had found me. He was on the ship. He was probably in this room. I pulled the hood of my coat a little further down over my face and leaned forward over my dinner plate. The other prisoners at my table were whispering. Idiots. Didn’t they know he would hear?
“Where are you, Will?” Luke’s voice, two tables over. “We haven’t finished our game, brother!”
I panicked. Luke’s games involved knives and pliers.
I pushed out my chair crawled beneath the dinner table.
“What’s the matter with you?” said a prisoner whose nametag said Walter. “Someone call security!”
Two of the prisoners at my table screamed. Luke might already be removing their eyes with a screwdriver. But I was safe for the moment. Hidden.
Legs scrambled away as the other prisoners fled the table. “Don’t go! He’ll know something is wrong. Don’t let him notice you!”
One of the guards pushed his head under the table. “Mister Turner? Would you come with me?”
“Get away! Run! Luke is coming!”
“Mister Turner, please!”
I kicked him in the face and he cried out and fell back. A moment later, another pair of guards appeared and threw back the tablecloth. Each of them grabbed one of my legs and dragged me from my hiding place. “No!”
“Take him to his room and lock him there,” said the first guard, dabbing a napkin to a bloody lip. “I’ll alert the captain.”
Look stood behind him, grinning manically. He pointed at the guard and drew a finger across his neck. 
“Luke will get you!” I screamed. “Look out behind you!”
But the guards were already dragging my back to my cell. This time, they wouldn’t forget to lock it. I’d be trapped on the prison ship with Luke forever. Fortunately, my escape vessel was already prepared. 
Once we were in the hallway, I elbowed one guard in the stomach and shoved the other one down a staircase. When I knew I’d lost them, I turned down a corridor and ran til I saw a bulkhead. I yanked open the latch, and a cold wind blew into my face. I pushed aside slabs of hanging meat and jumped ship, the bulkhead slamming behind me, and I landed on all fours in the cold Antarctic night.

Fact: The emperor penguin has no natural predators.

“What’s your name?” the penguin asked.
“William Turner,” I said, not certain that I wanted to be on a first name basis with this bird. It wouldn’t leave me alone. But it had been right when he told me the movement would keep me warm. With a piece of nylon webbing wrapped around my waist, I dragged the mattresses up the ice shelf, away from the roar of the ocean. What other secrets of the ice might this animal know?
“Will.” The penguin repeated. “My name’s Benny.”
I grunted and shifted my direction away from the penguin.
“Something wrong?” He sounded offended.
“No.”
“Then what’s your problem?”
“First of all, I said my name was William, not Will. Second, my grandfather’s name was Benny. Grampa Benny.”
“It’s a good name,” the penguin said. He even sounded like Grampa Benny, with that accent. 
“Why’d you come here, Will?”
“I moved here. I want to live here.”
“You moved to Antarctica?” The bird made a cawing sound that sounded suspiciously like laughter. “Why?”
I didn’t want to admit the truth. I was afraid if I mentioned his name that he might hear me, even though I knew he was trapped on the prison ship.
“I came here to...study birds,” I lied. “I’m a scientist.”
“Lucky you found me, then.”
“I guess.” I wasn’t so sure.
The wind blew, carrying clouds of powdery diamond dust that flickered in the aurora. A low bellow came from nearby.
“What was that?” I said.
The ice shivered beneath us. Something was coming. Something big. 
A shape emerged from the flurries of dust and darkness: enormous yellowed tusks, whiskers like tines of a wire brush, and eyes dripping with bloodlust. 
“Walrus!” I screamed. “Run!” I fought to free myself from the mattresses, but the webbing was fastened securely, and I was restrained to a herky-jerky shuffle. 
“What are you talking about, Will? Don’t you know that walruses are native to the arctic? I thought you said you were a scientist.”
Suddenly I was grasping at snow rapidly dissolving into open air as a crevasse opened beneath my feet and I plummeted into an icy crack. I felt my body jolt at the waist, and I hung limp like a deer on a hood. 
Above me, the mattresses were caught in the mouth of the crevasse. I swung below, suspended at the mercy of the webbing. From above came the panicked shrieking of a penguin followed by the sound of crunching bones.
A shape appeared at the lip of the crevasse, its blubbery form blocking out the starlight. Its breath made little clouds of flecked steam. Penguin blood ran down its tusks and dripped on the mattress hanging directly above me, soaking into the cotton.
I squeezed my eyes shut and waited to die.

Will loved spending time outside. He would spend hours wandering in the woods behind their trailer, looking at bugs beneath rotten logs, hunting for the largest toadstools, and trying to catch squirrels. In the forest, Luke left him alone.
Mom didn’t like Will to spend too much time outside. She was convinced that he was sick, even though he felt fine. She said that keeping inside would prevent him from getting worse. But when he was trapped indoors, Luke taunted him mercilessly.
When he was nine, Will convinced Mom to let him get a pet bird, a cockatiel he named Mister Chumpy. When Will was alone with him, Mister Chumpy preened his white feathers and squawked happily.
They also had a dog, a Rottweiler named Loki. It was Luke’s dog, even though Mom said she had gotten it for Will. Loki was mean. Anytime he appeared in Will’s room, Mister Chumpy went berserk in his cage.
On a Saturday afternoon in September, Will was stuck inside with Mister Chumpy. Luke kept shaking Mister Chumpy’s cage whenever Will wasn’t looking, and Mister Chumpy was agitated.
“Stop it, Luke,” Will said. “Go away.”
“You should let him fly around. He wants out. That’s why he keeps making that racket. You’re torturing him, keeping him locked up in there.”
Will felt a twinge of guilt. He didn’t like it when Mom kept him cooped up. Mister Chumpy might feel the same way. 
He unlatched the cage. Mr. Chumpy fluttered about, but Will closed a hand around the little guy and lifted him out. 
Will heard Luke snickering and looked up. Luke was gone, but he’d left the door open behind him. Loki burst into the room with a sudden violence.
Mister Chumpy took flight, but Loki was in his element. He sprang into the air and caught Mister Chumpy in his jaws. The bird screamed and Loki thrashed his head back and forth. Bloody feathers danced in the air like snowflakes.
Will stepped towards them, but Loki growled at him. Will started to shake and he felt his bladder give way. He screamed. 
Somewhere in the trailer, Luke was laughing.
Loki grew bored with Mister Chumpy once the bird stopped struggling. It dropped the parrot’s body onto Will’s bed, leaving behind streaks of blood and saliva. The beast turned and trotted out of the room.
Will ran to Mister Chumpy and held him. He was mangled, his beautiful wings turned at wrong angles and his iridescent feathers streaked with blood. His tiny breast rose once, twice, then stilled.

Fact: Pills are just bullets in disguise.

I must have crawled up out of the crevasse and passed out, because when I came to, the aurorae had died down and there was no sign of the walrus except for the bloodstained mattress I huddled beneath.
I got to my feet, stamping around to get my circulation moving. My blood was turning to syrup in my heart. I couldn’t feel much below my knees, and I couldn’t see anything beyond a few feet. The darkness was almost complete.
I tried to set up my fortress again, but without the fifth mattress, I couldn’t completely shut out the cold. And without the penguin, I had no idea where to find the fifth mattress. Up and up, I told myself. The sound of my own voice, cracked and raw, startled me. I hadn’t realized I’d been speaking aloud.
Then another voice startled me. “Just keep going up, Will. Up, and up, and up.”
“Benny?” I strained my eyes, trying to penetrate the murk, but all I could see was ice and shadow. “Aren’t you dead?”
“Do I sound like I’m dead?”
“No. But you were eaten by a walrus. Where are you?”
“It doesn’t matter, Will. Keep climbing up.”
I shouldered the webbing and began to drag the mattresses further up the ice shelf. I was exhausted. The recent nap had left me more tired than before. I needed to find the fifth mattress, if only so I could rest.
“It’s beautiful out here, isn’t it?” Benny said.
I looked around. I couldn’t see much, but I knew it was beautiful. Stark and blue and empty of people. Empty of civilization. “Yeah.”
“Will you be lonely without other humans around?”
“No. It’s safer here.”
“Did you remember to bring your pills?” 
I stumbled on one of the mattresses and fell over. “No pills!” I cried. 
My face struck the mattress and found a hole there, one that fit my face almost perfectly.

The doctors gave Will lots of different pills. Mom told him that he had to take them or he would get sick again. But taking the pills made him feel not himself, like he was in the passenger seat of a car that someone else was driving. Sometimes he took them, but most of the time he threw them away.
Will began to understand that not everyone knew about Luke. Mom knew, of course. She had to know. She’d given birth to them. They were twins. But anytime Will mentioned Luke’s name, she panicked or flew into a rage. 
Other people didn’t know about Luke. He was a secret, and Luke got angry if Will tried to tell people about him. It was dangerous to get Luke upset. He did bad things. Other people got upset, too. Will learned to avoid talking about Luke at all.  
In school, Will tried his hardest to ignore Luke, who sat behind him in every class. Luke kicked the back of his chair and whispered all the mean, cruel things he wanted to do to the other kids. Sometime he made Will do them. Together, they stole things from their classmates--protractors and lunch money and teenage romance paperback novels. They lit matches and threw them in the trash can in the bathroom. They put centipedes and spiders in Lilly Walker’s backpack. They put a razorblade in Todd Sykes’ coat pocket. They poured toilet bowl cleaner in Mr. Gaines’ morning coffee in first period.
Will got expelled but Luke never got in trouble. Will understood they had done terrible things, but he tried to explain that it was Luke’s fault. No one had believed him. He had to go to a different school, but Luke came with him, taunting him mercilessly. Mom took him to more doctors. The doctors gave him more pills. 
Will started to take the pills, finally. Luke hated it. Luke wanted him to feed the pills to the neighbor’s dog. But Will took them, each morning. And Luke began leaving him alone during the day. 
School became easier. He finished high school, got a scholarship to a local college. For a while, he even managed to have a few friends. He studied biology. He read about the 19th century naturalists in Grampa Benny’s books and wished that he had lived in that adventurous time of discovery. Whenever he was out of class, he went on excursions into local parks. During the day, he was mostly happy. He even met a girl. Kate.
But the nights were the worst. Luke snuck into the dorms and slid into bed next to Will. Luke stroked his hair and whispered in his ear all the awful things he wanted to do with paring knives and fishhooks and drain cleaner. Will tried to ignore him, but it was hard to sleep. 
Night after night, Will buried his face in his mattress, smothering himself with the pillow over his head, trying to ignore Luke’s crooning voice. Luke sat on his back pushed his face deeper into the mattress until Will could hardly breathe. Will bit tiny holes in the casing, slowly tearing a space into the ticking that fit his face like a mask. And he survived.

Fact: Antarctica is a long way from Pennsylvania.

I shook myself out of my reverie. Benny was talking to me again, asking me more inane questions. He was a disembodied voice in the darkness, a ghost in the ice.
“Your answers are full of holes, Will.”
“I don’t owe you any answers.” I shook my head. “Why won’t you leave me alone?”
“Because without me, you wouldn’t find the fifth mattress, and then you’d probably die out here.”
“I’ll be fine,” I said, knowing the penguin was right. The truth was, I was less comfortable answering his questions than I was freezing to death.
“Does your family know that you moved here? Are they coming too?”
“It’s just me.”
“What about your wife?”
“I don’t have a wife,” I lied. 
“Kate,” Benny said. “Her name is Kate.”
“No!” Her name was acid in my ears.
“Say her name, Will. Say it!”
“Don’t call me Will! I told you, my name is William!”
“Everyone calls you Will.”
“William! Only Luke called me Will. And I hated it.”
“Fine, Will.”
It was too much. I dropped to my knees and pulled the mattresses up around me in the best semblance of a shelter I could manage. Almost instantly, my body temperature began to plummet. I felt relieved.
“Don’t quit now, Will. You’re almost there! The fifth mattress is close.”
“Leave me alone!”
“Come on, you baby!” It was Luke’s voice and Kate’s voice all in one. “Don’t be such a fucking coward.”
Rage boiled up inside me. There was a shadow at the edge of my shelter. I lunged for it. Benny was slick with oily shadows, but I was strong with hate. “Leave me alone! Goddammit, leave me alone!”
I shook the penguin and it screamed a woman’s scream. Kate’s scream. My frozen heart shattered into fragments.

The day that Will lost his job, Kate was waiting for him at home. She sat at the tiny kitchen table in their single bedroom apartment, her eyes bloodshot and her nails bit down to the quick. She looked terrible, and Will was nervous.
“Hi, Kate,” he said. He tossed his jacket over the back of the chair and avoided making eye contact as he opened the refrigerator. It was empty.
“What happened at work today.” It wasn’t a question. It was an accusation, flat and hard, like the head of a hammer. She knew.
“Nothing special,” he said. His hands began to shake.
“You got fired, Will. You got fired and we now have no income. I’m due in four weeks. What are we going to do?” She put a hand on her belly, swollen like an overripe melon.
She knew. Someone had told her. Will tried not to imagine who. But a name hovered there, lurking at the edge of his awareness like a nightcrawler. “It was a mistake. They made a mistake.”
“A mistake? Then you can go in tomorrow, apologize, and get your job back.” There was anger and panic in her voice.
Will said nothing. He already knew he wouldn’t go back. But it wasn’t his fault.
“You threw a pot of coffee on your boss! You hurled a computer through a plate glass window. They thought you were going to jump out the fucking window!”
“It wasn’t me!” he cried. “It was Luke!”
Kate blanched. “Jesus Christ. Not this again. I thought the new medicine was helping.” 
“It was poison. I flushed it away two weeks ago.” 
“You did what?” she shouted. Her lip quivered and she began to cry. “I’m pregnant and you’re sick! What the fuck am I supposed to do?”
“I flushed it!” he shouted. He was getting angry now, thinking back on the night. It was the first night he’d heard them together, doing it while he slept. He knew then that they were trying to poison him, to get rid of him. In their bed. On their mattress. Kate and Luke.
“Will, I want you to go to the hospital, right away.” She choked back her tears. “You’re scaring me. You need to get control of yourself so that we can take care of this baby. Please.” 
Kate reached out to him, and Will’s heart shuddered in his chest. He loved her so much. Ever since they’d met, five years ago, she’d taken care of him. She’d helped him. She’d trusted him. And he had trusted her. He’d given everything to her. And Luke had stopped coming around.
Until recently. Luke had come back. Or maybe he had been there all along.
“You’ve been together with him since the beginning, haven’t you?” Will asked, horrified by the sudden realization.
“What?”
“You and Luke. The two of you have been lovers this whole time. You hid it from me, but I know!”
“No!” she sobbed. “You’re sick! Please, stop it!”
Then the truly horrific realization struck him. The baby in Kate’s womb. Not his. Luke’s. 
“How could you!” he shrieked. “I loved you, you traitorous slut!” 
Rage overwhelmed him. He heaved the table aside and grabbed her by the arms.
She screamed, the terrified cries of the guilty. Will shook her. “It was his all along! I knew it!”
Kate kicked him in the shin and he released her. She scrambled away and cowered behind the kitchen island. She grabbed a gun from the kitchen counter. “Stay away from me—I love you, but you need to step back,” she shouted. Her voice and the gun wavered. “Just take these,” she said, pointing the gun in his face. 
Will was overcome with rage. But there was someone else here, too. Luke. Standing beside her. Standing in front of her. I grabbed the gun from her trembling hands and shoved it in my back pocket with a plasticky clatter.
“Come here, Will. Let’s play a game!” taunted Luke.
“No,” Will said. “I’m done with you, with all of you. I’m leaving.”
“Stop it, Will! Just calm down. Open the pill bottle, you can do it hon.” I ignored them and put my hand on the doorknob. “Where will you go?” It was both of their voices, Luke and Kate, together.
“Far away! Somewhere you’ll never find me again!” Antarctica, Will thought. Luke would never find him there.
He ran to the closet and grabbed his winter coat. In the kitchenette, Kate was sobbing into her cell phone. Luke, for once, was blessedly silent. Kate saw him and fell silent, staring at him in the fur-lined hood. 
Will stepped out onto the back porch and immediately began to sweat in the July sun. He began to walk. Down the street, around the corner, and out of the block of squat apartments. Antarctica was a long way away. Now, where am I going to find tickets? he thought.

Fact: Antarctica is fleeting, fickle.

Benny was gone. He’d been gone for, I didn’t know how long. It was good. If Benny wasn’t here, he couldn’t pester me with questions. Questions I didn’t want to think about, didn’t dare answer.
There was only the freezing wind and the darkness and the strain on my back as I dragged the mattresses further and further up the ice shelf. I might have been here for days or years. It was impossible to tell. Without the sun to mark the days, time had ceased to have meaning. I was not afraid. I was home.
It was while pondering this sense of timelessness that I realized something was different. Off to my right was a faint smudge of light on the horizon. I had barely noticed the subtle shift. It was dawn in Antarctica.
And in that faint twilight, I saw the crest of the ice shelf. There was something there. The fifth mattress.
I began to run, huffing as the mattresses and the wind dragged upon me.
Yes! There it was. A mattress, faintly crusted with ice, but otherwise undamaged. I heaved myself forward.
There was something beneath the mattress, propping it up. I paused. The mattress shifted. There was a snuffling sound.
“Benny?” 
The mattress shifted again, as if whatever was beneath it was struggling to escape but was too weak. Finally I grabbed the mattress and hauled it over.
Huddled on the ice, shivering, was a little boy in a tuxedo. Frozen snot crusted his upper lip. It was Luke. 
I was horrified. Somehow, even in Antarctica, the most remote corner of the planet I could reach, he had found me.
His skin was the same blue hue as the ice. He was almost transparent. “Help me, Will.” His voice was small but familiar, the same voice I’d been hearing over my shoulder my entire life.
I was frozen. But I had the fifth mattress. 
“How did you find me?” I said.
He looked puzzled. “Find you?”
Anger snowed down upon me. “Don’t play dumb with me!”
“It’s night still, Will. I’m night.”
“It’s dawn!” I shouted. “It’s dawn and I’m day.”
I drew the mattresses together into a fort: one on the ground, insulating us from the ice, one leaning against the other, forming a pup tent, and one on either end to block out the crosswinds. I lifted Luke, who weighed no more than a snowflake, and placed him inside the crude shelter. Then I climbed in myself, pulling the mattress up to seal us in. We were finally warm.
“Thank you,” Luke said. “You’re good. You’ll keep me safe.”
I took Luke’s head and pressed his face into the mattress. 
“Will!” he shrieked, “Wwwwww!”
But I didn’t stop. I kept pressing, harder and harder. I climbed atop him, smothering him, pushing him down with all the love I could muster. He was fading, thinning, seeping like oil into the fifth mattress. 
“I’m day!” I yelled, “I’m day. I am day. I am day! I am day!”
I shouted and pushed with a all my might until finally, he was gone, and I sat alone. The wind howled outside, but inside my fortress, insulated by my five mattresses.
I was safe, almost comfortable except for something poking into my buttocks. I grasped at my back pocket, hand shaking.
	In the crisp of the Antarctic air, the gun clattered against the ice like a plastic rattle. With relief, I hefted it, cocked it, put the trigger to my temple. 
I pulled the trigger. But the mechanism was frozen, so I took out the bullets and swallowed them one by one. Better they kill me slowly, I thought.
