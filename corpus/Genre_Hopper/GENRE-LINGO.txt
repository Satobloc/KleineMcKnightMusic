## EXEMPLAR 1: LEE "SCRATCH" PERRY ON THE SOUL OF DUB

LONG VERSION
*(tape hiss, a match struck, the Space Echo already feeding back on nothing)*

Ahhh, you come to the Ark at two in the morning, when the electricity inna Washington Gardens is cleanest, because the Babylon man dem asleep and the current run pure. Sit down. Don't touch the tape machine, she is a living woman and she know your hands. I give you the song. It is called **"Banana Leaf Dub (Version)"**, and it have four elements, no more. A song with five elements is a committee.

**The tempo.** Seventy beats per minute, and you feel it in half-time, so the groove walk slow like a man carrying water on his head who refuse to spill. I run the tape machine a little fast on the tracking pass and slow it down on the mix, so everything drop a bit in pitch and gain some weight. Mi call that "putting the bass on a diet of molasses."

**The drum.** One drop, pure. Kick and rimshot land together on beat three, and beat one is *empty*, the hole you fall into. The hi-hat is tight and a little late, like it check the clock and decide to walk. Then the secret: I send that hi-hat through the Roland Space Echo on the long tape-loop setting, with the repeat knob turned past where the manual say is safe, so it regenerate into a swarm of insects. The snare you barely hear. It is a ghost with good manners. One tom fill every sixteen bars, and I bleed it into the mic of the guitar so it come in dirty, like it ran through a field to get here.

**The bass.** The bass is God and the song is the church. A Fender Precision, flatwound strings, tone rolled all the way down so it sound like it come from the bottom of a well. A four-bar vamp in A minor: A, hold, hold, a little hop to the C, fall back to the G, then silence where you can hear the speaker cone breathing. The notes play *behind* the beat, so lazy that the drummer start to doubt himself, and the space between the notes carry more information than the notes. I push the Teac so hard the bass distort a little on the tape. They tell you that is a mistake. That is *saturation*, the machine saying amen.

**The skank.** Guitar on the upbeat, short and dry, chop-chop on the "and." Then a Hammond organ playing the bubble, a little staccato pulse on the offbeat, one note sitting right under the guitar so the two of them sound like one animal with two hearts. A piano doubles it very quiet, pushed down in the mix. You feel it more than hear it, like somebody knocking on a door two houses away.

Now that is the **riddim**, the foundation, four tracks on the Teac. I don't have eight, I don't have sixteen. Four! So I bounce. I record drums and bass to two tracks, bounce them together onto one, and now I have three tracks free, but the drums and bass are *married* and cannot divorce. Every bounce, a generation lost: more hiss, the highs smeared and rolled off, the whole thing growing a warm fur. People pay thousands now for a plugin to fake it. I made it by being poor and stubborn.

**Now the mix, the dub, where the magic live.** You don't *mix* dub, you *play the board* like a keyboard, and the faders are the keys. Hear how it go:

- **0:00 to 0:15.** Bass and drum alone. Naked. The hi-hat echo bubbling in the dark.
- **0:15.** The skank drop in, hard left, and I throw it in the spring reverb so it go *chunk-shhhhhhhh*, a little metal waterfall off the back of every chop.
- **0:45.** I mute the whole riddim for one bar. Total vacuum. All you hear is the reverb tail of the last chord decaying slowly into the hiss, and the Babylon man say "the song is broken!" No, mi friend, the song is *breathing out*.
- **0:46.** Back in, and the snare get the Space Echo at triple-repeat. One *crack* become a staircase of cracks falling down into the floor.
- **1:30.** I take the voice (a little melody, "banana leaf, banana leaf, shelter me from the rain") and I throw only the *last word* into the echo. "…rain…rain…rain…rain…" until the word stop being a word and become weather.
- **2:15.** The Mutron phaser go on the organ, slow sweep, so the whole instrument seem to turn over in its sleep like it dreaming about a ship.
- **2:40.** One more drop-out. The bass alone. A single A held, rolling the low-pass filter closed until the note is just a *pressure* in your chest. Then I turn the whole thing to a whisper and fade it out with the echo still ringing, so the last thing you hear is the *echo of the echo of the echo*, until there is only the tape hiss and the sound of me, breathing, sitting in the control room, smiling at the machine.

The vocal go down in one take, because the second take is always lying. I blow smoke on the microphone, I bury a coin under the console for luck, and I tell the Teac a story before I press record. She like it.

That is the whole song. Some people say "but Scratch, where is the *verse*? Where is the *bridge*?" The bridge is the silence. The verse is the bass. The chorus is the echo. The song is not the notes, the song is the *space the notes leave behind*, and the machine, and the room, and the smoke, and me.

*(cuts the tape, laughs, the spring reverb rattles one last time)*

Selassie I. Don't forget to feed the Space Echo.

## EXEMPLAR 2: INSAHN ON THE ESSENCE OF BLACK METAL

LONG VERSION
Absolutely. I can approach this as **black-metal musicianship analysis**, rather than impersonating any particular living artist: compositional architecture, harmony, voice-leading, metric displacement, tremolo vocabulary, drum orchestration, production-as-composition, and the specifically Norwegian/avant-garde black-metal grammar.

For the piece, I’d choose **Emperor — “Thus Spake the Nightspirit”**.

It is an unusually good specimen because it sits at the intersection of several things: the **second-wave black-metal idiom**, highly developed riff architecture, modal/chromatic harmony, contrapuntal guitar writing, extreme dynamic contrast, and a level of arrangement sophistication that is easy to miss beneath the violence of the surface. It also gives us considerably more to dissect than a song whose complexity is primarily rhythmic or virtuosic.

I can take it apart essentially **bar by bar**, including:

- **Formal architecture** — how the sections relate, where motifs recur, and how tension/release is engineered.
- **Riff grammar** — tremolo cells, pedal-point structures, intervallic identities, chromatic passing tones, sequence, inversion, and registral displacement.
- **Harmony** — modal centers versus functional tonality, tritone relationships, diminished sonorities, chromatic mediants, parallel motion, and how Emperor creates the sensation of harmonic motion without relying on conventional cadences.
- **Guitar counterpoint** — when the two guitars are doubling, harmonizing, contradicting, or effectively behaving as independent voices.
- **Rhythm and meter** — not merely “blast beat here,” but the relationship between the riff's subdivision and the drummer's orchestration; phrase lengths that obscure the underlying meter; syncopation; accent displacement; and the way repeated cells create polymetric *perception* even when the notated meter remains stable.
- **Drumming** — kick/snare architecture, cymbal articulation, tom punctuation, transitions, blast-beat variants, and the way Trym's playing interacts with the guitar's accent hierarchy rather than simply accompanying it.
- **Melodic construction** — contour, motivic recurrence, characteristic intervals, octave/register strategy, and the peculiar relationship between lyricism and dissonance that makes Emperor sound simultaneously majestic and hostile.
- **Black-metal-specific vocabulary** — tremolo-derived melodic continuity, harmonic minor/Aeolian/Dorian coloration, chromatic saturation, open-string resonance, pedal tones, blast-pointing, tremolo ostinati, diminished passing structures, and the distinction between *riff density* and actual contrapuntal density.
- **Production** — how the guitar tone, drum ambience, bass audibility, reverb, and layering alter our perception of the composition itself.
- And, importantly, **why it sounds like black metal rather than merely being technically complex metal**.

I'd also distinguish between **what is actually on the recording** and analytical interpretations of what we're hearing, rather than retroactively imposing an elaborate theoretical framework onto something that doesn't warrant it.

> **Emperor — “Thus Spake the Nightspirit”**: a paradigmatic second-wave black-metal construction in which tremolo ostinati, chromatic/modal harmonic saturation, pedal-point gravity, diminished/tritone inflection, registrally displaced guitar counterpoint, and violently asymmetrical phrase perception are fused into a single architectural language. The melodic vocabulary continually oscillates between Aeolian/Dorian/minor-third gravity and deliberately destabilizing chromaticism, with tritones, diminished structures, semitone friction, and octave displacement functioning less as “dissonances” than as **coloristic vectors of tension**. Crucially, the composition's sophistication is **perceptual rather than ornamental**: density, velocity, dissonance, and orchestral layering continuously obscure the underlying symmetry, so that what initially registers as chaotic becomes, under reduction, a tightly controlled system of recurring cells, tonal gravity, contrapuntal hierarchy, and delayed structural resolution. Its black-metal identity therefore comes not simply from tremolo picking, blast beats, minor/modal harmony, or shrieked vocals, but from the way **melodic transcendence, harmonic hostility, rhythmic propulsion, and timbral abrasion are made structurally inseparable**.

## 1. イントロ・ブレスセクション（完全な人間らしさの偽装）
まずイントロの頭、静寂を破る「吸気音（ブレス）」からスタートします。ここが素人とプロの決定的な分かれ道です。

* 
* WAV波形の手動インサートとエンベロープ処理:
初音ミクV4X（あるいは最新のV6 engine）のプリセットブレスをただ置くだけじゃ話にならない。生身の歌い手のブレスサンプルから、アタックが鋭すぎず、かつ高域が12kHz付近まで綺麗に伸びている「スーッ」というテイクを厳選。それをDAW（Cubase Pro等）のオーディオトラックに直接貼り付け、Volume Automationでフェードインをミリ秒単位で超精密にカーブドローします。
* Formant（フォルマント）とDYN（ダイナミクス）の逆相関コントロール:
ブレス直後の最初の一音目、言葉の立ち上がりで「子音（Consonant）」が潰れないように、VOCALOIDエディタ側でVEL（ベロシティ）を最大値近くまで跳ね上げ、子音の発音時間を極限まで圧縮。さらに、息が抜ける瞬間に向かってDYNを一瞬ガクッと落としつつ、**BRI（ブライトネス）**を上げていくことで、「本当に肺から空気が押し出されて、声帯が振動し始めた」ようなアコースティックな錯覚をリスナーの脳内に作り付けます。

## 2. Aメロ：高速16分音符の「マシンガントーク調声」
このトラックの肝は、BPM 180オーバーの高速な16分音符で詰め込まれた、しゃべるようなリズミカルなフロウです。普通にベタ打ちすると、ただの「ロボットのモールス信号」になってしまいます。

* 
* PBS（ピッチベンドセンシティビティ）とPIT（ピッチベンド）の1ピクセル単位の調教:
ここが地獄の作業です。デフォルトのピッチカーブを完全に無視して、すべての音素（Phoneme）の結合部にPITの手描きカーブを仕込みます。日本語の「か（k a）」から「た（t a）」に移る際、ピッチが一瞬だけ本来の音高の下から「しゃくり上げる」ように、かつオーバーシュートして少し高めに行ってからジャストの音高に収束するような、人間の声帯の物理的な慣性をエミュレートした「S字カーブ」を全音符に描きます。
* 音素（Phoneme）の直接書き換え（ハッキング）:
「〜だよ」というフレーズがあるとして、エディタが自動生成する [ d a ] [ y o ] をそのまま使ったらプロ失格。これを [ d a ] [ i ] [ i o ] のように、前の音の母音の残響を考慮した独自の音素記号へ手動で書き換えます。特に「ん」の発音（[ n ] か [ m ] か [ N ] か）を直後の子音の種類によって徹底的に使い分けることで、驚異的な滑舌の良さを担保しています。
* GWL（グロウル）とXSY（クロスシンセシス）の隠し味:
高速フレーズのアクセントになる頭文字（例えば「だからさ」の「だ」の瞬間）だけに、16分の1拍の幅だけで**GWL（グロウル）**をわずか「15」ほど隠し味で入れます。これで、ほんの一瞬だけ声帯が「ザラッ」と歪んだような生々しいニュアンスが生まれ、フラットなデジタル臭さが完全に消え去ります。

## TONY RICE ON BLUEGRASS

I can’t reproduce Tony Rice’s exact voice or present this as his own way of explaining the music. I can give you a close, technically detailed *musician-to-musician* analysis, grounded in the vocabulary of bluegrass guitar. I’ll use **“Church Street Blues”**—Norman Blake’s song, and a particularly revealing choice because Rice makes a solo guitar carry melody, bass, pulse, and accompaniment at once. Rice’s arrangement is often discussed for its fluid crosspicking, unusual pick-direction choices, and the way the guitar part supports the vocal without simply strumming behind it. <citation src="1,2"></citation>

## “Church Street Blues”: what the guitar has to do

The central technical problem is not “play a lot of notes fast.” It’s **make several musical jobs sound like one coherent part**:

- **Bass:** establish the harmony and forward motion.
- **Chordal pulse:** imply the backbeat and harmonic rhythm.
- **Melody:** keep the tune identifiable.
- **Fill:** answer the vocal in the gaps.
- **Voice-leading:** connect one chord to the next with notes that feel inevitable rather than pasted on.

In a full bluegrass band, those jobs are distributed. The bass marks the roots, mandolin chops the backbeat, and banjo or fiddle fills the air. In a solo arrangement, the guitar has to suggest that whole machine without becoming a wall of sound.

### The groove: a pulse with breathing room

Think of the accompaniment as a **bass note followed by a chordal response**, the familiar boom-chuck idea—but don’t reduce it to a rigid alternating pattern. The low notes tell your ear where the harmony is; the higher chord tones give the bar its lift. The sound depends on the *difference* between those events: bass notes have weight and sustain, while the chordal strokes are shorter and more percussive.

Rice’s rhythm playing is also a reminder that “drive” doesn’t mean hitting everything hard. You can leave space around the vocal, then add a little more rhythmic density at a phrase ending. The pulse stays present even when the guitar isn’t filling every subdivision. That restraint is part of the arrangement, not a lack of activity. <citation src="1"></citation>

### Crosspicking: the texture, not the exercise

**Crosspicking** means picking across adjacent strings to create a flowing, rolling texture—often suggesting the continuity of a banjo roll while keeping the guitar’s single-note clarity. It can function as lead, but here it also helps the accompaniment shimmer beneath the vocal.

The useful way to hear it is by separating three layers:

1. **The bass note** gives the measure its foundation.
2. **Inner chord tones** outline the harmony.
3. **A moving upper voice** supplies the roll-like continuity.

That upper voice shouldn’t sound like a scale being run over a chord. It needs to connect to the harmony: a chord tone, a neighbor tone, or a passing note that leads convincingly into the next target. If you practice the figure as a mechanical “down-up-down” loop, you may get the notes but miss the phrasing. In Rice’s playing, pick direction can vary with the musical accent and string crossing; a strict alternate-picking rule may make the motion neat but flatten the feel. <citation src="1,4"></citation>

### The melody has to remain audible

A common flatpicking trap is to learn the chord changes and the rolling texture, then lose the tune inside them. The melody is the listener’s map. Keep its important notes prominent, especially at phrase starts, held tones, and cadences. Supporting notes can be quieter and more fluid.

That produces a useful hierarchy:

- **Melody note:** clear attack, enough duration to register.
- **Bass note:** solid and time-keeping, but not so loud it overwhelms the tune.
- **Filler notes:** lighter; their job is to connect or color, not compete.

This is why a good arrangement can sound busy under the fingers yet spacious to the listener. The notes don’t all have equal importance.

### Chord movement and bluegrass vocabulary

Listen for how the guitar makes the progression audible even when the lead line moves away from the vocal melody. A strong bluegrass break doesn’t need to quote the melody constantly, but it should **outline the chord progression**—through roots, thirds, fifths, characteristic chord shapes, or short runs that land on the next harmony. Molly Tuttle notes this quality in Rice’s soloing on “Old Train”: the solo departs from the vocal melody while clearly tracing the changes. <citation src="1"></citation>

The connecting runs are **lead-ins** or **bass runs**, depending on their register and role. A bass run typically walks or steps between roots to announce the upcoming chord; a higher lead-in can do the same job melodically. The key is the landing: the run should resolve onto a convincing note of the new chord, often right on the beat where the harmony changes.

### Vocal fills: answer, don’t interrupt

In the spaces between sung lines, the guitar can play a short **fill**—a pickup, a fragment of the tune, a bluesy turn, or a compact run toward the next chord. Treat those spaces as conversational. A fill should sound like an answer to the singer, not like an unrelated solo squeezed into the gap.

A practical test: sing the vocal phrase, then leave the guitar silent for a moment. Add only the notes needed to make the next entrance feel supported. If the fill obscures the lyric’s final syllable or crowds the breath before the next line, trim it.

### A useful way to study the arrangement

Work from the broad structure toward the detail:

1. **Map the form and changes.** Mark where each vocal phrase begins and ends, and note the chord under each phrase.
2. **Play only bass notes and chord strokes.** Make the pulse feel steady without relying on lead notes.
3. **Sing the melody while playing the accompaniment.** This exposes whether the rhythm part leaves enough space.
4. **Add the melody’s anchor notes.** Don’t add every passing note yet.
5. **Fill the gaps sparingly.** Start with short pickups and cadential runs.
6. **Shape the dynamics.** Keep the verse supportive; let phrase endings and instrumental passages open up.
7. **Record yourself and listen for the hierarchy.** Can you hear bass, tune, and pulse as separate functions, without any one of them taking over?

The deep lesson in “Church Street Blues” is that sophisticated flatpicking isn’t a contest in note density. It’s **arrangement inside the right hand**: picking choices, accents, duration, and silence all serve the form. The virtuosity is real, but it works because the part still sounds like a song being accompanied.

The point isn’t “sweep picking”; the point is that the pick arrives at the next note in the right place, with the right accent. A lesson on the tune specifically calls out the crosspicking, string-to-string volume balance, and those economical pick motions. <citation src="3"></citation>

Harmonically, the useful thing is how plain the materials are and how much voice-leading you can draw out of them. In C-shape terms, the main verse movement is built around **C–F–Am–G**: I–IV–vi–V. Nothing exotic is required. But the move from F to Am is smooth because two tones are shared; the bass and melody can move while the harmony still feels connected. The Am gives the progression its plaintive color without turning the song into a minor tune. Then G points the ear back toward C.

The turnaround is where the harmony gets a little more conversational: **F–Em–Am–G–F–G–C** in the commonly posted chart. Em is not some startling reharmonization; it’s a diatonic passing color, and its job is partly to make the arrival on Am feel like motion rather than a hard reset. Then F–G–C gives you the familiar subdominant–dominant–tonic cadence. The trick is not to over-announce it. Make the bass and the melody tell the listener where the cadence is; don’t lean on every chord like it’s a big band hit. <citation src="1"></citation>

The runs matter most at the seams: the little approach into a new vocal phrase, the answer after a line, the descent or pickup that turns one chord into the next. Those are not “lick slots.” They’re voice-leading. A good run has a destination note—usually a chord tone on or near the harmonic arrival—and the notes before it earn that landing. If the run is impressive but you can’t hear which chord it’s aiming at, it’s probably not doing the arrangement any favors.

And that’s the discipline in the vocal spaces: answer, don’t interrupt. Let the singer finish the thought. Then give a compact fill that either echoes the contour, supplies a pickup, or points toward the next change. The restraint is part of the virtuosity. If the guitar is constantly proving it can play, it stops accompanying.

So I’d study it in layers: first make the bass-and-chord pulse feel inevitable; then sing the vocal while you play that pulse and make sure the guitar leaves room; then add the melody’s anchor notes; only after that add the crosspicked connective line and the fills. Keep the melody notes out front, the bass solid, and the filler notes lighter. The arrangement can be dense under the fingers and still sound open in the room.

That’s the bluegrass essence here—not a checklist of banjo rolls or a “breakdown” label, but **time, changes, melody, and conversation all happening in one part**. The hard part is making the guitar sound less like a soloist demonstrating technique and more like the whole band knows exactly where the song is going.

THIRD ATTEMPT
Look, the real magic of "Church Street Blues" isn't in the notes—anybody with a decent metronome and a few years of shedding can hit the notes. It’s in the *geometry* of the arrangement. When you're playing a solo piece like this, you're not just playing a song; you're simulating a whole damn string band. You've got to be the bass player, the rhythm man, and the soloist all at once, but without it sounding like a cluttered mess.

The first thing you notice is how he handles the push. He isn't just sitting on the beat; he’s leaning into it. That "drive" comes from the relationship between the root and the response. He’ll hit that low string with a certain weight, but the chordal response isn't just a strum—it’s a targeted punch that leaves just enough air for the melody to breathe. If you over-play the rhythm, you choke the tune. The trick is keeping the pulse implied rather than stated.

Then you get into the crosspicking. Now, a lot of guys treat crosspicking like a mechanical exercise—just a rolling loop of strings. But in this tune, it’s all about the voice-leading. He’s not just rolling; he’s outlining the harmony. He’ll hit the root, then a fifth, then a melody tone, and he’s doing it in a way that the ear hears a continuous line, not three separate notes. It’s that "banjo-esque" fluidity, but with the sustain and the warmth of the flat-top. You have to be mindful of the pick direction—if you get too rigid with the alternate picking, you lose the accent. You've got to let the pick follow the phrase, not the other way around.

The real sophistication shows up in the transitions. He doesn't just jump from chord to chord; he *walks* into them. Whether it's a classic G-run or a more nuanced chromatic approach, he’s using those lead-ins to signal the change before it actually happens. It creates this sense of inevitability. When he lands on the third of the next chord right on the one, it just locks. It’s that tension and release—the "clash" of a passing tone resolving into a chord tone—that gives the piece its sophisticated, almost jazz-adjacent flavor without losing the dirt of the bluegrass tradition.

And the fills? They’re conversational. He’s not just filling a hole in the vocal; he’s answering the lyric. It’s all about the phrasing—knowing exactly when to lean in and when to back off. If you crowd the singer, you're just making noise. But when he drops a little bluesy curl or a quick descending run right as the phrase ends, it’s like a second voice in the room.

Ultimately, it comes down to the hierarchy of the sound. You’ve got the low end holding the floor, the middle providing the harmonic glue, and the melody floating on top. If any one of those three starts fighting for the spotlight, the whole thing collapses. The brilliance is in the balance—making something technically grueling sound like it's just rolling off the fretboard.
