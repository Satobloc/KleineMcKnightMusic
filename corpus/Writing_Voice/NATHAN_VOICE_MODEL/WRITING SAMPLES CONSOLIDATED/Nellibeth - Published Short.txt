	I could tell Poula was hiding something. I knew that look, that guarded play-it-straight look. I'd practiced for hours in front of the mirror trying to erase that same look from my own face. 
	“So, you grew up right here on a cattle conservatory?” She gestured with hands covered in dirt. 
“More or less,” I said. “Gene's my uncle--we've always just called him him Unc. I think I was in the third grade when I moved in with him. Been ranching ever since.” I jammed the trowel into the ground and let my haunches settle into the dirt. 
“I like it,” she said. “I bet the woods are beautiful in the fall.” 
She had put a daisy behind her ear. The flower was a strange intruder against her pale-olive cheeks and coaly eyeliner. 
“So, you're going to buy it?” I asked.
“I wouldn't have spent the last hour helping you put in a flower bed if I wasn't going to stay and enjoy the flowers.” She made a sort of snurf noise that must've been a laugh. 
“You got a deal worked out with Unc?” She made a noncommittal head-shrug. Of course not, I thought. Unc would get my go-ahead before he did anything firm. “You don't much look like a rancher,” I said. 
Snurf. “Well. I raise animals sort of.” She stopped as if that were a satisfactory answer and looked down at her hands, awkwardly packing in a row of phloxes. 
I wanted to like her, but something triggered my suspicion reflex. I didn't think she was a sinkfarm scout--we'd had a handful of those come through already, wanting to strip the land, put in carbon repositories. She didn't seem right, but she didn't seem that sort of wrong. Their shiftiness usually had a missionary quality about it, trying too hard to make a good impression on everybody, not content with taking your land, they had to convince you of their holy mission too. Hers was much more of a skittish shiftiness.
“Look,” I said. “Me and Unc, we just want to see the ranch in good hands. While we've been running the conservatory here, we've always sheparded the land carefully. We let the wild plants and animals do their thing and we make sure the pastures and gardens don't dick up the wilderness too much. So, tell me what you've got planned and I'll tell you if we can do business. Unc'll back me up.”
She brushed the loamy soil off her hands and frowned. “Fair enough,” she said. And after another of her interminable pauses, she said, “Do you know much about...I guess you'd call it...meattech?”
I laughed out loud. “You've got to be kidding,” I said. 
“Look, if you don't want to--” But I cut her off.
“No, no. It's okay. Why didn't you just say so? Here I was thinking you were some kind of prospector.”
“Me? A prospector? Hardly.” She wiped her face with her wrist, leaving a smudge along her graceful jaw. “So...you're okay with me doing tissue engineering here?”
“Okay? Hell, we've been synthies for years. I would've showed you the vats in the shed, except that tends to scare away some buyers.”
“You do tissue engineering out here?”
“You can't not. I mean, we keep it to a minimum, but it's practically illegal to sell truly organic meats anymore.”
	“And that doesn't bother you?” she asked with raised eyebrows.
“Mmmm--ten years ago, you might've gotten some argument from me, back when the regulations were first creeping in. It was a hard transition, especially for old-timers like Unc. But anymore, hardly a day goes by we're not taking core samples so we can tweak the mix.”
“Core samples? That's you call it?” She snurfed herself into a full-snort laugh. “Cow core samples? Ha! You're terrible.” 
She shook her head and scrunched her nose like I'd made a horrible pun, but a full-face smile split her lips and her dark eyes skewered me. 
I spread my hands and did a little head bow. “Glad to be of some amusement.” 
“So...you'll put in a good word for me with your uncle?”
“We'll see,” I said with a wink. 
“Well. Thank you. I'm glad we got that cleared up, but unfortunately it's about time for me to head back home,” she said. “Let me give you my card. I teach classes at Nutrielle. I'm not sure how applicable it would be for a ranch lab, or even if you want to keep up with tissue work after you sell the place, but if you're interested...”
“Moi?” I said, taking the card. 
After she rinsed her hands under the brittle old garden hose, she said, “I don't suppose I could bother you for a lift to the train station?” 
#
On the way back to the ranch, I kept thinking back to the smiles she gave me as we planted together. Poula was young, no more than about twenty, and maybe I was reading her wrong, but I swear there was something between us.
When I pulled back up the gravel drive, I saw the flowerbed she'd helped me finish. The front, where Poula had worked was precise, meticulous. In the back of the bed, along the barn wall, her neat ranks of peonies gave way to my free-form clots of lambs' ears and wildflowers. 
Unc was sitting on the astroturfed front steps, sipping soda from a juice glass. 
“Nice work,” said Unc, waving the glass at the flowers. “Although, your half's a little messy.”
	“Ain't that the truth,” I said. “You're looking pretty rough yourself, old man.”
“That's 'cause I'm old. What's your excuse?”
I flipped the John Deere cap off his head when I walked in the door and chuckled as I left him cursing in the twilight. 
When I got inside, I examined the card she'd given me. It was a classy translucent white with a repeating threedi of a butter knife slowly slicing through a steaming fillet mignon. It read:  
	Poula E. Santiban
Nutritional Tissue Consultant
Serving the Five Burroughs of New York
Teladdress: @SantaPoula2033
Scribbled on the back, in pen that made the animation fizzle and crack:
@NutrielleLLC
#
	I left a message in Nutrielle's callbox as soon as I got inside, but my phone immediately chirped back an auto-reply telling me all the classes were filled. 
When Unc came in, clutching the hat, I wiped the frown off my face and asked, “How'd it go?” 
He made a humorless snort, and said “Well, it seemed like they might take the financial hardship claim seriously until her attourney brought up the fact that I'm selling the ranch. He'd printed off a bunch of those classifieds we took out. The judge said he'd consider the request again in six months.” 
“That's good news,” I said, trying to cheer him up. “All we have to do is not make the sale, then I'm sure he'll grant us an exemption and we can put it all behind us.” 
“Maybe,” he said, shaking his head. “But that's not the point. Y--we owe her,” he said, the frustration in his voice apparent. He should have said, almost did say: You owe her. 
I couldn't say anything to that, so I just said, “I'm going for a ride.”
“It'll be dark soon, Mac. Come on. What are you thinking?” 
“I know, I know. But that's just it. I have to do some thinking,” I said.
“You ain't been drinkin, have you?”
“Never touch the stuff,” I said.
“Uh-huh,” he said. “Go ahead then. I can't stop you.”
#
The spring air had cooled off, and I could see my breath and hers as I saddled and mounted old Nellibeth. I trotted her out to the tree-edge. 
Nellibeth knew the drill. She brayed and launched herself into the woods, arrowing between the trunks of the dewy oaks. Moon-stippled litter crunched underhoof as she deftly cantered over roots and rocks. She moved quick, and I had to hold my body hunched like a motorcycle racer to keep from getting hit by low-hanging vines. I didn't need to see where we were going. That was Nellibeth's job.
When Unc had first brought this beautiful brindled mare home from a 4-H meeting, I'd wanted to name her Nelly--after Nelly Square, who sang the theme to Adirondack & Quentin, my favorite twodi kidstoon at the time. Unfortunately, it happened to be the one weekend a month when my step-cousin, Rayne was visiting, and Rayne had thrown a hissy. For some reason, she'd wanted to call the horse Beth, and the two of us ended up in a muddy brawl over which one of us was Unc's real kid, even though we knew damn well he wasn't really either of our dads. 
Unc had split the proverbial baby and christened our new mare Nellibeth. 
The moonlight broke wide through the trees when we came upon the creek. The wild in Nellibeth broke out, as it always did. I loostened her reins and held tight to the gullet as she galloped through shallow water. The splashes gleamed, sparkling in the corners of my wind-watering eyes.
Nellibeth slowed after a time and grazed in a marshy sandbar. When I rode at night, I usually allowed my mind to wander where it would, and usually I allowed Nellibeth to lead the way. But tonight, it happened that Nellibeth was wandering away from the creek, starting to descend one of the numerous slopes towards The Way I Didn't Ever Want To Go Again; the path where I had my drunk horseback riding accident so long ago. It sounds funny to say it that way, but it's a serious matter. Serious as a dead cousin, sister, lover. 
I yanked the reins, and Nellibeth slowed her descent down the Oak Hill path until she was moving slow enough that I could turn her around. 
And as far as my mind was concerned, I didn't want to think about anything but the little animated card I'd left on my dresser, and the way Poula's dark eyes had pinned mine when she'd given it to me.  
#
As usual, Unc was sitting over the old threedi set when I got up, still in his longjohns. There was a Steelers game on, and he pounded his knee with the heel of his hand, sipping his a Budweiser straight from the pouch and yelling at the little players who faded in and out as they dashed across the worn metal screen. 
“A little early for a beer, don'tcha think?” I said.
	“You're one to talk.” 
	He leered at me as I plodded into the kitchen to make a pot of coffee. When I went to fill up the coffee machine's reservoir I saw that, out of habit, I'd left my phone on the sink window sill. Poor thing chirped plaintively because it wasn't getting enough sunlight there this late in the spring and the battery was about dead. 
I scrolled through my messages as the pot was brewing. Calls about the ranch had picked up. Most of the teleddresses were unlisted, probably scouts, so I reflexively deleted them. I didn't want to see the place go into a sinkfarmer's hands. A couple of the messages were private numbers, and I almost started listening to them just as the coffee maker finished dripping. 
Fuck it, I thought. 
“I don't want any y'all to have it,” I muttered and emptied my callbox, then poured myself a mug of coffee and had a breakfast of a couple spicy dill pickle spears and a week-old powdered donut. Afterwards, I went out to the shed. 
Our setup was just a little cubby with a fume hood and fan. There was a twodi screen sprayed onto the dusty wood, hooked up  to a cracked glass pane for magnifying, but we never used them anymore. Marred by white scum, a small window afforded a view of “the Fleet,” as Unc called our herd, studding the hill behind the horse stable. We used to corral them up over the hill to the Knudsen's place for slaughter but Dale closed shop a couple years back and now we had to pay to truck them out to Oneida. I pulled the long syringe tips out of the dewar flask, noticing I'd need to head into town to top off the nitrogen soon.
Me and Unc didn't do much as far as tissue engineering was concerned, just seeding a few core samples with engineered cell lines and innoculating the cattle a couple times a year to keep their meat uniform and lean. I had only once made a batch of fully synth steaks. 
When he was gearing up for the switchover, Unc had first bought the wrong starter kit and we had a bunch of unused armatures and nutrient mixes laying around for a while. Eventually, I'd figured I'd give it a shot and see how they'd turn out. After a week's work, I'd had a gross of tenderloins ready for slicing. Unfortunately, they didn't turn out picture perfect like the ones stamped out by the millions in the industrial labs. We froze a couple to eat ourselves, and slapped the rest with an Artisinal label. They didn't sell that well, but at least it hadn't been a total loss. I snapped the cores onto the agar-loaded syringes and put them in the incubator. Tomorrow was the big day. 
A couple of the town boys who had helped us in the past were coming over to give us a hand wrangling the cattle, but we'd probably still have to drive over to the hardware store and pick up some day labor. It would be a good opportunity to top of the nitrogen.
I was lugging the dewar onto the bed of my pickup when Unc came running out, waving something in the air. 
“Mac!” he shouted. “Get over here. Don't make your old man run.” 
This was as excited as I'd seen him in years. 
I figured the Steelers must've won, but he panted, “It's that girl. Says she's been trying to get a hold a you all day. You shadowed your phone again, dint'ya? Anyway, she wants'ta make an offer but said she had to talk to you first.” He handed me the phone he'd been waving wildly, and jabbed a finger towards me, saying “You make sure this doesn't fall through. This is your recompense young man.” 
Poula? Trying to get a hold of me? I sat the dewar down and took the phone, shaking. I hadn't realized until that moment how much of an impression she'd made on me. I'd barely talked to a girl since Rayne passed away.
“Hi, MacElray?” she said. “So, I was glad to see you called about the classes. Your name's on my wait list for next semester, but I wanted to tell you...there's an opening in a special training class I'm running here for employees of one of our subsidiaries, and I figured since you already had some experience, you'd fit right in. They're all from different branches and they're all relative new-hires, so you could even audit the class for free and nobody would be the wiser.” 
“Um,” I was a little foggy-headed and I wasn't sure how to reply. 
“Sure, I guess. Yeah.” I said.
“Great. Show up at the Nutrielle building at eight sharp. You know where it is?”
“Wait, tomorrow?”
“Yeah, sorry. I forgot. That's the only catch. But this could be a really good thing for you. It might even be a foot in the door here or at BetaSynth, or wherever.”
“I wish I could, but I can't make it into the city tomorrow. We've got a lot of work lined up on the ranch,” I said.
“Oh, okay.” She sounded disappointed. “Well...think about it. If you show up, you're in. If not... Maybe some other time, I guess.” 
There was a long silence, then we both tried to speak at once. “Um...I was going to ask you to join me for dinner afterwards.”
“Wow,” I said, astonished. 
“Don't answer now. Just think about it, okay?” 
“Yeah,” I said. “Okay.”
	Another long pause, then I said, “Unc says you wanted to make an offer on the ranch?”
	“Oh, right. I, um...” And just then, the usual hesitation which I'd almost started to appreciate in her voice turned a corner and that skittish shiftiness came back. 
	She said, “I'm not a hundred percent certain, but I, I think I can offer seven-fifty, with maybe twenty percent down. The thing is, I've got some...research and they're going to buy it and usually they pay so much and they really like my work and I've got a meeting lined up and...well, I should know in the next couple days.”
	“Alright,” I said, again at a loss for words. 
“We'll talk about it tomorrow,” she said. “Either on the phone or, I hope, over dinner.”

?	Unc threw a fit when I told him I wanted to go into the city while he was here inoculating the Fleet. 
“What do you mean you're going to the city?” He said. “You can't leave me here to run the inoculation alone. I don't care how many extra hands we hire, I can't lead them all on my own. We don't keep the Fleet in shape, we don't make our money, we don't fix up the property, we don't sell the property. You don't get it. It's important, son.”
“That's why I'm doing it, Unc. That's why I'm going. Because she wants to buy the farm and she wants to talk about it over dinner,” I yelled. “Don't make it about me and you. So yeah, I'm going.” 
“Bull. Shit. What kind of dinner lasts all day long, MacElray? This ain't about the ranch, or she'da made a for real offer already. And this sure in hell ain't about you and me, Mac. This is about you. Just you. So, fine. Fine! Go.” 
I pushed over the dewar and let its contents sizzle out, sending ice-cold steam across the gravel.
“I'm takin the fuckin horse out,” I said.
#
This time, me and Nellibeth rode all the way down the creek to Oak Hill path and kept going. It was still early, and though my memories were crisp, the midday sun had the effect of washing out some of the pain. During the day, it was just a thing that had happened. At night...
I shook it off. I remembered the late steed Peanut Butter, after me and Rayne and Lizzie had ridden through the creek. The horses shaking off in the clearing here, sending water over the three of us. 
Me and Rayne used to sneak Budweisers out of Unc's fridge in the barn whenever she visited. Usually, the two of us got drunk and took turns daring one another to climb on the roof of the stable or run buck naked through the cow pasture. But Lizzie was younger, and when we offered her a Bud, she must've been afraid to touch the alcohol stash of a man who was, after all, a stranger to her. Rayne followed her friend's lead and I'd just figured more for me.
Night riding is always a blast, but the alcohol had raised the lid for me. It was ecstatic. When we broke out into the starlit creek, with stars made of water flying by on both sides, and real stars above reflecting in the water below...
It was like flying through space. 
After the horses shook off, we dismounted to wipe our faces with our hands and some kind of drunk humor made me want to fingerpaint with mud all over Nellibeth's flank—I think I'd been thinking something like, see if you can shake this off. Well, she shook alright. 
Lizzie got down, ducked right under Nellibeth. Stupid. Nellibeth sat right down on top of her, and although I heard the popping crack that might have been her collar bone, I hadn't recognized it for what it was, and I couldn't help but burst into laughter. Rayne had tried to pull her friend out, but Nellibeth had other plans. She rolled over, smashing Lizzie's face into the mud and knocking Rayne onto the rocks, unconscious. The mare had wiggled around on her back until she was satisfied she'd gotten all the mud off, then stood and trampled Lizzie as she ran off into the darkness.
Even in my drunken stupor, I knew better than to move Lizzie. She'd been broken up too bad. I didn't think to check if she was breathing. I just lugged Rayne onto Peanut Butter and rode back to the ranch as fast as I dared.
Me and Unc had left Rayne on the porch and called an ambulance, then we'd gone off on foot to get Lizzie. 
After I don't know how long hiking--all the while Unc muttering about how he should've put a goddamn lock on that goddamn refrigerator--we found the spot and when we shined a flashlight in Lizzie's face, we saw her mouth and nose jam-packed with mud. She was clearly dead. Until Rayne passed on after more than a year in and out of a coma, Lizzie was the only dead body I'd seen. She's still the one that haunts me.
The next day, we'd discovered that Peanut Butter had suffered a broken leg somewhere along the way. Nellibeth didn't turn up for three days. We'd found her grazing in the cow pasture, listless and bothered, but completely clean.
You'd be surprised at how much paperwork there is these days when a large animal dies, but me and Unc had gone through it all together, and I think it even brought us a little closer together. The other deaths, we didn't talk about.
I broke myself out of my memories, and let Nellibeth find her own way home. After I got her back into her stable, I made a call to Dale Knudsen's son, Rick. He agreed to give Unc a hand with the inoculation, waving off any notion of payment. Me and Ricky used to be buddies, and he'd spent plenty of time helping out here, not to mention working for years on his own dad's ranch. 
He'd be a more than a passable substitute for me. Besides, I think Unc was always fond of Ricky, so I figured they'd be happy to see each other again. I hoped that would take some of the sting away from my abdication. 
When I told Unc, he seemed satisfied, but said: “Alright, go to the class. But could you try to be back early enough to swing by Tanninger's for a nitrogen refill? The account's in your name, so I don't think me or Ricky can do that without you.”
I nodded and pulled on my dusty brown leather jacket. I stayed silent all the way out the door, my anger from the argument dulled but not forgotten.  
#
The Nutrielle building was a small shard of glass, lost among all the more famous spires of the Manhattan skyline. When I showed up, Poula checked me off on her list like the other few students who arrived at the same time, even asking my last name, though she must've known it perfectly well. 
“Welcome, Mr. Dresch,” she said in a professional tone. Only the barest hint of a smirk let me know that she even recognized me.  
In the classroom, Poula wasted no time.
“Okay guys,” she said. There was a dozen or so of us, and at thirty-five, I'm pretty sure I was the oldest. “You all have some experience or BetaSynth wouldn't have hired you, but I'm sure there's a wide range so let me know if I'm going too fast.”
The class was being held in a sterile white conference room covered in deactivated threedi screens. The room's only character came from a few scuffs on the floor and a plastic plate, knife, and fork at each seat. The plates each bore a beige paper cone, and I swear it looked like a we were preparing to eat dunce caps for breakfast.
“On the table in front of you,” announced Poula, “you'll find a sample of edible synthetic flesh. Go ahead and take a look. Smell it, touch it, taste it.” 
Poula's demeanor shocked me a bit. Her halting sentences and awkward intonations were gone and she spoke with a force and certainty beyond her years. It reminded me of the way Unc used to talk to me and Rayne when I we were young. I lifted the cover on my sample and poked at the synthmeat. It was pale, mushy. I cut off as thin a slice as I could manage with the tiny plastic knife and fork. 
“Don't eat too much now. You're sharing. Okay, so who thinks--” she started. 
The flesh in my mouth had a chemically, acrid flavor, so I raised my hand right away to tell her mine was the flawed sample. 
 “I haven't even finished asking the question,” she said with her little snurf. “A little over-eager, are we? I was going to say, who thinks they have a good sample?”
I looked around, embarassed, but nobody else put up their hand.
“Good,” she said. “Every single one of these has something wrong with it. Too much EDTA, too little EDTA, not enough synth blood, too much fat, too long on the armeture, poor l-sally ratio.”
This isn't too bad, I thought. Nothing more complicated than the booklet that came with Unc's acidentally-purchased synth kit.
“Under each plate, you'll find a card listing the sample's vitals,” she said. “Taste them all, do a mental analysis, and see if you can figure out what causes the off flavors, colors, textures. Make a note of what's wrong with it and how you know what's wrong with it.” 
Suddenly, I realized I was in over my head. I'd just picked up what little I knew from the tiny booklets and instructional videos that came with the Unc's farm synth kits--more than anything, I'd thrown myself into learning how to keep our meat up to code to get my mind off Rayne and that little girl Lizzie. I didn't know thing one about analyzing synthmeat for biochemical flaws. 
But I didn't want to embarass myself, so I played hung in there as best I could. 
Poula went on, apparently off the top of her head, barely taking a second to breath between sentences.
“Learn to recognize the signs of an off batch. You won't want your instruments be a crutch once you get on the line. When you walk into a lab, if you aren't tasting your work, you aren't doing your job,” she said. “Eventually, you'll be able to tell a bad batch by tasting the agar. But to start, you should come to work hungry every day. Alright guys, now rotate and do the same thing with the next sample.” 
Poula lectured almost non-stop the whole morning. She didn't seem to pay me any special attention, even though I tried to catch her eye every chance I got. The class rotated and tasted, sniffed and took notes, and Poula made the rounds. Each time she came by my station, she nodded and carefully avoided my gaze.
“Now that you've ruined your appetite, I see it's lunch time,” she said finally. “Break.”
	When the other students mingled and ate packed lunches, sinking into the cushy leather sofas in the building's lobby, I went to the restroom and made sure my collar was straight and my hair wasn't sticking up before perusing stiff portraits of Nutrielle executives while I waited to ask the receptionist where Poula's office was. 
	I found her in her sixth floor office, eating sliced apples and dulce de leche and sitting crosslegged in a big swivel chair behind an even bigger threedi desk, surrounded by little plastic vats and boxes marked with chemical safety infomration. 
“So,” I said, hoping I sounded more jovial than insecure, “did you already forget me?”
“Of course not,” she said with a smile. “But I can't play favorites in class. Close the door.”
I did as she asked, now feeling overwhelmed by jittery anxiousness. She asked if I was enjoying the class, and I told her I thought she was phenomenal. Her cheeks turned five shades of crimson.  
“Take a look at this,” she said, kicking off the desk and riding the chair across to the full-wall window behind her. For a second, I thought she was going to fly through the glass and plummet to the granite sidewalk, but she stopped herself gently and reached down into a large paper-towel-lined aquarium on the floor. Delicately, she coaxed a tiny black mouse into her cupped hand, then wheeled back to the desk.
“Cute,” I said. “What's his name?”
“This is Percy-2,” she said. All at once, her tone dropped nearly to a whisper. It was that same strange skittishness, only more...conspiratorial. 
“I made him,” she said, petting with her pinkie between his translucent pink ears.
“What's that?” I wasn't sure I'd heard right. ”Did you say you made him?”
She just nodded, biting her lip and pegging me with her eyes. The confusion must've been plain on my face, so she went on.
“Look, you and I, we both make tissue, right? On the farm, in the lab, what's the difference?” she said with a shrug, then went on, “Nothing, except that I have more equipment, more reagents. So, I can grow...more tissue.”
“But how did you...” How do I say this? “...h-how did you...put him together?”
“Well, not that long ago, we used to have to make whole synthetic organ systems to keep our synthflesh alive, before we got the gist of the nutrient baths and stuff. So, I was practically synthing whole organisms from the start. The first time I tried really going all the way, with Percy-1, I had to grow most of the organs seperately, suture everything together. P-One wasn't perfect, but he wasn't Frankenstein's monster either.”
“But he didn't survive?” I asked.
“Well, he lived five and a half years...that's a really long time for a mouse.”
“But you didn't suture this one together?” I asked.
“Nope,” she said. “I grew him in situ, using normal armatures--the new ones that dissolve on their own. I had to go through quite a few test runs. Fifty or a hundred maybe. Most of those failed because the I didn't monitor the circulatory system or the nervous system development closely enough, but I got better with each try.
“Now,” she said, “I can make the whole integrated animal, nerves, muscles, capillaries, everything--for the most part...well...almost indistinguishable from the real thing unless you're looking closely with a good magnification screen.”
I reached out and pet the little thing. Not completely indistinguishable, I thought. Not if you know what you're looking at anyway. 
On first glance, the only strange thing about it was the violet eyes. The ears were beautiful, completely symetrical right down to the tiny veinlets. If you didn't know this mouse was synth nose to tail, you might not think much of it, the closer I looked, the more I could tell there was something weird about the little guy. Some parts were too perfect, others weren't quite right. Its legs were slightly stunted, its ribs bowed out too wide. 
“I love the eyes,” I said.
“They're my signature,” she replied in a whisper.  
Hushing her tone even further, as though she were speaking to the mouse, she said, “You can see I still have some trouble with the bones. Y'know, nobody's ever really tried to grow bones before, never needed to. I think I'm the first. I'm ready to move on to larger animals.”
Those last words were just mouthed, nearly voiceless. And I...well, I was speechless. 
“You're amazing,” I said, feeling a fizzling tingle run up my back and over my shoulders. 
Her lips parted as if to speak, and in that moment, I was more attracted to her than any other girl I'd ever met, seen, or imagined. 
A knock on the door broke my reverie. 
“Oh, hey Doug,” she said, waving somebody in. 
Feeling out of place, I stood and nodded curtly at the stranger. He was a slight fellow, with a slack face and etchglass spectacles. I hadn't seen a pair of eyeglasses actually in use for years; he must've been waiting for corrective surgery. He couldn't have been too poor--the suit he wore was obviously tailored for his lank frame. He didn't introduce himself, and he barely looked at me, so I gave Poula a wave and an awkward wink and went to let myself out.

Their lawyers say they already own the patents because I worked for them when I made them. They just gave me a two thousand dollar bonus and recommended me for an honorary doctorate. It's bullshit. I don't know what I'm going to do, but I still want to buy the conservatory. I think if I talk to Doug--”
I hung up and went to bed.
#
The next morning, the full brightness of the sun was shining in my window as I tossed and turned, but I couldn't will myself to get out of bed. I sobbed into my pillow, trying to keep Unc from seeing my useless state, but he interrupted my pillow-drowned darkness with a nock on the door.
“You look bad,” he said.
I shrugged, then sat up with the sheets pulled over my shoulders.
“I just had a long conversation with Poula,” said Unc. “She was grilling me about you.”
“She's here?” I sputtered.
“Over the phone.”
I wasn't sure if I was feeling relief or disappointment, but I nodded and asked, “What did you tell her?”
“I didn't tell her nothin. We talked about the ranch, and told me she had the funds and wanted to buy it.”
“You're selling it to her?” I said with a dead flat tone.
“We'll talk about that,” he said. “The thing is...she asked about you and Rayn's accident. She told me you said Rayne was the one who'd been drinkin.”
I closed my eyes.
He went on, “It didn't occur to me that you would lie about such a thing, so I didn't even think to try to back up your little fib. I don't mean to hurt you, but I just told her what happened as best I knew it.”
I took a deep breath.
“What did she say?” I asked.
“Nothin. Not for a long time, then she said she had to go.”
Unc waited for me to say something, but when I kept the silence, he said: “I accepted her offer. It's not as much as she thought she was going to offer, but it's enough for me to buy a little house maybe. Probably not enough to pay our--your--debts.”
#
Poula came out with her lawyers to finalize the purchase with us and our lawyers, and I managed to drag myself out of bed and look presentable. 
I was surprised to see they came in three separate vehicles, with a horse trailer in tow. I wished I'd been surprised to see bathroom boy get out and stand alongside Poula.
She introduced her three lawyers, and we introduced our one. 
Finally, she said, “And you know Doug.”
“Shouldn't you say, Doug-1?” I asked, bitterness clinging to my teeth.
It couldn't have come as a surprise that I knew, but she looked confused. 
“Did you finish Doug-2 yet?” I spat.
She still shook her head, and I said, “He's obviously one of yours. Did you sew up that Frankenstein thing in your closet, or was it another failed experiment?”
Doug looked back and forth between us, then I saw him tilt his head back, forming his lips into an 'O' of recognition. 
“Stop,” he said. “No. Poula didn't make me.” 
He almost laughed--incredulous snurf, but not the adorable sound Poula made. 
“I made myself like this,” he said. “I used her techniques, but I did it myself...to look younger. I know it's wrong to blame her. I was mad. But it's my fault, not Poula's.”
Poula stood agape.
“You thought I made Doug?” she said. “Jesus, Mac. Oh my god, no, no, no...”
The herd of lawyers stood by like they were trying not to interfere with a barroom brawl.
“Then what was that monster in your closet,” I said.
“I was going to start with a cow, but you inspired me. It was supposed to be a surprise, like I said,” she said. 
She tapped Doug on the shoulder, pointed over her own shoulder. He nodded and went around back of the trailer. 
“She won't be perfect,” said Poula. “There aren't vats big enough, so I had to make her the old way. But at least Nutrielle--” she gave a sour glance to the lawyers. “Nutrielle is going to set up a new lab, right here. I'll be able to put my techniques into production.”
Doug backed a horse out of the trailer, walked it around, awkwardly. He obviously had never handled a horse before, but they made a good pair, him with his glasses and bent back, and the horse...well, she was beautiful. Her ribcage was a little wide, and her ears were too perfect. But her eyes were beautiful violet. 
Poula said, “I was going to name her Nellibeth-2.”
	# 
“So,” I said, “that's how I got into edible tissue engineering. The judge ended up awarding Lizzie's family almost twice what Doug Lenzler bought the ranch for. I can't let my uncle pay that on his own, and that's why I'm looking for work. I see her on the threedi a lot, so I guess Poula must be doing well.”
I leaned back in the reclining chair and spread my hands in a there-you-have-it gesture.
	The guy behind the desk looked slightly agog, and when he regained his composure, he said “I appreciate your honesty, MacElray. We'll message you in a few days to let you know our decision.”
As I stood, he added, “I can't begin to understand how you must feel about her, but having Dr. Santiban's name on your resume puts you way ahead of the crowd. Good luck.”



Afterward: Review by Fred Patton of Flayrah.com
(Excerpted from review of "Bronies: For the love of ponies", edited by L. Lambert Lawson)

“Nellibeth” by Nathan McKnight, the final story in the anthology, is both the longest and the only one about a normal, non-magical horse. In fact Nelibeth, an old mare, is barely a supporting character. In the slightly far future, “Uncle Gene”, the elderly owner of a livestock farm in New York state, is relying on his young nephew MacElray Dresch to advise him whether to sell out to Nutrielle LLC, a large corporation engaged in nutritional tissue experimentation. Mac is wooed in more ways than one by Poula Santiban, Nutrielle’s charismatic “Nutritional Tissue Consultant”; a combination of a head project scientist and the company’s leading recruiter of research staff.

Poula both wants to add Unk’s farm to Nutrielle’s holdings, and to add Mac to the new class of promising trainees. She gets close enough to Mac to show him some of her private research into experimental tissue growth, which goes beyond developing new edible meats into creating new life forms. Mac has been looking forward to developing new foods for a hungry world, but playing god is something else again. Are you wondering where Nellibeth comes into this story? Don’t worry; she plays a very small but very important part. “Nellibeth” arguably does not belong with the fourteen prior stories, but it is a very good science fiction story and I thank Bronies for making it available to us.
