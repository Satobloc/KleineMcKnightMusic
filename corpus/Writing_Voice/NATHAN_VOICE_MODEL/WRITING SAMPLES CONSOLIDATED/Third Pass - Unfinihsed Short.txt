FRIST PASS: The Astronomer

Anno M.DC. [IX Kal. Nov.] In vespere ad solis occasum, quando secundum habitum contemplatus sum sidera sereno iam animadvertit sidus lucens prope horizontem novum et insolitum. Obscura admodum fuit, et cognovi quod non est ita certum corpus aethereum ut semel neque eorum similes, ut prioris cometae wisi. (Magnus astrologus non statim agnoscere habet.) Tulisset, quorum ambitu infra Lunam, et ex his quae ego fecerim et erubescat Uraniborg ad alios ostendere, quae saxosas inter alia conclusi sicut Deus ab homine plus quam figuris.

On that evening [October 24, 1600], after sunset, when, according to my habit, I was contemplating the stars in a clear sky, I noticed that a new and unusual constellation was now shining near the horizon. It was quite dim, and I knew that they were not fixed Ætherial lights, as they appeared at first, nor were they like the comets of the previous decades. (It takes no great astronomer to quickly recognize.) Their circuit had taken them below the Moon, and from my observations which I made at Uraniborg and which I was too ashamed to show to any others, I concluded that among the rocky objects were other regular shapes more like those created by Man than by God.

Hitherto unpublished manuscript of Tycho Brahe 


	One year later, Tycho died.

	Four-hundred twenty-five years later, the invasion began. 



SECOND PASS: The Warriors

	
It wasn't the chlorine-stinking film the Cats-N-Beans left all over the ground that made her shake in her khakis, and it wasn't the burning wall of skyscrapers on the horizon or the memory of the firing squad. It wasn't even the insanity of the Plan. 
	It was the door in front of her. This home that had been theirs and now was his, and now the door opened. She heard the flashlight swing against the door back, where she'd always left it hanging. 
	"Lilac."
	"Dylan."
	"What do you want?"
	"We have to talk."
	"Not about us we don't."
	"No," she agreed. "Not about us."
	"Then what?"
	"Can I come in?"
	"You know that's a bad idea."
	His soap-and-leather scent eased into her nostrils, displacing the familiar odors of alien monsters and human misery. 
	"It's not like that, Dylan. I finally gave up on getting you back. For real this time. I just want to talk to, um, I...I...damn it, sorry. I thought I knew what I wanted to say. It's still hard for me. But really, this isn't about you and me. Can we just sit down and talk? And you can kick me out any time you want. Please?"
	He backed up and let her let herself in. 
	"War is coming," she said as she crouched on the once-blue, once-hers, pleather footstool. "A real war."
	"What. We finally have a plan after all these years? You can't expect me to--" but she cut him off.
	"A war between people, Dylan. Did you know they expelled us?"
	Dylan nodded. "Mom has a scanner, we hear more than most. But you're not Americano."
	"No, I'm not. I mean, being on a US unit is bad enough, even as a trainer. It's suspicious. But you're right, I'm probably safe."
	"Then what?" He stared at her, a meniscus of water rimming his eyelids.
	Lilac reached a hand toward his good leg, but he pulled away.
	"Dylan, it's not safe for you here."
	He laughed, "What are they going to do, discharge me again?"
	"You have no idea how bad it's gotten. Everyone knows the So-Called War is hopeless, but even the troops are throwing in the towel now. They're coming home."
	"Baltimore isn't their home."
	"You know how they are, Dylan. They'll come here and wait for their UN transports, And they'll bring their hatred and frustration and pent up shit with them. And when the transports don't come, they'll take it out on...people like you." 
	"Lilac, just stop. You don't know what it's like. You think being treated like shit in your own country is something new? I got lots of practice playing servant to management and fucking tourists. 'Si señor, tip please? Schöne mädchen, want buy necklace? Ole hyvä ja ikävä, haluan avata oven sinulle ja miehesi?"
	His eyes burned angry.
	"Is that how you think of me? Like--" her voice cracked, "--some spoiled little girl? In case you didn't notice, who's wearing the khakis around here?" 
	Lilac wiped her eyes, they felt itchy and red. 
	She wiped her eyes again, wetting her fingers. 
	"Just listen to me," she wept.
	He sat back, arms crossed.
	"We can be safe. We can hide out someplace safe until the situation on the ground changes."
	"On the ground?"
	"Just listen. It's a solid plan. It's going to sound crazy, it did to me at..."
	"Just spit it out, Lilac."
	"We can go on board the ships--"
	"You've got to be fucking with me."
	"No, listen...they've got water, soil, everything we need."
	"You don't actually think they'd let us stay there."
	"You know they ignore us."
	"Yeah, but on their ship?"
	"We've already done it. Two groups of settlers, about fifty total. They're already living aboard the ship. About two months now."
	He blinked his eyes, started to speak a couple times, uncrossed his arms, then just shook his head. 
	Lilac smiled, "It'll work. Really, it will. We got a crate full of seedlings--carrots, potatoes, spinach. It's like an unsettled island in there. We just have to setup camp and stay out of the Cats-N-Beans' way."
	He sighed and smiled. "You're insane," he said, breaking the tension with an awkward laugh.
	"Then, you're in?" 
	"No," he chuckled. "No, definitely not. I'm sorry."
	"But Dylan," she protested.
	"You have to go now."
	"But--"
	"You promised." 
	"But--" she stammered, even as she got up to go.
	He opened the door and she started backing terout, but as he shut it, she stopped it with her foot.
	"Dylan, they're going to kill you. They're going to kill all the Americanos, they're--"
	"Go!" he shouted.
	She twisted her hands in her hair, looked down at her knees.
	"Lilac! You have to leave!"
	She stared into the camo on her legs.
	Her training kicked in, and already regretting the irrevocable change she'd initiated, she jerked the heavy flashlight from the door and thudded him on the back of the head. He slumped in the doorway, and she kneeled over him. 
	
The truck rumbled over cracked and melted soil, avoiding the paths of the intruders and avoiding the deepest meteoric furrows and smoking debris. Lilac held Dylan's head in her lap. She patted drops of sweat away from his upper lip stubble and held a leaky sack of frozen succotash on the back of his head. Now and then he turned and made a troubled face, but she already felt him distancing himself from her. And though she died inside, she knew he would live.
	The ship's hulking mass radiated heat into the air. It sat overlooking the city, on the crushed antennae of Television Hill. 
	"Are you ready sir?" said private Richfield, struggling to hold her hands on the wheel. 
	Lilac nodded once. 
	The truck pulled to a stop, and Richfield got out. She twisted her arms in the small crate's rope handles, donning it like a backpack, then helped Lilac lug Dylan out of the back seat. 
	Ammonia penetrated her sinuses. 
	Weighted down, but moving smoothly, they slipped between the giant radiator fins that protruded from all over the hull of the vessel.
	Warmth engulfed them, and she felt Dylan try to shift his weight. 
	They struggled into a corridor with an awkwardly tilted floor, and onward up a series of narrow inclines along a battery of air ducts. 
	She'd been dealing with the Cats-N-Beans long enough not to be surprised by their lack of gauards or security measures. That was the most frustrating thing about them. You could fight them, kill them, even blow them up entirely--assuming you had enough ammo--but they acknowledged neither victory nor defeat, they just flowed forth with the inevitability of nightfall. 
	When she and Richfield and Dylan's limp body neared the area Lilac knew as the Dirt Field, Lilac could hear the clamor of human activity. It was distinctly different from the mud-puddly sloshing sounds that accompanied the motions of the Cats-N-Beans. It was all crying babies, yells and shouts and barking military back-and-forth. 
	At the top of an upper ramp, Lilac and private Richfield dropped, exhausted, to the grated floor. A torrent of humans streamed into the ship from adjoining corridors. 
	"How's he doing?" said Richfield. 
	"He's breathing and he's got a strong pulse, so I guess he's not all bad."
	"Better than those poor suckers in Gaithersburg. Can you believe they just shot them in cold blood?"
	"Americanos got the blame. Maybe they deserve a lot of blame for a lot of things, but they didn't bring on the invasion, and they don't deserve to be rounded up like animals. Especially when we should be fighting together, as humans."
	"The So-Called War. It was a joke from the start."	
	"That's no excuse to turn against each other."
	"Yes sir," said Richfield.
	"You don't need to call me sir. There's no rank anymore. It's every man and woman for herself."
	"What you did was right, sir. I'll respect you for that regardless of your rank."
	"What, kidnapping my ex boyfriend?"
	"You did what you had to do. For his own good."
	"Hmphf."

They picked up and continued on, melding with the throngs of refugees. What started as a couple score had become thousands. 
	The Dirt Field was packed with humans, though they surged in the shadow of the globular alien monsters lurching on a terrace far above. 
	"I thought the Cats-N-Beans stink was bad," said Richfield, wiping her nose on her shoulder even as she contiued to lug Dylan's dead weight, "but sweat and shit and the alien smell all together--that's the worst."
 	"Get used to it," said Lilac. "This is home. For now."
	The overhang dripped soil as whatever activity the Cats-N-Beans were engaged in above got close to the edge, so the broad terrace was marked by a moraine blobbed together from freshly fallen dirt. On the flat parts grew sparse mushroom-like mounds, supplemented by what looked like Earth grass just starting to grow in.
	Clutches of humans were huddled against the steeply angled wall, crowding under the overhang. 
	"Looks like people are starting to set up camp."
	"Mmh. We better stake out some ground before it gets too scarce."
	"There's so much space though," said Richfield.
	"Yeah, but people are just going to keep streaming in, and we don't even know what counts as good land here. Best to grab something now than take the risk that we'll be crowded out later."
	Richfield sighed, saying, "I can't shake the feeling that we're becoming parasites on these things."
	"Well. If we're the cockroaches here--that's better than being dead."
	"Don't you think at some point, they're going to try to stamp us out?" 
	"Maybe," said Lilac. "We'll fight that battle when we come to it." She'd been trying not to think more than a few days in advance. All imaginings were at the ends of their roads. "Over there," she said, nodding her head toward a free space between a crowd of tobacco-chewing teens and something that looked like it was shaping up to be a field hospital. "When he comes around, we can all do some exploring, but for now we'll have to split up duties. Do you want to stay with Dylan or see if you can scare up some bedding?"
	"I think I want to rest,. sSir."
	Lilac nodded. 
	They lugged Dylan over, laying him next to the wall. His breathing had become more even and he seemed almost comfortable, laying on the dirt, resting his head on crumpled paper from the crate. Almost as soon as they got to the clearing, a huge slice of the wall turned transparent, exposing the smokescape that covered most of Baltimore. The ship's walls seemed annealed, the border between dull ceramic-like wall and dull glass-like wall was marked by a wide swath of melty-looking welding bead. 
	Agog, Lilac stared out at the burning world. 
	"Scary, isn't it?" said one of the teens, in a war-matured voice. "That's why we moved over here."
	She shook off her astonishment. 
	"Might come in handy later. We'll need sunlight to grow crops," said Lilac. 
	Private Richfield had busied herself planting the seedlings in a careful square around their little plot of land, the most forceful demarcation that seemed appropriate for what amounted to a sprawling refugee camp. Lilac lay down next to Dylan, feeling a shade of the comfort they once shared. She faded in and out of sleep in natural coordination with Dylan's sleep motions.
	
When Dylan started to fully rouse, Lilac instinctively tried to nuzzle him. 
	He pulled away groggily, and yawned "Where is this?"
	Lilac didn't say anything. She just put an arm over his lap.
	"Seriously, Lilac. Where the hell are we? What happened?"
	"We're safe."
	"Did you hit me? Why are we on the ground?"
	"We're on the ship, like I said," said Lilac.
	He jumped to his knees and backed away. 
	"Dylan," said Lilac.
	"How could you?"
	"I had to keep you safe, Dylan. I love you. Even if you don't love me back, I need to know you're out of harm's way."
	"Fuck. You."
	"I saved your life," said Lilac.
	"So says you, but that's not your decision. I'm going back." 
	"No! They'll kill you. You don't understand, I saw them shooting people. Anyone with an Americano accent. They'll line you up and they'll shoot you in the head." 
	"They wouldn't."
	"They would. I saw it with my own eyes. You're not safe out there."
	Dylan stared out the window. "You think I don't know that? You think it's safer in here? I don't know, you might be right...but I have family out there, I have friends. So do you. Did you ever think of them when you hashed this plan to run away?"
	"I only thought of you."
	Dylan rolled his eyes, shook his head, and started walking off.
	"Where are you going?"
	"I don't know," he said. "I just need to think."
	"Then you might stay? Here, with me?"
	"Not with you." 
	"Okay, but here? Where it's safe? It's only temporary, until the death squads end, or until the civil war burns itself out. Maybe we can even really fight against the aliens from inside."
	"For now," said Dylan. "I don't know where else to go."
	"It's only temporary," she repeated.
	"I--" Dylan was interrupted by a quake that knocked him to his feet.
	A shout came up from the crowds. 
	Lilac looked around. 
	Richfield ran to the window.
	"We're taking off!" she shouted.
	The teens crowded around the window, and Dylan and Lilac pushed their way to the front.
	The whole ship lurched, quaking to a low hover. Dylan put his hands against the window as if to push through it.
	The ship shook again and the smoke and wreckage dropped away. 	
	Dylan pounded on the glass, then pushed back through the crowd, Lilac chasing behind.
	"Dylan!" 
	"I have to get off!" he shouted.
	"Dylan!"
	She chased him a ways, but lost him in the crowd. Another burst of acceleration knocked the entire crowd to its knees. Lilac looked up. Through the tall slice of window, she could see the curvature of the Earth, for the first time, seeing the full extent of destruction wrought by the invaders.
	The globe no longer looked like the Earth she'd known. Black and gray smoke scarred the beautiful white clouds. 
	Home fell gradually away below, and the time passed in a confusing blur of dirt and despair and huddled humanity.

When the Earth was little patch of tranquil-seeming blur in the window, in the background of the alien fleet, and the wails of despair and disbelief subsided, Lilac walked.
	Occasionally, somebody would approach her and try to cajole her to joining thiier camp/family/batallion. But she just kept walking. If somebody gave her trouble, she smashed their face and went on. You'd think the camos would tip them off that she wasn't to be fucked with. But humans are stupid. Stupid enough to turn on their own in the middle of a crisis.
	More people than she imagined had taken refuge aboard the ships. She overheard stories of people getting trapped in corridors and overrun by the Cats-N-Beans, but it didn't sound like they were making any deliberate effort to exterminate them, and the main terrace seemed to be completely off their radar. 
	At one point, she was startled by a glop of alien goo landing right in front of her. She looked up, and though it was far above, the awkward movement of the alien sack of organs chilled her. It lolled over the edge, looking like an animate pile of human kidneys and screeching tiger heads. 
	But she kept walking, and as she walked, an uncomfortable calm seemed to set in. At several points along her path, volunteers had organized to distribute food. She had to admit, people weren't all bad.
	She accepted bread and tea and packets of katsup, and it made for a passable lunch with enough left over to stuff in her thigh pockets.
	The muddy terrace stretched on for miles, it seemed. And all along it, people moved and tried to find meaningful activities. 
	Eventually, Lilac ran up against a tightly-packed crowd blocking the entire width of the terrace. People piled up and over the central morraine and squeezed shoulder to shoulder.
	A window high above showed nothing but black space punctuated by the glowing drives of a handful of the alien ships.
	Ominous chatter circulated among the crowd, and Lilac asked a young man what was happening.
	"Not sure," he said. "Somebody saw something, I think."
	She pushed her way through the crowd until she reached an edge. 
	"There's another camp," she heard somebody say.
	She looked between the shoulders of the front line of people, and separated by a stretch of dirt, there was a line of hooded figures. They weren't humans, and they weren't Cats-N-Beans. And they were armed. 

	Five minutes later, the battle began.

	Six weeks later, the battle was over.

	"Hmmm. Days. Alright then." 
	The continual orangey light of the ship had grated on everyone's nerves. The military government had confiscated all generators and electrical devices, and the only thing approaching a genuine day-night cycle was Captain Sweet's private quarters, which she shared with no one. 
	"Any progress on the effort to obtain a sample of Cats-N-Beans tissue?"
	"No sir," Richfield shook her head, trying to seem repentant. It wasn't just the fear of the aliens that got to her. They'd lost too many to the aliens by accident, and throwing away people to retrieve a piece of one didn't seem worth it. They already knew they weren't related to the Fungi Eaters. They were alien. Couldn't they just leave it at that? 
	Captain Sweet stepped up and put her hand on Richfield's shoulder.
	"I'm only going to explain this to you once. We need to regiment society, we need to make a well-oiled machine, and we need to understand the biology of the Cats-N-Beans so that we know what they're looking for, and the next time they conquer a planet, we're prepared." 

	

THIRD PASS: The Conquerers


Dovae XIV bowed his head, and intoned, "In the name of Ursuul the Savior and Dovae the Conquerer, guide my hand in battle."
He took a rasping breath and ordered the Fungarians out of his tent with a wave. They scattered, leaving filth in their wake. It was too bad they were so damned useful. 
"Aloo!" he shouted, swabbing the revitalance injection point, and Aloo came running.
"Yes, Lord?" 
"Status?" He threw the swab in a bin.
"We're approaching the first pa--"
"I know we're approaching first pass. What I want from you is--"
"Sorry," said Aloo, "this is my first invasion."
Why does it have to be so difficult? he thought. Forty years' rule, six planets conquered. And he keeps losing his assistants in battle, so nobody worthwhile is available when the next time comes.
"Just tell me--what do we know about this system?"
"Well, we're just in telescope range, and it looks pretty typical for a Catsabees target. Water world, a great deal of pollution and environmental damage, but no space technologies that we can spot."
"And navigation?"
"Well, the astrogators aren't sure. Since the Catsabees cleared out Windoward village, we've had a hard time finding anybody who knows the skies well enough."
Mother had warned him to distribute knowledge more widely. But what could he do? There were only so many humans to go around. And Fungarians couldn't--or wouldn't--learn human science. Didn't stop them from taking part in the colonizations, tough. 
"Prepare my entourage."
"Yes Lord," said Aloo.

Dovae XIV had personally taken part in the colonization of eight worlds, six of which had civilizations that needed to be conquered first. It would have been thirteen worlds if the Catsabies didn't have the frustrating habit of visiting the same systems every few hundred years. 
When Aloo returned, Dovae allowed himself to be dressed. 
Here we go again, he thought. In spite of the injections, he was getting too old for this. 
He left the imperial quarters, making his way through the great hall made of scrap metal and Catsabee fibers. His advisors and phalanxes joined him along the grand promenade. He kept his encouragements short and led them to the balcony.
Then he stepped up to the dias overlooking the inner crowds--those wearing the colonist lottery yellow, and the outer crowds in shipboard drabs. In the far back, lined up against the newly built mud houses along the great window, a smattering multiform of captive aliens that had survived previous conquests. 
He wondered what strange creatures he would add now to his minions this time around. 
He spoke of his forbears, of the original colonists, of the generations he'd watched be born and die. He spoke of the great Ursuul, who set the path before them, and of the Catsabees, offering the assurance that they had been tamed when, in his heart he knew they could wipe out all of humanity's very existence if they even noticed they were there. For a thousand years, he'd watched his people change around him, shaped as his father and his father's father, and his father's mother before him had shaped them. Stowaways, they'd become a survival machine. 
And after he was finished speaking, as his advisors had timed it, the shaking of the ship entering an atmosphere knocked everybody to their knees. 
Having more practice than anyone, Dovae XIV stood strong through those long moments, until the ship had settled. 
And when the vents cracked open, flooding the human lands with strangely fine-smelling air, the conquerors poured forth again.
Only this time, they were greeted by their own kind.
