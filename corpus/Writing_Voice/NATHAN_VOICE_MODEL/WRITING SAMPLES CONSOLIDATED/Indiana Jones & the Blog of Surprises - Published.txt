
TITLE: Indiana Jones and the Blog of Surprises (blog, published in two parts on Myspace)
(c) 2005 by Nathan McKnight

WARNING, DO NOT READ THIS BLOG IF YOU'RE PLANNING TO SEE THE NEW INDIANA JONES MOVIE
Okay, some people are going to feel the need to criticize this movie, but they're wrong, and this is why…

The thing you have to understand is that the Indiana Jones movies are straight up pulp fiction lifted directly from the men's adventure magazines of the 30s and 40s. What Lucas is doing with this literature is exactly what Tarantino does with 70s exploitation flicks--he's celebrating the schlocky, overdrawn ridiculousness of those crumbly, glorious old magazines. And the fourth movie is absolutely true to form.

And don't mistake me for a wide-eyed fanboy either. When it comes to movies, I'm a hard man to please. If Ebert and that other guy give a movie two thumbs up, it's too lowbrow for me. If a movie won an award for foley engineering at Sundance, it's not obscure enough for my refined cinematic sensibilities. I am a movie snob. I went in with cautious optimism bordering on terror at what new jarjarbinksian debacle old George had in store for us. And yet, the new Indy managed to capture the old genius in every way and then it did something amazing … it expanded the scope of the series without breaking form at all.

Now stop right there because I know what you're going to say. If you've seen the movie, you must be thinking, oh come on, they made Indiana Jones into a New Age alien psychic "Learning" Channel parapsychologist. Doesn't making it into a science fiction adventure betray the spirit of the fantasy adventure carried through the first three movies? Isn't this space alien business incongruent with the religious undertones of the old Indy? Doesn't this change what Indiana Jones is all about!?

Well, no. No, it doesn't. Because the ramshackle bang-up of adventure and fantasy and romance and war and occult and science fiction and western and any other genre or subgenre you can imagine was precisely what made those magazines the spectacle they were. This is slipstream, folks. This is what happens when writers throw away the rules and just have fun. Now, don't get me wrong. Much of that old pulp fiction was perfectly crappy hack, and Indiana Jones is anything but a hack-job (unless we're talking machetes). What makes these four films the only Hollywood series in which there has not yet been a bad movie is that the Indiana Jones form is a solid story-telling framework that allows Lucas to explore a huge amount of territory without forcing the character to become something he's not. Yes, it is a formula, but it is a unique formula. And, unlike Coca Cola, they stick to their original formula.

The way these movies are built is interesting. They are the inverse of a gothic novel.

What does that mean? Well, when you think gothic novel, think Scooby-Doo. This cartoon is one of the only surviving storylines that still follows that outmoded style. See, the gothic novel arose in the hyper-rationalist era of the mid 1700s, when educated society (read: the people who bought books) considered any lip service to the old superstitious ghosts-and-goblin days to be a sort of a blasphemy against the newly-forming scientism. A writer who wanted to write ghost stories could kiss his patron goodbye. But, of course there will always be people who are interested in the mystical side of the human experience. So, the gothic form originated a way of sneaking fantasy elements in your stories without making yourself look like an unenlightened rube. It works like this: You write your ghosts-and-goblins story and fill it with as much mysticism and mumbo-jumbo as you please. Then, at the end, you catch the bad guy, the mask is removed, and it's "Zoinks, Scooby I guess it wasn't ghosts and goblins after all, it was just Monsignor Otranto behind the special mirror the whole time." All is explained…you got your spooky fantasy story with your happy rationalist ending. But this was a short-lived thing. Pretty soon, the Romance movement got into full swing, the gothic genre split. Some writers, free from the bonds of rationalist explanations produced works, like the old vampire tales and ghost stories, that were truly fantasy. Other writers pursued the rationalist side full swing and wrote the amazing early works of science fiction, in which we are expected to interpret the fantastic plot devices as fully-explainable scientific phenomena. The writers of Scooby-Doo are the only ones who still walk the line between fantasy and reality for the whole story, then explain it all away at the end.

Indiana Jones is just the opposite. Each of these movies walks the line between fantasy and reality, and in the end it turns out the Ark of the Covenant really does channel the awesome might of Jehovah, the stones really do have occult powers, and the clay goblet really is the chalice of the son of God. It turns the gothic tradition of rationalism vieled as mysticism into mysticism vieled as rationalism. The fourth movie follows this form exactly... everything appears to be rational, the zombies are men in costumes, Kate Blanchette is a kook, and then at the end it turns out that the aliens are real. (By the way, while we're on the topic of old genres, I'll mention that the wedding at the end makes the whole series into a Shakespearian comedy.) Still, it's aliens, right? Doesn't this break the supernatural theme by turning to science fiction? Well, not really. The line between science fiction and fantasy has been blurry for a hundred years, and pseudo-sciencey mysticism is as alive and well today as it was back in 1957 when flying saucer scares were just as prevalent and Buck Rogers and Captain Video were still in their first round of reruns.

So, the I think the fourth movie is being true to its roots. But what about internal consistency? The way I see it, this series has been alternating between two types of occult mysticism. Raiders dealt with Christian occult, Temple was pagan occult, Crusade went back to Christian occult, and Kingdom returns to (neo)pagan occult.

Whether that's really what Lucas had in mind, I have no idea. But if so, then we can expect a return to the Christian themes in the next movie. So, I close with a prediction for the title of the next Indy movie:

Indiana Jones* and Bible Code Prophesy

Mabe then, we'd finally get a bad Indiana Jones movie. Regardless, if I were to rank the movies as series buffs always do, I would have to put the Crystal Skull as number two, just below the Last Crusade and just above Raiders and Temple (which, itself is underappreciated for what it was trying to do--see the making of documentary on the DVDs).

Best,
Nathan
