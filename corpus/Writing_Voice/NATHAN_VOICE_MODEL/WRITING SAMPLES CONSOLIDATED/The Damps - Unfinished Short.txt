

TITLE: The Damps
(c) 2006 By Nathan McKnight




Mr. Eberts drove through the night to make sure he arrived in Straitsville right at sun up. He figured he'd need most of a day's work to get the deal done, and he wanted to be out of there come nightfall. Already, he looked over his shoulder at every knock of the engine and flutter of the heart.  
*   *   *
"She's been running from that fire her whole goddamn life," said Mr. Eberts, "What makes you think she's going to stop running now?"
"Please, Bill," said Mr. Ambrose. "It would mean an awful lot. That woman practically raised me." 
Mr. Eberts finished the last crumbs of his lemon chiffon.
"Okay," he said, "but if I talk to Lizzie Green, you have to buy every last drop of Hock-Hocking I've got left."
"Hang on a sec," injected J.M., leaning over from the little window booth, "You ain't really going up to Lizzie Green's? That place ain't right, I tell ya. You're like as not to get snuffed out by mine gasses. And all that sulphur in the air--I'm telling ya--it'll eat yer eyes right outta yer head."
"This whole dang town smells like sulphur," said Mr. Eberts, "Gives me the creeps. It's why I left."
Eberts pushed his plate away.
"Don't have much choice," said Eberts. "If I don't sell this beer--and quick--I'm out of a job."
"The way I hear it, you're out of luck any case," said J.M., waving his fried egg sandwich.
"Maybe," said Eberts, "but if I unload my last few cases, I'll get my severance, and a crack at a position in the new company."
"New company?"
"The brewery's thinking of switching to soda pop come the new year."
That brought a guffaw that blew the whipped cream off J.M.'s milkshake.
"Nobody's gonna be drinkin soda pop, Mr. Eberts. If they really take away whiskey, folks around here'll just switch to mine rum."
"It's a done deal," said Eberts, turning back to Mr. Ambrose, "Prohibition is coming. So, what about us? Do we have a deal?" 
He stuck out his hand, Ambrose yanked it toward him, Eberts yanked it back, they shook high, shook low, and ended in an Indian-style "How!"
Just like old times.
"One last thing," said Mr. Eberts. 
He scribbled out a note on the back of his napkin. 
"Could I ask you to send a telegram to Hocking Brewery?" he said.
Ambrose took the napkin and read it aloud: "SALE CONFIRMED SEND THREE TRUCKS PAREN. SEVEN-FIFTY CASES CLOSE PAREN. STRAITSVILLE. A-S-A-P."
*   *   *
He could feel the breath of the ghost horses on the back window of his car as he sped up the road to Dutch Ridge. They had haunted him all his life, but the ghost hooves pounded on his trail as he fishtailed around a gravelly corner. 
He shook his head, took one hand at a time off the wheel to shake out the knucklewhite. He never should have returned to New Straitsville. 
A veil of smoke encompassed the car, and Eberts cursed. The mine fire had been burning since '94. He'd only been eleven at the time, but he way he remembered it, the Sun had died the day the striking workers started the fire. Not long afterwards, Grampa Lisander had died fighting for the town that vilified him. Family enmity burned long in these parts.
Twenty-five years later, the mine fire was still going too, spewing thick smoke from an ever-shifting landscape of fissures and sinkholes. 

When the gravel ran out, Mr. Eberts and slipped out of the lightly-worn front seat of his company car. He would miss the 1917 Pierce-Arrow.
The slam of the door wumphed softly against the low, smoky sky. Mr. Eberts held his handkerchief over his mouth and hiked an unnamed trail up to the last place he remembered Lizzie Green living. She'd never go far. Even as the ground burned, she'd refused to abandon Dutch Ridge. Just kept moving back and forth, scrounging together a new little shack each time an old one burnt down or was inundated by the damps. 
His shoes weren't made for hiking, and his slacks caught on burrs along the way through the coal-stinking woods.
After a while, he ran across the trace of the coal seam on the ground. It was a thick line, exuding the stench and heat of a coal oven, broken in places by slumping dirt. Following Dutch Ridge, he had to cross that line.
Eberts sidled up to one of the dirt slumps and dug his foot in as best he could. With teeth gritted and muscles tensed, he crossed the seam. His shoes steamed with sweat.
He kept the handkerchief tight over his mouth, but it was quickly was blotted black, despite repeated refolding. 
When he reached the top of the ridge, the Sun was a dull red glow in the black sky. The sound of galloping rushed up behind him. He turned, but nothing was there except a dark billow of smoke.
The lights of a cottage glowed through trees queerly tilted trees. He felt a quaking.
"Eeeeberrrrrts!" the shriek came from everywhere, and he knew it was the voice of Shep Tinker, coming to reclaim his partner in crime. 
The ground below began to lift. At first, he felt like the whole world was bending. He jumped and looked back. It was just one tree falling over, its enormous root system tearing out of the ground, smoking, charred.
He approached the house, watery-eyed. 
Lizzie's was a tin shack ensconced in burr oaks and a glorious old gum tree. The remains of the wagon carriage Eberts remembered the long-dead Mr. Green and the Green girls riding around town had been reduced to a weedy stack of bone-dry planks. But one wheel still held together, leaning on the gum tree. Him and Ambrose had pissed on that wheel a thousand times. But they stopped doing it after Mr. Green was taken by the mine. 

Eberts took a leak behind the gum tree for old time's sake, calming his nerves. Where his stream hit the prickly brown tree droppings, the hiss of red-hot embers hissed ammonia steam.
"Whaddaya waunt, Billy?"
Eberts fiddled with his fly and spun, dribbling on his trousers.
"Oh, uh...Lizzie Green. Well, good to see you!"
Silence burned, then Lizzie said, "Tiredest lookin smile I seen all week. Well, don't just stan there--come awn in."
Eberts sighed and followed Lizzie into the dim shack. 
"I've come to talk to you about movin," he said.
"Oh naw you don't," she said, turning right round and pushing him back onto the porch. 
"Done had enougha y'alls commin up here tryna 'talk some sense inta me.' Whatdday'alls think y'gonna say y'ain't already sed? And here's me thinkin you was just commin out to catch up with poor old Lizzie Green," she scowled. "At'n wiz on mah tree. Why should I listen to some babified city slicker, son of a horse thief, anyway?"
Mr. Eberts flushed, the sound of a thousand sets of hooves chasing him through life exploded upon his memory. 
"Now see here, Lizzie Green..." he started.
"No, you see here, boy," she said in an ancient-sounding voice. "Yer family ain't nothing but lousy good-fer-nothin's right down to the person. Shoulda got run outta town before you was even borned."
	"I've had about enough of this," shouted Mr. Eberts, stepping up to take Lizzie by the arm. 
	She backed up, trying to close the door on him, but he pushed his way in.
	"Listen here, boy..."
	"No, you listen here. I am sick of you people disrespecting my kin cause-a your stupid hillbilly legends. My grampa warn't no horse thief, and even if he was, that was a hunderd years ago! I've taken the last lip from you that I care to. You're comin with me." 

As Eberts' spoke, his eyes adjusted to the dim, and he saw the one-room house was almost entirely taken up by an improvised distillery made from a welded battery of fish kettles and the belly of an old green enamel stove. Jars piled three high filled the floor, neatly labeled: MR. BENTLEY, JACK DONLEY SR., RALPH MACKENZIE.
Eberts gaped, picked one up: EDGAR
"Why, you've got a proper nip joint here," he said.
"It wasn't the damps kep me movin," she said, "was the spirits. They died in the fire, or in fightin it. All'a them. I was the only one heard their shrieks when the ground collapsed, their whimpers when the gas overtook 'em. An' they never forgot." She shuddered. "But they tried to take me. Every night, all night, they'd jump into my body, try'na take over, failin'. It was terrible, Billy. Ah tried'ta run, I moved'ta one house after'nuther, but they always found me, and the further I went, cuss'der they got. But I finally learnt. It was the damps'at kep'em, filled the mine'n gave'em life in death. I learnt'ta capture those vapors. In the mine rum," she said, gesturing at the jar in Eberts' hand.
"You captured the mine gasses in...moonshine?"
"It's moonshine, aight, but not juss. After I distill the shine, I run it through again, purify it, and I open the kettles to let the damps in. I realized, accidentally, I silenced one. I knowed'em all by name, an Doc Able weren't swirlin bout no more. He was in the jar."
Eberts peered into the jar he held, and though it was crystal clear, he could see the barest glint of pale orange light.
He shook his head, and said, "Lizzie," steeling himself. "Lizzie, you've finally gone off your rocker."
Quick as he could, Eberts seized her around the waist and swung her bony little body over his shoulder, and turned to leave.
But he was jolted by a crash and a splash on the back of his head. He fell to his knees, his lungs filling with the sting of moonshine vapors.
As Lizzie scampered away, Mr. Eberts staggered out the door and tumbled down the porch steps. He turned with his hands protecting his face. Lizzie stood, holding a jar ready to throw. 
Delicate tendrils of vapor coalesced as they rose from Eberts' face and chest. 
He braced himself against one of the big burr oaks, and said, "Lizzie, please. I only want what's best..."
A jar burst over Eberts' head, against the tree trunk. He ducked and scampered away on all fours across the hot dirt, but fell on his face, feeling as though a powerful wind had hit him suddenly on the back. He rolled over and gaped at the burgeoning cloud of vapors luminescing fire-orange amid the ever-present smoke.
The ground was hot. His back began to burn, and he leapt to his feet. The vapors had materialized into an apparition, sweeping through the air, into his mouth, then out the back of his head. It left him simultaneously burning and icy.
Eberts gasped as another jar whirred by, thwacking his ankle before bursting into flames on the smoldering ground.
"You see what it's like now! Now you see why I gotta trap'em. The still's the only way I know how. Runnin away never worked. No matter how far you go, Billy, they're gonna chase you down."
She hefted another jar of moonshine.
He tried to stand, but only succeeded in rolling over onto his side, painful, fetal. 
The jar on the ground in front of his face was effervescing on the hot dirt. Its label said: LISANDER EBERTS. 
He reached out, forcing his hand to grab the hot jar. Ethanol steam spurted from the lid, prickling orangey-blue flames where it touched the ground.
With all his strength, he wrenched the lid, and with a puff of pressure, the jar was open.
Wavering at the maw of the jar, golden flames formed a tormented face.
"Paw?" he said. 
With all his will, he brought it to his lips for a painful-hot kiss, a swallow, a gulp, another, and another. He felt the hot alcohol surge though his gullet. He could barely breath, but with the heat, his chest tightened, his muscles tensed, hard like anthracite, and he stood, unbreathing, monolithic. 
He felt the ground rumble, and heard a single set of hooves bearing down.
"Eeeeeberrrrtsss!" 
Lizzie staggered, hand over mouth, then darted into the shack.
The burning white smoke of a ghost horse reared up and stamped its wispy hooves on the ground in front of Mr. Eberts. Riding the horse was a shriveled-looking old man.
"So, I've finally caught up with you, Mr. Eberts" said the spirit. 
Against his will, Eberts opened his mouth, and in his grandfather's voice, he said, "What of it, Shep?"
"You took my property," the mounted phantom said, "now I'm going to have to take your life."
"I had to return them to their rightful owners," said the voice from within Eberts. "But the black damp got us just as we crossed the ridge."
The ghost of Shep Tinker sidled up to Eberts, and to his shock, he stood his ground, even as Tinker placed a spectral gun against his temple.
"Say your prayers, Eberts."
At that moment, Lizzie swung the door wide, and the light of the fire under the mash pot on her still flooded the clearing. 
Tinker and Mr. Eberts both swooned. He could feel the spirit of grampa Lisander being pulled out of him. With all his might, he tried to keep the ghost inside him, hugging the spirit with his arms, his chest, his soul.  
Just before he lost his grip, he saw the spirit of Shep Tinker pulled off its mount, twisting into the cabin. It swirled above the still, fighting the inevitable. 
And there was silence.
*   *   *
The refrigerated trucks rolled into the lot, reflecting lamplight onto the gravel.
"What do you mean, you didn't talk her down?" spat Ambrose.
"Trust me, Mr. Ambrose. I've got it covered," slurred Mr. Eberts, drunk on a couple of Manhattan cocktails.
"Yeah, well, you ain't made a sale yet, so wouldn'ya say bringin all this dang beer into town is a bit hasty?"
A crowd had gathered around the trucks, and the Hock-Hocking men unloaded the cases onto Ambrose's front porch.
"Aw, sshhut up and have a bheer," said Mr. Eberts.
"But you promised," protested Mr. Ambrose.
"Shhh..." said Mr. Eberts.
The door to Ambrose Lunch Counter tinkled open.
Ambrose started, mouth agape at seeing Lizzie, dressed in her Sundays.
"Hello, Mr. Ambrose," she said, setting a jar of moonshine on the countertop. "I believe Mr. Eberts has a different proposition for you."
