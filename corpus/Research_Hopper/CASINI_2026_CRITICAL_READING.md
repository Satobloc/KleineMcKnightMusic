# Critical Reading: Casini et al. (2026)

**Paper:** Luca Casini, Laura Cros Vila, David Dalmazzo, Anna-Kaisa Kaila, Bob L. T. Sturm, “Data-Driven Analysis of Text-Conditioning in AI-Generated Music: A Case Study with Suno and Udio,” *Transactions of the International Society for Music Information Retrieval*, 9(1), 194–209. Published 7 May 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5334/tismir.273

## Why this is a cornerstone source

The study analyzes 101,953 songs generated by 60,342 users between May and October 2024: 81,434 Suno songs and 20,519 Udio songs. It examines prompts, tags, lyrics, language use, and metatags using text embeddings, UMAP dimensionality reduction, HDBSCAN clustering, automated cluster descriptions, and manual refinement.

This is not a controlled experiment on the causal effects of individual words. It is a large-scale observational study of how users interacted with the platforms and how their text inputs were distributed.

## Crucial interface-field distinction

The paper explains that Suno metadata field names do not map intuitively to the visible interface:

- **Suno tags:** high-level labels from the interface's “Style of Music” field.
- **Suno prompt:** text entered in the Lyrics box before generation, sometimes user-written, sometimes produced by an LLM.
- **Suno lyrics:** the resulting song text, but the stored data does not establish whether it was user-written or AI-generated.

This distinction must be represented explicitly in our records. Never merge Style-field text and Lyrics-field text into one generic “prompt” column.

## Findings useful to our research

1. The authors identify two broad prompt-writing tendencies: comma-separated qualifiers, and literary/narrative descriptions of the song content or sound. Most prompts sit between these poles.
2. They report 41,108 prompts across both platforms, of which 20,589 are Suno prompts. After language filtering and deduplication, 16,881 English prompts remained for one analysis.
3. The dataset shows repeated identical prompts, suggesting users publish multiple attempts at the same song, though the authors cannot establish the unpublished generation rate.
4. Their analysis of lyrics reveals thematic clusters including technology, absurdity, meme language, food, daily life, fantasy, and other non-musical content.
5. Users employed metatags in square brackets, including structural, instrumental, tempo, duration, and other instructions. Some long or elaborate tag strings were reportedly ignored, misinterpreted, or sung as lyrics, as noted in the paper's discussion of prior community sources.
6. The authors identify outliers as potentially important: rare and unusual prompt practices may reveal more nuanced behavior than the largest clusters.

## Method

- Collect public-facing “new songs” playlist metadata.
- Extract text metadata for songs.
- Embed prompts, tags and lyrics separately using NV-Embed-v2.
- Reduce embeddings with UMAP.
- Cluster with HDBSCAN.
- Label clusters using keyword summaries plus manual inspection.

The method is a useful blueprint for our own community-text corpus, especially for vocabulary mining, cluster discovery, and identifying unusual prompt subgroups. It does not imply that the text embedding space is Suno's own hidden conditioning space.

## Limitations

- Collection window is May–October 2024; the paper does not model current v6-era behavior.
- Model version is not captured in the dataset.
- Only published songs are represented, creating publication and visibility bias.
- Lyrics provenance (human-written vs AI-generated) is not available in metadata.
- Audio is not jointly analyzed with text in this study.
- The paper does not publish the collected metadata; it shares song URL lists and code to retrieve/visualize material. The authors state that direct collection conflicted with platform terms and explain their own legal/ethical reasoning. We must not treat that as permission for this project to repeat their collection method.
- The source repository has no verified reuse license at the time of this review. Review code and data separately.

## Direct implications for KleineMcKnightMusik

1. Add a required input-field distinction: style, lyrics, exclude, simple_prompt, reference_audio, persona, other.
2. Preserve original prompt text only when authorized; otherwise use short excerpts, paraphrase, source URL and provenance.
3. Separate user-authored prompt text from LLM-expanded text whenever evidence exists.
4. Build our own current-era dataset with exact model/version, settings, field placement, output link and generation count where available.
5. Prioritize audio-linked examples, because the paper mostly maps text use and not the resulting acoustic changes.
6. Examine outlier and unusual-language cases rather than only the most common genres and tags.
7. Treat the paper's 2024 data as a historical baseline, not a description of v6.
